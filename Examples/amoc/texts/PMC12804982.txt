To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Abstract Ammonia monooxygenase (AMO) oxidizes ammonia to hydroxylamine. Limited knowledge of the structural information of AMO hinders our understanding of the molecular mechanism underlying ammonia oxidation, impacting the mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions and enhancing agricultural productivity using ammonium as a nitrogen source. Herein, we report the cryo-electron microscopy structure of the AMO complex from an isolated strain of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB). AMO is a cylinder-shaped homotrimeric assembly composed of five subunits. A single-transmembrane protein and a soluble protein are potentially crucial in signal transduction during ammonia oxidation and mediating interactions with the outer membrane protein assembly machinery. Three modeled coppers, along with an adjacent water-mediated hydrogen-bond network, may facilitate an efficient proton transfer pathway from the periplasmic Cu B to the active site Cu D within the inner membrane, where Cu C and Cu D will act in concert to catalyze substrate reaction. The distinctive surface charge characteristics of AMO provide valuable insights into the structural features that govern ammonium assimilation and material transport during ammonia oxidation. These findings shed light on the molecular complexities of AMO and provides a structural foundation for elucidating the catalytic mechanism of ammonia oxidation. Introduction Ammonia oxidation (AO), the pivotal and rate-determining step in nitrification, holds a crucial position in the global nitrogen cycle. This process is primarily orchestrated by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB), ammonia-oxidizing archaea (AOA), and complete ammonia oxidizers commonly referred to as comammox 1 – 4 . These three types of ammonia oxidizers exhibit distinct variations in their environmental distributions due to differences in substrate affinity 5 , 6 . In addition, AOB produce higher levels of nitrous oxide (N 2 O) than AOA both in natural environments and in pure cultures 7 – 10 , thereby exerting a significant impact on global warming. The initial step in AO entails the oxidation of ammonia into hydroxylamine (NH 2 OH), a process catalyzed by copper-dependent ammonia monooxygenase (AMO) 11 through the reaction NH 3 + O 2 + 2e - + 2H + → NH 2 OH + H 2 O 12 . Biochemical data have demonstrated that the AMO core is composed of AmoA, AmoB and AmoC 13 – 15 . Subsequently, two genes ( orf1 and orf5 ) located downstream of the amoCAB gene cluster were regarded as accessory components of the AMO in AOB 16 . AMO in AOA has recently been identified as a complex consisting of six subunits ( amoABCXYZ ), exhibiting only a 40% sequence similarity to their bacterial counterparts, underscoring the unexpected complexity of this enzymatic complex 17 . More recently, Tucci et al. reported the 2.8 Å structure of AMO from Nitrosomonas europaea and discovered a single transmembrane protein subunit 18 . However, the enzymatic mechanism of AMO and the identity and function of its cofactors remain largely enigmatic due to the limitation of resolution and the deficiency of the active Cu D site. The particulate methane monooxygenase (pMMO), a key enzyme in carbon transformation and a member of the membrane monooxygenase family alongside AMO, is responsible for methane oxidation 19 – 22 . Copper ligands are indispensable to the catalytic activity of AMO and pMMO 11 , 23 . Over two decades of structural investigations have unveiled the α3β3γ3 trimeric arrangement of pMMO, consisting of three components—PmoA, PmoB and PmoC. While several copper ligand environments have been identified in pMMO, the identity of the authentic active site has long been a subject of debate 22 . The bis-His site (Cu A ) has historically been considered unlikely to be the active center due to the lack of conservation in coordinating residues 24 . Previous extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) data indicated a dinuclear feature of Cu-Cu scattering interaction in Cu B site 25 , 26 . Subsequently, the high-resolution structures showed a mononuclear Cu B site 27 – 30 . Cu B presents as Cu(II) 31 and has no obvious hydrophobic pocket for substrate binding. Further, the mutation of one of the Cu B ligands in a related hydrocarbon monooxygenase did not completely abolish activity 32 . Consequently, scientists currently tend to believe that the Cu B site is not an active site. The earlier crystal structures revealed that zinc ions occupy the Cu C site due to the artificial addition of crystallization reagents ZnSO 4 24 , 27 . However, its strict conservation and completely abrogated activity upon mutation of coordinated residues suggest that the Cu C is the likely active site 32 . Recently, the presence of the Cu D site, the binding of a substrate analog near the Cu D and the electron nuclear double resonance data supported the notion that the Cu D is the active site 33 , 34 . However, quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations suggest that both Cu C and Cu D tend to remain in the Cu(I) active state 35 , highlighting the potential relevance of a dicopper state. Given that the short distance between them (~5.7 Å resolved in previous pMMO structures containing single Cu C or Cu D ), the possibility of co-occupied Cu C and Cu D sites remains open 22 . In short, fundamental inquiries persist concerning the precise locales of substrate oxidation under the cooperative influence of these copper ions. Here, we have determined the 2.36 Å structure of AMO from an isolated AOB by leveraging the cryo-EM single particle analysis. This structure revealed a homotrimeric assembly of the heteropentamers AMO complex, each composed of five distinct components. Notably, the recently discovered single transmembrane protein AmoD 18 and an identified component AmoE were resolved to side-chain resolution, offering insights into their potential roles in signal transduction and interactions with the outer membrane protein assembly machinery during AO. Moreover, extensive lipid densities were observed within the AMO structure, with their distribution, shape, and arrangement closely mirroring the native membrane environment, suggesting that AMO particles are likely preserved in their native functional state. In addition, the high-resolution structure revealed the coordination environments of the three copper ions within the AMO, along with an adjacent water-mediated hydrogen-bond network, which may facilitate an efficient proton transfer pathway from Cu B to the co-occupied copper site (Cu C and Cu D ), where the potential intermediate density was identified. Furthermore, the surface electrostatic potentials of AMO imply potentially divergent mechanisms governing the binding and delivery of various substrates and products for AMO and pMMO. Results and discussion Isolation of AOB and purification of AMO Chemolithotrophic AOB serve as a pivotal architect in the global nitrogen cycle, orchestrating the transformation of natural ammonia into nitrite 1 , 36 . The initial step of nitrification, wherein ammonia is oxidized to hydroxylamine, is catalyzed by the integral membrane-embedded AMO complex 11 , 37 , 38 . To delve deeper into the structural insights of AMO within AOB, we successfully enriched and isolated a pure culture of AOB ( Nitrosomonas halophila ZJB1) from mangrove sediment using traditional enrichment techniques (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). This identified subspecies, classified within the N. europaea/Nitrosococcus mobilis lineage based on 16S RNA phylogenetic analysis, exhibits high enzymatic activity for AO (Supplementary Fig. 1a ). We engineered a genetic manipulation system that introduced a high-specificity fusion peptide (3 × Flag) tag to the C-terminus of AmoA to affinity purify the high-quality samples of the AMO complex (Supplementary Fig. 1b ). Following this, a gentle yet efficient purification protocol yielded monodispersed and uniform AMO complexes solubilized in the mild detergent digitonin (DIG) (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). Enzymatic assays were performed using electron donors commonly applied in studies of pMMO to assess AO activity of the purified AMO sample. However, no detectable AMO activity was observed under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b ). Through screening potential redox-active compounds, we identified ubiquinol (CoQ 10 H 2 ) as a candidate electron donor that moderately enhanced AMO activity (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d ). In addition, a significant decline in activity was observed concomitant with the progressive loss of native cellular integrity (Supplementary Fig. 3e ), which is not caused by disruption of the AMO structure, as compared to the recently reported AMO structure in the native membrane 18 (vide infra). This result suggests that efficient AO relies on factors beyond CoQ 10 H 2 alone. Importantly, the AO activity of the purified AMO sample was compared with that of the membrane-bound form, indicating that the purified AMO retained substantial enzymatic activity (Supplementary Fig. 3f ). Then, these freshly isolated AMO samples were subsequently subjected to single-particle cryo-EM analysis, resulting in a high-resolution 3D reconstruction map with a nominal resolution of 2.36 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1 ). This resolution is adequate for unambiguously building an atomic model of AMO at the side-chain level with high confidence (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5 ). Overall structure of AMO The overall architecture of AMO exhibits a cylindrical shape with a volume of ~105 Å x 105 Å x 115 Å. AMO oligomerizes as a homotrimer of heteropentamers. Each protomer consists of AmoA, AmoB, AmoC, and a recently discovered single transmembrane protein and an identified subunit, hereafter referred to as AmoD and AmoE (distinct from the previously predicted AmoD and AmoE 39 (vide infra) (Fig. 1 ). Two β-barrel domains from the N-terminus and C-terminus of AmoB interact in an almost perpendicular orientation, arranged in a counterclockwise manner as a trimer, forming the periplasmic regions of AMO (Fig. 1 ). This structure is supported by 45 transmembrane (TM) helices, with contribution of six from AmoA, two from AmoB, six from AmoC, and one from AmoD in each protomer respectively (Fig. 1b ). Similar to the pMMO, the intracellularly soluble region of AMO is primarily composed of AmoA, AmoB and AmoC, forming a triangular scaffold bridged by the extended C-terminus of AmoA. A central pore spans the entire AMO complex and is lined with a bilayer of lipids (Fig. 1a ). In accordance with the findings from the pMMO nanodisc system 33 , extensive potential lipid densities were discovered within the central pore and on the surface of the TM region (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 6a ), suggesting that the native cellular environment is preserved during protein purification. To identify the specific lipid entities present in the sample, we conducted a comprehensive lipidomics analysis. In contrast to the predominant lipid composition in intact bacterial cells, the major lipid species identified in the AMO sample were free fatty acid (FFA), monoglyceride (MG), diglyceride (DG), and phosphatidylserine (PS) (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 6b, c ), suggesting a selective enrichment of lipids that interact with AMO. By integrating the spectrometric evidence with the cryo-EM density maps, we ultimately incorporated 84 lipid molecules into the AMO structure (Fig. 1e ). These lipids were predominantly distributed across three regions. One cluster of lipids resembling a plug is situated at the upper part of the central pore, with its head groups facing the periplasm and acyl chains stretching into the inner pore (Supplementary Fig. 6a , left panel). Near the cytoplasmic region of AMO, a propeller-shaped lipid buckles the outlet of the pore. Compared to the lipids in the upper part of the central pore, these lipids are arranged in an opposing fashion, with their polar head groups directed towards the cytoplasm. Lipids from two adjacent AMO monomers forms three blade-like structures (Supplementary Fig. 6a , right panel). In the transmembrane region, a bilayer of lipids is observed, with their hydrophobic acyl chains aligned along the equatorial plane of the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 6a , middle panel). Collectively, the distribution, shape and arrangement of aforementioned of these lipids closely mirror the native membrane environment. Copper centers and proton transfer pathway in AMO Similar to pMMO, AMO has been established as a copper-dependent oxidase 11 . Extensive structural data have previously shown diverse copper centers in pMMO, as summarized in Supplementary Fig. 7 . The absence of the proposed active Cu D site in the recently reported AMO structure 18 leaves the exact copper centers and active site in the AMO unresolved to date. The high-resolution structure of the AMO resolved in this study enabled the identification of nine metal-like density regions within the trimeric AMO, positioned at sites analogous to the Cu B , Cu C and Cu D binding sites in pMMO. Each AMO protomer contains three copper ligands that form two distinct copper centers (Fig. 2a ). The first copper center resides within the N-terminal β-barrel of AmoB and is coordinated by H38, H142 and H144 (Fig. 2b ), a configuration conserved across all observed pMMO (Supplementary Fig. 7 ) and the recently reported AMO 18 . Consequently, it is designated as the Cu B site. Despite the conservation of these coordinating residues, the number of copper ions at this site has been a subject of debate. For instance, dinuclear copper centers are observed in crystal and cryo-EM structures of pMMO from several species 24 , 27 , 40 , 41 , whereas many other structures exhibit mononuclear copper 28 , 33 , 34 (Supplementary Fig. 7 ). In the high-resolution AMO resolved in this study, the Cu B center unambiguously displays a mononuclear copper (Fig. 2b ). Consistent with crystallographic and spectroscopic studies of Cu B 28 , 29 , 31 , 42 , an axial water molecule positioned below the Cu B site forms a weak interaction with Cu B at a distance of 3.9 Å. In contrast to the individual Cu C and Cu D sites previously observed in pMMO structure and the recently resolved AMO, the second copper center in our AMO structure is unexpectedly characterized by two simultaneously occupied copper ligands—Cu C and Cu D (Fig. 2c–e ). This prominent feature is reminiscent of the recent QM/MM MD result that both Cu C and Cu D tend to remain in Cu(I) active state 35 , suggesting the potential synergistic effect of Cu C and Cu D on AO. Examination of the neighboring environment around the second copper center reveals that five TM helices—TM1 from AmoA and TM3, TM4, TM5 and TM6 from AmoC—converge to form a hydrophobic channel. In contrast to the single copper center in pMMO and AMO, TM5 of AmoC from the dinuclear copper center resolved in this study is bifurcated into two smaller helices. The upper helix obviously shifts towards the central channel, thereby expanding the volume of this hydrophobic channel to accommodate the dinuclear copper ligands (Fig. 2h ). The observed change in the diameter of this hydrophobic channel with an increase in the number of copper ligands may reflect regulation of AMO activity, and the TM5 of AmoC may play a crucial role in this process. Sequence alignment demonstrates that TM5 is conserved among AOB and methane-oxidizing bacteria (MOB). Notably, the histidine and asparagine residues that coordinate Cu D , as well as the residues at the kink site, are highly conserved (Fig. 2i ), indicating a shared role in regulating the Cu D site and hydrophobic channel in both AMO and pMMO. Cu C establishes a stable coordinated environment with two positively-charged residues (H140 and H153) and two negatively-charged residues (D 136 and D146) (Fig. 2d ). It is worth mentioning that Asp146 exhibits less conservation and may be replaced by Arginine/Glutamate 33 , 34 , 41 , or even be absent in pMMO 27 , 28 , 33 . Analogous to the Cu B site, a spherical density approximately 3.3 Å from the Cu C site was observed and modeled as a water molecule (Fig. 2c ). Cu D , recently identified as a potential active site in pMMO 34 , engages in an interaction network through H211, H225, N207 and E218 (Fig. 2e ). E218 was absent in the pMMO structure (Supplementary Fig. 7 ). To probe the functional relevance of these metal-coordinating residues, we constructed single-copy mutations in H140A, H153A, D146A, N207A, E218A, and H211A. Attempts to generate D136A and H225A mutants were unsuccessful, likely owing to the severe functional defects caused by these substitutions. Enzyme activity assays revealed that most mutations resulted in an approximately 30% reduction in activity (Fig. 2g ), comparable to the effect caused by the deletion of single AMO subunits 43 . Given the presence of three copies of the amo operon in the genome, these findings underscore the essential role of these residues in AO. Close inspection of the coordination environments of the three coppers revealed the consistent presence of a relatively weak hydrogen bond associated with these sites (Fig. 2b, d, e , red stars), suggesting their potential role in facilitating proton transfer during substrate oxidation, beyond merely stabilizing the copper sites. Between the Cu C and Cu D ligands, we observe two distinct densities (Fig. 2c ). While these are tentatively modeled as water molecules based on precedents in related metalloenzyme structures 18 , 27 , 33 , we cannot exclude the possibility that they represent oxygen species derived from dioactivation. The dinuclear nature of this center invites comparison to type III copper sites involved in O 2 binding and activation 44 ; however, the longer metal–metal distance and atypical coordination geometry suggest a distinct mechanism in AMO (Supplementary Fig. 8 ). Recent studies have proved that Cu D serve as the active site for substrate oxidation in pMMO, with the substrate binding site located in proximity to Cu D 33 , 34 . Interestingly, an elongated, rod-shaped density was observed in the hydrophobic channel adjacent to the Cu C –Cu D center (Fig. 2c ). While its identity remains uncertain, its location suggests a potential role in substrate access or product release. Further studies at higher resolution or under different catalytic conditions are necessary to elucidate the nature of this feature. Integrating the aforementioned structural features, it appears more plausible that the co-occupied copper center represents the genuine active site of AMO, functioning in a manner analogous to a type III copper center, despite the presence of certain distinct catalytic characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 8 ) 45 . In this context, the metalloenzyme utilizes a pair of copper ligands as its catalytic active center to facilitate the catalytic process. Based on the Grotthus mechanism 46 , we discovered an extensive water-mediated hydrogen-bonding network from Cu B to the Cu C and Cu D site. The hydrogen bond distances, ranging from 3.9 Å (near the Cu B ) to 2.6 Å (near the Cu C ), may reveal an efficient proton transfer to the co-occupied Cu C and Cu D active center to facilitate the AO reaction, as indicated by the high-resolution structure of photosystem II 47 – 49 (Fig. 2f ). However, further experimental evidence is required to substantiate this proposal. In addition, the bis-His site is not present in the AMO structure due to the substitution of the first Histidine by Glutamine in the AmoB sequence, as observed in the pMMO structure of Methylocystis sp. str . Rockwell, suggesting less conservation of the bis-His site. AmoD and the identified AmoE Over the years, extensive efforts have been dedicated to elucidating the structure and composition of AMO by integrating methodologies from genomics, biochemistry, bioinformatics and biophysics. In addition to three proposed components based on the homology to pMMO—AmoA, AmoB and AmoC 50 , scientists have recently uncovered evidence suggesting the presence of additional components within AMO 17 . However, the precise architecture remains enigmatic due to the limitation of the direct structure. Beyond the aforementioned three core subunits, the structure of AMO resolved in this study reveals a recently reported single transmembrane protein (identified as NZ4318 in this study) 18 and an identified component (NZ3624) at the periphery of AMO based on the high-resolution structure at the side-chain level (Supplementary Fig. 5 ), assigned as AmoD and AmoE, respectively. These proteins diverge from the earlier predicted AmoX/Y/Z in archaeal AMO 17 , 51 . In contrast, AmoD is relatively conserved with the addition of a reported transmembrane helix 18 (Supplementary Fig. 9 ), indicating a compositional difference between bacterial and archaeal AMO. AmoD is positioned adjacent to AmoC with its N-terminal facing the cytoplasmic side and its C-terminal oriented towards the periplasmic side (Figs. 1 and 3a ). A similarly enigmatic helical structure has been observed in pMMO, but efforts to elucidate its identity were impeded by inadequate resolution 27 , 28 , 40 . In line with the crystal structure of Methylocystis sp. str . Rockwell pMMO, a robust density of phospholipid, was modeled near the AmoD. The conformation of this phospholipid differs from that of pMMO, as it is fully embedded in the hydrophobic interior surface of AmoD and AmoC. The lipid species was identified as phosphatidylmethanol (PMeOH) by integrating the lipidomic data with the corresponding density. The phosphate group of PMeOH interacts with R178, a conserved residue from the AmoC subunit, while its alkyl tails extend into the hydrophobic interface formed by AmoC and AmoD (Fig. 3b ). In addition, a second previously undetected phospholipid, identified as diacylglycerol (DG), resides at the interface between the interprotomer AmoC and the C-terminal of AmoD (Fig. 3c ). The first alkyl chain of DG interacts with AmoD through W37 and penetrates the hydrophobic core formed by AmoD and AmoC. Concurrently, the second alkyl chain anchors onto the surface of TM1 in AmoC and extends into the lipid bilayer of the cell membrane. This distinct lipid-binding mode suggests that these interactions may contribute to stabilizing the structural integrity of the AMO complex and facilitating the proper integration and localization of AmoD within the membrane environment. We performed bioinformatics analysis to investigate the potential functional roles of AmoD and AmoE. Interestingly, the phylogenetic tree revealed that amoD is exclusively present in the N. europaea/N. mobilis lineage, with its genomic location associated with certain key enzymes involved in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) and the synthesis of crucial signal molecules (such as c-di-GMP) (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b ). We propose that AmoD may play a role in coordinating electron flow from central metabolism to AMO. This model is supported by our identification of ubiquinol (CoQ 10 H 2 )—a metabolic electron carrier regenerated by TCA cycle-derived reducing equivalents—as an effective electron donor for AMO (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d ). Therefore, AmoD may facilitate metabolic coupling to maintain AO activity under diverse physiological conditions. In contrast, the genetic neighborhood of amoE encompasses genes encoding components of the ATPase and outer membrane beta-barrel assembly machinery complex (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 10a, c ), suggesting a potential, though still speculative, functional relationship. Given its periplasmic localization, we hypothesize that AmoE may help coordinate interactions between the inner membrane-embedded AMO complex and outer membrane-associated components, possibly contributing to regulatory or substrate delivery processes. This is consistent with the previously predicted interaction between the AMO and the S-layer outside the membrane in archaea 17 , 52 . However, further experimental validation is required to elucidate its precise role. Surface electrostatic potentials of AMO The assimilation and transformation of ammonium into hydroxylamine play a crucial role, mediated by AMO 11 . Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms underlying these vital processes within AMO remain elusive. Close inspection of the surface electrostatic potentials of AMO revealed distinctive characteristics that diverge from those of pMMO. Notably, the periplasmic β-barrel domains of AmoB coalesce into three densely negatively-charged regions (DNR), a feature that is absent in pMMO (Fig. 4a , left panel). These DNR may contribute to facilitating the capture of positively-charged ammonium ions from the ambient environment. In addition, two parallel charged zones were distributed along the equatorial plane of the lipid bilayer (Fig. 4a , middle panel). Within the periplasmic layer of lipid, a negatively-charged ribbon, designated as the periplasmic negatively-charged regions (PNR), is situated on the lipid surface adjacent to the extracellular cupredoxin domain of AmoB. Beneath the PNR, a positively-charged ribbon, termed the periplasmic positively-charged regions (PPR), runs parallel spreads and is embedded within the lipid bilayer. Analogously, on the cytoplasmic side, cytoplasmic negatively-charged regions (CNR) and cytoplasmic positively-charged regions (CPR) are arranged in parallel (Fig. 4a , middle panel). These charged regions do not exist at the hydrophobic and hydrophilic interface of the lipid bilayer in pMMO (Fig. 4a , middle panel). The distinct electrostatic profile at the membrane interface may facilitate the recognition and translocation of ammonium. This architecture is reminiscent of recently reported cation-translocating membrane proteins 53 , suggesting a conserved mechanism for substrate guidance and uptake. Moreover, unlike the densely charged distribution of the cytoplasmic region of pMMO, AMO exhibits a more evenly distributed charge landscape, attributed to the extended cytoplasmic regions of AmoA, AmoC and AmoD relative to their homologous counterparts PmoA and PmoC (Fig. 4a , right panel and Supplementary Fig. 11 ). In particular, the AmoA features an elongated C-terminus consisting of a positively-charged β hairpin and a negatively-charged helix. These two structural elements are intricately intertwined in a head-to-tail configuration through robust electrostatic interactions, forming an equilateral triangular scaffold within the cytoplasmic AmoA trimer (Fig. 4b ). This architectural arrangement serves as a linchpin in stabilizing the three adjacent AMO protomers and fine-tuning the surface electrostatic distribution of the cytoplasmic regions of AMO. In essence, AMO diverges from pMMO in its surface electrostatic potentials. Given the charged disparities between the respective substrates and products of pMMO and AMO, these distinctive attributes may suggest a potentially divergent mechanism for the binding and delivery of substrates (ammonia, methane, electron donors and oxygen) and products (hydroxylamine and methanol) for AMO and pMMO. In conclusion, our cryo-EM structure of the AMO complex from N. halophila ZJB1 provides key insights into the molecular architecture and catalytic potential of this environmentally and agriculturally important enzyme. We resolved AMO as a homotrimer of heteropentamers, with each protomer comprising five subunits—AmoA, AmoB, AmoC, AmoD and AmoE—thereby revising the conventional model of its composition. The presence of the single transmembrane protein AmoD and the soluble subunit AmoE suggests roles in structural stabilization, complex assembly, as well as potentially in coordinating cellular metabolism or substrate delivery processes related to AO, supported by their genomic context and localization. Notably, our structure reveals a dinuclear copper center (Cu C -Cu D ) with simultaneous occupancy, offering the direct structural evidence for a proposed catalytically relevant state. The adjacent water-mediated hydrogen-bond network suggests a plausible pathway for proton transfer, supporting a mechanism wherein Cu C and Cu D act in concert to facilitate AO. Furthermore, the electrostatic features at the periplasmic and cytoplasmic interfaces may assist in substrate recruitment and product release, highlighting functional divergence from the related pMMO. While this study establishes a foundational structural framework, further functional analyses and high-resolution investigations are required to fully elucidate the substrate-binding intermediates, clarify the specific roles of AmoD and AmoE, and delineate the catalytic mechanism of AMO. Methods Isolation of pure AOB strain Surface sediment samples (0–2 cm) were collected from the mudflat in a mangrove wetland in Zhanjiang Bay in November 2020 (Supplementary Fig. 1c ). The sediment samples were placed in a cooler and transported to the laboratory for the enrichment and isolation of AOB. Detailed procedures for AOB enrichment and isolation processes are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a . Briefly, ~2 g of sediment samples were added into 40 mL AOB enrichment medium 54 , and the resulting mixture was incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 120 rpm in the dark until a high ammonia oxidation rate was detected. Subsequently, a small volume of the enriched culture was transferred to a culture flask containing fresh medium (10% inoculation) for further cultivation until ammonium depletion. The dilution procedure was repeated multiple times until no culturable heterotrophic microorganisms were detected using nutrient agar. The abundances of AOB and total bacteria in these enrichments were quantified based on quantitative PCR of the β -AOB amoA 55 and bacterial 16S rRNA genes 56 , respectively. When AOB accounted for ≥90% of the total bacteria, the enrichment culture was diluted and then inoculated onto AOB solid medium (2% agar) 54 . A single colony was selected and isolated for further study. Finally, we obtained a pure AOB strain whose 16S rRNA gene sequence exhibited high sequence similarity (>98.8% identity) to that of isolated N. halophila Nm1 1 and formed a distinct cluster with N. halophila Nm1 in the phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Fig. 1d ). Given its sampling location, this pure AOB isolate is named N. halophila ZJB1 (Zhangjiang Bay). The phylogenetic tree of the AOB 16S rRNA gene The 16S rRNA gene sequences of representative AOB were retrieved from GenBank ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ ) for phylogenetic analyses. These sequences were arrayed with MAFFT-L-INS-I 57 and pruned using BMGE (BLOSUM30) 58 . Maximum-likelihood tree was inferred with IQ-TREE under GTR + F + I + G4 model 59 . Ultrafast bootstrapping (1000 replicates) was used. Phylogenies of AmoD and AmoE sequences The AmoE sequence from N. halophila ZJB1 was used as a query to search against the nr database using BLASTP 60 , and hits with > 50% amino acid similarity were downloaded. For AmoD phylogeny, the bacterial sequences containing the lipoprotein family motif of AmoD (PS51257) were downloaded from the Interpro database ( https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/ ). After excluding proteins longer than 100 amino acids, a total of 474 homologous sequences were obtained. These sequences were aligned with MAFFT-L-INS-I 57 and pruned using BMGE (BLOSUM30) 58 . Maximum-likelihood trees were inferred with IQ-TREE (cpREV + G4 for AmoD; WAG + G4 for AmoE; -bb 1000) 59 . Chemical analyses Ammonium and nitrite concentrations were measured using the optimized indophenol blue method 61 and the modified Griess-Llosvay procedure 62 , respectively. Briefly, 100 μL of culture or buffer was aliquoted into sterile 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature to pellet large particulates and cells. The supernatant was transferred to fresh sterile 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and diluted to a final volume of 1 mL with NH 4 + -free ultrapure water. For the optimized indophenol blue assay, 50 μL of phenol-nitroprusside solution and 50 μL of alkaline hypochlorite solution were added; alternatively, for the modified Griess-Llosvay assay, 50 μL of sulfanilamide solution and 50 μL of naphthylamine solution were used. Following complete color development, absorbance was measured at 630 nm for ammonium and at 543 nm for nitrite using a microplate reader (EnSpire). Final concentrations were calculated using standard curves for NH 4 + (0–10 μM, n = 3, R 2 > 0.998) and NO 2 - (0-20 μM, n = 3, R 2 > 0.999), respectively. DNA extraction, gene cloning, and plasmid construction DNA was extracted from 2 mL culture using the PowerSoil ® DNA Isolation kit (MoBio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The DNA concentrations were measured using the NanoValue™ Plus micronucleic acid analyzer (GE Healthcare Life, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The PCR primers used in this study are shown in Supplementary Table 2 . (i) pJTU1278 (Catalog No. TSPLA16579, Ningbo Testa Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), a plasmid used for the construction of a suicide vector harboring the KmR gene. The recombinant suicide plasmid consists of three fragments: the first fragment (2.9 kb) was PCR amplified using the primer pair pJTU-F/pJTU-R, incorporating the ori, lacZ, and AmpR genes from the pJTU1278 plasmid 63 along with an XbaI restriction site. The second fragment (558 bp) was PCR amplified using the primer pair oriT-F/oriT-R, containing the conjugative ori and the traJ gene. The third fragment, a kanamycin resistance gene (KmR) 64 , was synthesized by GENEWIZ Biotechnology Co., LTD (Suzhou, China) and PCR amplified using the primer pair KmR-F/KmR-R, resulting in a 978 bp product that includes the KmR promoter. Subsequently, the three fragments were assembled into the plasmid, pJTU1278-KmR, using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). (ii) pJTU1278-KmR, a plasmid used for the assembly of amoCAB genes and fusion tags. A 1.42 kb DNA fragment encompassing the amoC gene and its promoter sequence from N. halophila ZJB1 was amplified by PCR using primers amoC -F/ amoC -R, with the reverse primer amoC -R containing a Xba I restriction site and Strep-Tag II fusion tag. The resulting PCR products were purified via gel extraction and ligated into the Xba I site of the vector, followed by transformation into E. coli DH5α cells. Similarly, the amoA (1.08 kb) and amoB (1.28 kb) genes were PCR amplified using respective primer pairs amoA -F/ amoA -R and amoB -F/ amoB -R. The corresponding PCR products were also gel-purified and ligated into the Xba I site before being transformed into E. coli DH5α cells. In addition, 3 × Flag fusion tag was introduced downstream of amoA via reverse primers. Ultimately, we successfully constructed an 8253 bp suicide plasmid, designated as pJTU1278-KmR-AMO. Electroporation-mediated cell transformation N. halophila ZJB1 cells (1 L) from an early-stationary-phase liquid culture (OD 600 ≈ 0.2) were harvested by centrifugation and washed three times with sterile water. The sedimented cells were resuspended in 1.5 mL of sterile water and kept on ice until use. Electroporation was carried out using an ECM399 Electroporation System (BTX Harvard Apparatus, voltage range of 60-2500 V, fixed capacitance of 36 μF, fixed resistance of 150 Ω). Aliquots (100 μL) of electrocompetent cells were transformed with 5 μL plasmid DNA (1 μg DNA/μL) in 1 mm gap parallel electrode cuvettes (Fisherbrand) at a constant voltage of 1.2 kV. A control for the electroporation experiment consisted of cell aliquots without added DNA. Immediately after electroporation, the cells were transferred to 200 mL of fresh medium and incubated for 24 h under nonselective conditions at 30 °C with shaking (100 rpm). Kanamycin was then added to a final concentration of 25 μg/mL. Cell growth was monitored by measuring consumption of NH 4 + and accumulation of NO 2 - . Finally, a small volume of cultures was transferred to a culture flask with fresh medium (10% inoculation) containing kanamycin (25 μg/mL) for further culturing until NH 4 + was depleted. This process was repeated, and after two months, kanamycin-resistant cultures were successfully obtained. AMO purification 120 L N. halophila ZJB1 cells expressing AmoA-Flag were harvested by vacuum filtration through a 0.22 µM pore polycarbonate membrane filter (Millipore). The collected cells were subsequently resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM PIPES pH 7.2, 50 mM NaCl, 30 µM CuSO 4 ) and lysed by sonication on ice. The lysate was then supplemented with n-Decyl-β-D-Malyranoside (DDM) to a final concentration of 2%, followed by stirring for 2 h at 4 °C to extract the membrane proteins. Subsequently, cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 mins at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and incubated with anti-FLAG beads for 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed extensively by SEC buffer (50 mM PIPES pH 7.2, 50 mM NaCl, 30 µM CuSO 4 , 0.06% digitonin (DIG)). Proteins were eluted by 200 µg/mL 3 × FLAG peptide in the SEC buffer. The eluent was loaded onto to a Superose S6 3.2/60 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with SEC buffer. Peak fractions were collected for cryo-EM sample preparation. Activity assays The ammonia oxidation activity was measured separately using intact AOB cells, cell lysates (either centrifuged supernatant or non-centrifuged suspension), membrane fractions, and purified AMO protein. Briefly, N. halophila ZJB1 was cultured to the late exponential growth phase, and approximately 3 g of cells were harvested via filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter (Millipore). The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 50 mL of SEC buffer (OD 600 ≈ 5.0) and aliquoted into sterile 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes (1 mL per tube). Cell disruption was carried out using an ultrasonic disruptor (Xinzhi Instrument, Zhejiang, China) under the following conditions: 80 W power output, with a 2 s pulse followed by a 4 s pause, for a total duration of 3 min. The disrupted cell suspension was either used directly as the non-centrifuged cell lysate or further processed by centrifugation. For preparation of the centrifuged extract, the lysate was centrifuged at 2000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min to remove intact cells and large cellular debris. Total protein concentration was determined using a micro-UV spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad). The non-centrifuged lysate exhibited a protein concentration of approximately 22.3 mg/mL, whereas the centrifuged supernatant showed a reduced concentration of 12.1 mg/mL—approximately half that of the non-centrifuged sample. To account for this loss during centrifugation, twice the initial biomass was used in the preparation of centrifuged extracts to ensure equivalent protein loading in downstream assays. For the membrane fractions, ~ 3 g of N. halophila ZJB1 cells pellet was carried to ultrasonic disruption. The resulting lysate was centrifuged at 21,500 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and then ultracentrifuged at 150,000 × g and 4 °C for 1 h. After discarding the final supernatant, the membrane pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of SEC buffer. The total protein concentration of this membrane fractions was determined to be 9.5 mg/mL. Given that the AMO protein constitutes approximately 80% of the total membrane protein 65 , the estimated AMO protein concentration in this membrane fractions was about 7.6 mg/mL. The AMO protein was purified according to the method described above in this study, yielding a final concentration of ~3.0 mg/mL. To investigate the factors influencing AMO activity, a series of reductants was evaluated using intact AOB cells and cell lysates. The reductants tested included duroquinone (5 mM), formate (26 mM), and NADH (10 mM), which have been widely employed as effective electron donors for measuring pMMO activity in methanotrophs 28 , 30 , 66 , 67 . In addition, the effects of several potential reductants or electron donors on the ammonia oxidation activity of intact cells and cell lysates were investigated, including ubiquinol (CoQ 10 H 2 , 50 μM), sodium ascorbate (Asc., 10 mM), DL-dithiothreitol (DTT, 10 mM), and tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP, 10 mM). In reductant or electron donor assays, 1 mL of intact cell suspension or cell lysate was transferred to a 10 mL centrifuge tube. To this, 1 mL of SEC buffer, NH 4 ⁺ (~5 mM), KH 2 PO 4 (12 mM), NaHCO 3 (13 mM), and essential trace metals for AOB (1 mL/L) were added. A negative control without any reductant was included in each experimental series. For activity assays using membrane fractions and purified AMO protein, 1 mL of sample was transferred into 10 mL centrifuge tubes; the membrane fractions were subsequently supplemented with 1 mL of SEC buffer to achieve a final volume of 2 mL. The reaction mixtures were supplemented with CoQ 10 H 2 (50 μM), NH 4 ⁺ (~5 mM), KH 2 PO 4 (12 mM), NaHCO 3 (13 mM), and essential trace metals for AOB (1 mL/L). A blank control containing CoQ 10 H 2 but lacking membrane fractions or purified AMO protein was also included. All assays were incubated in the dark at 30 °C with shaking at 120 rpm in a temperature-controlled incubator. Aliquots were collected at predetermined time intervals to measure NH 4 ⁺ consumption, allowing for the monitoring of ammonia oxidation activity. Construction of mutants and enzyme activity assays DNA fragments containing point mutations at specific amino acid residues associated with Cu C and Cu D were generated. Briefly, PCR amplification was carried out using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 2 , and the resulting PCR products were purified. Following the cloning procedure described previously in this study, the mutated DNA fragments were initially ligated into a kanamycin resistance gene-free pJTU1278 vector to reconstruct the complete amoC gene. Subsequently, a kanamycin resistance cassette (KmR) and the amoA gene were inserted downstream through ligation. A final PCR was then performed using the primer pair amoC -F1/ amoA -R1, and the amplified product was purified to yield the full-length mutated DNA fragment. The constructed fragments were introduced into the N. halophila ZJB1 wild-type strain via electroporation as described above. Transformants were selected on agar plates supplemented with 25 mg/L kanamycin, yielding six kanamycin-resistant mutant strains. Ammonia oxidation activity of the mutant strains was assessed by monitoring NH 4 + consumption over time. In brief, each mutant culture was adjusted to an OD 600 of approximately 0.06 and transferred into AOB growth medium to a final volume of 10 mL, with NH 4 + provided at a final concentration of ~2 mM along with other essential nutrients required for AOB growth. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C under shaking conditions (120 rpm). Aliquots were collected at regular intervals to determine NH 4 + concentrations, allowing for the evaluation of ammonia oxidation activity in each mutant. Lipidomics analysis The lipid extraction and LC-MS/MS analysis for N. halophila ZJB1 cells and purified AMO protein were performed for three independent biological replicates, respectively. Internal standards, including TG (16:0-d9/16:0/16:0), SM (d18:1/16:0-d9), DG (16:0-d9/16:0), CE (16:0-d9), PC (16:0-d9/16:0), PE (16:0-d9/16:0), PI (16:0-d9/16:0), PS (16:0-d9/16:0) and LysoPC (16:0-d9/0:0), were added to N. halophila ZJB1 cells and AMO protein samples suspended in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline:methanol (1:1, v/v). The mixture was homogenized using a Precellys homogenizer (Retsch) through three cycles of 30 s at 8000 rpm, with 5 min incubations on ice between each cycle. A methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE)-based liquid-liquid extraction method was subsequently performed. Four milliliters of MTBE were added to each sample and vortexed vigorously. The mixtures were incubated for 1 h at room temperature on a platform shaker (Shanghai Jinsin Technology Co., Ltd.) operating at 500 rpm. Phase separation was achieved by the addition of 1 mL of water. After shaking for 10 min, the samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min. The upper organic phase from each sample was carefully aspirated using a Pasteur pipette and transferred to a fresh glass tube. The residual aqueous phase was subjected to a second extraction with 2.5 mL of MTBE/methanol/water (10:3:2.5, v/v/v). Following the same shaking and centrifugation steps as described above, the resulting organic phase was collected and combined with the initial extract. The lipid extracts were dried under a stream of nitrogen using a SpeedVac vacuum concentrator. The dried lipids were reconstituted in 200 µL of n-butanol/methanol (1:1, v/v) and transferred to autosampler vials for lipidomic profiling via LC-MS (ExionLC TM AD, SCIEX) tandem MS (QTRAP 6500 + , SCIEX). The LC-MS analytical conditions were as follows: HPLC column, Shim-pack VP-ODS C18 (pore size 5.0 μm, dimensions 2 × 150 mm); mobile phase, water containing 0.04% acetic acid and acetonitrile containing 0.04% acetic acid; gradient program, 100:0 (v/v) at 0 min, 5:95 (v/v) at 20.0 min, 5:95 (v/v) at 22.0 min, 95:5 (v/v) at 22.1 min, and 95:5 (v/v) at 28.0 min; flow rate, 0.25 mL min⁻¹; column temperature, 40 °C; injection volume, 5 μL. The eluate was alternately directed to an electrospray ionization triple quadrupole–linear ion trap (Q TRAP)–MS or an electrospray ionization QqTOF–MS. The mass spectrometer was operated under the following conditions: ion source temperature, 500 °C; ion spray voltage, + 5500 V in positive mode and −4500 V in negative mode; nebulizing gas 1 (GS1), 45 psi; auxiliary gas 2 (GS2), 55 psi; curtain gas, 35 psi. In the triple quadrupole mode, each precursor-product ion pair was monitored using optimized declustering potentials and collision energies. All mass spectrometry data were acquired and processed using the Xcalibur 3.0 software suite. Lipid annotation and data processing were performed using Lipostar software (version 1.3.1b18; Molecular Discovery). Feature detection, noise and artifact filtering, retention time alignment, and lipid identification were conducted. Peak picking, signal smoothing and alignment algorithms were applied to resolve unique m/z and retention time pairs. A retention time window of 1–32 min was implemented during analysis. Background signals arising from blank solvents, features lacking MS/MS spectra, peaks without detectable isotopic patterns, and nonspecific noise were excluded from further analysis. The identification and quantification of lipid molecular species were performed using LipidSearch software (version 4.1; Mitsui Knowledge Industry, Tokyo, Japan). The key processing parameters were configured as follows: target database, LIPID MAPS; precursor ion mass tolerance, 5 ppm; product ion mass tolerance, 5 ppm; product ion intensity threshold, 5%; m-score threshold, 1; quantitative m/z tolerance, ± 5 ppm; quantitative retention time (RT) window, ± 0.5 min; application of the main isomer filter and ID quality filters A, B, C, and D; adduct ions included + H and + NH₄⁺ in positive ion mode, and – H, + HCOO⁻, and – 2H in negative ion mode. The major lipid classes selected for the search including glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, diacylglycerols, triacylglycerols and sterols—in both positive and negative ionization modes. Identified lipids were assigned confidence scores on a 1- to 4-star scale, with higher ratings indicating greater identification certainty. Only identifications rated at 2 stars or above were retained for downstream analysis. The integrated peak areas of all identified lipids were normalized to those of their respective class-specific internal standards. For the five most abundant molecular species within each lipid class—ranked by normalized peak area ratios—identifications were manually validated and confirmed through careful inspection of MS/MS fragmentation patterns and chromatographic peak profiles. Negative stain 4 µL purified AMO sample was applied onto the freshly plasma glow-discharged carbon grids (30 sec at 15 mA current) and stained with 2% uranyl acetate twice for 60 sec each. Images were taken with a FEI Morgagni transmission electron microscope at a magnification of 730,000 × under a defocus value of ~ − 1.5 um. Preparation and data acquisition of cryo-EM sample For cryo-EM sample preparation, the Au grids (Quantifoil, R2/1, 300 mesh) coated with single-layered graphene (gifted by Jiayue Su, Tsinghua University) were used to address the issue of AMO particles failing to enter the grid holes. The grids underwent glow discharge treatment (10 mA, 10 sec) and mounted on forceps in a Mark IV Vitrobot (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C and 100% humidity. A volume of 5 μL of the fresh AMO samples were floated on the grid for 15 sec, after which the grid was plunge-frozen in liquid ethane following blotting. Cryo-EM data of AMO was collected on a Titan Krios electron microscope operating at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector and a GIF Quantum energy filter. The automatic software-EPU 68 was used for data acquisition. Images were recorded at a pixel size of 0.668 Å under a magnification of 130,000 ×. The defocus value of each image was set from − 1.5 to − 2.0 µm during data collection. Each micrograph was dose-fractioned into 32 frames with a total dose of about 50 e – /Å 2 . Image processing The beam-induced motion of the whole micrograph was corrected by MotionCor2 1.1.0 69 . All the following data processing procedures were carried out in cryoSPARC 4.3.2 70 . Contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated by Patch CTF estimation for the AMO, and micrographs with poor CTF fit, as determined by Manually Curate Exposures were rejected. Particle picking was carried out using Blob Picker with diameter ranging from 8 to 20 nm. Auto-picked particles were extracted by a binning factor of 2. Following two-dimensional (2D) classification, good classes were used to train Topaz 71 . This dataset underwent additional rounds of 2D classification and Topaz train and extraction, repeating the process four times until no further improvements were observed. All particles from these iterations were combined, and duplicated particles were removed. A total of 382,927 particles were subjected to ab initio 3D reconstruction, followed by heterogeneous refinement to generate four EM maps. The map with AMO complex contained a total of 153,286 particles, which was subjected to two rounds of AMO heterogeneous refinement to remove the bad particles using the maps generated in the previous step as references. The best map was subsequently selected for further NU-refinement 72 , leading to an overall resolution of 2.36 Å as estimated by the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation at a correlation cutoff value of 0.143 73 . Finally, DeepEMhancer 74 was employed for post-processing the final maps to facilitate model building. Model building and refinement The predicted AlphaFold structures of AmoA, AmoB, AmoC, AmoD, and AmoE were manually docked into the cryo-EM density map using UCSF Chimera 1.12 75 and subsequently adjusted in Coot 0.8.9.1 76 according to the corresponding density and sequence information. Lipid molecules were selected based on lipidomic data and identifiable features, and were manually fitted into the map using Coot. Water molecules within the AMO complex, particularly those near copper centers, were modeled with reference to the previously reported pMMO structure (PDB: 7S4H ) and supported by mass spectrometry data 31 . Additional cytoplasmic and periplasmic water molecules were placed based on the pMMO structure (PDB: 7S4H ) and validated by the recently published AMO structure (PDB: 9CL6 ). Real-space refinement and validation of the final model were carried out using Phenix 1.14-3260, 77 . Structural figures were prepared with UCSF Chimera 1.1.2, ChimeraX 1.3 78 , and PyMOL 1.8.2.1 ( http://www.pymol.org ). Western blot The western blot assays were performed following the standard procedure. Briefly, the purified AMO samples obtained from gel chromatography were resolved on a 4–20% SDS-PAGE (Yeasen Biotechnology) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was then blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBST buffer, followed by incubating with a suitable horseradish peroxidase (HRP) labeled primary antibody (Abclonal, Cat# AE024, 1:5000) for 2 h. Visualization of the blots was achieved using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Tanon), and images were captured using ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA).
