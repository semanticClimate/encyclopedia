In particular, green synthesis of peptides, large-scale peptide production, oral peptide drugs, and peptide antibiotics are key research directions in the near future. With the growing demand for peptide drugs, peptide manufacturing enterprises have substantially expanded their production capacity. Recent announcements from several polypeptide manufacturers reveal that the annual treatment capacity of their DMF waste liquid processing facilities has reached 50,000 to 100,000 tons. However, the designed production capacity of each plant is merely 1 to 2 tons per year. These data are consistent with the findings reported in literature. Given the ratio of output to raw material consumption, these enterprises have been compelled to launch polypeptide production projects with low raw material utilization rates because advanced manufacturing technologies are not available. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop polypeptide synthesis methods that not only achieve high raw material utilization efficiency but also conform to green, environmentally friendly principles. The methods for polypeptide chemical synthesis mainly include solid-phase synthesis, liquid-phase synthesis, and enzymatic fragment ligation. The Fmoc polypeptide solid-phase synthesis, which features simple operation, mild reaction conditions, and high synthesis efficiency, is currently the most widely used method for the chemical synthesis of polypeptides. Despite these advantages, present polypeptide chemical synthesis still faces two major bottlenecks related to sustainability and crude peptide quality. First, the solvent recovery and recycling processes remain challenging. , Second, undesired side reactions may occur during the coupling and deprotection steps. These limitations in Fmoc-based polypeptide synthesis are largely associated with the use of the Fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc) protecting group. , Typically, deprotection of Fmoc requires approximately 20% piperidine solution, but this introduces 3 main issues. First, the high concentration of piperidine solution makes solvent recovery difficult in the later stage, with a low rate of solvent recycling, leading to significant environmental pressure; second, the strong alkalinity of the high-concentration organic bases solution can cause some known or unknown side reactions, − resulting in low purity and poor quality of the crude peptide. Third, according to data from the National Institutes of Health (NIH), piperidine and pyrrole pose significant health risks, which are commonly used for efficient Fmoc deprotection. Piperidine is strongly irritating to the eyes and skin, causing poisoning and burns, and can lead to adverse symptoms such as nausea and breathing difficulties. In addition, its strong unpleasant odor presents further hazards to lab personnel. Therefore, the development of environmentally friendly peptide synthesis strategies that simultaneously improve product quality and synthetic efficiency has become necessary. Several alternative approaches have been proposed to address these issues. For instance, Junfeng Zhao’s group reported peptide synthesis using Ynamide coupling reagents in combination with unprotected amino acids. In another strategy, Kounosuke Oisaki and co-workers have successfully converted the carboxyl group of the N-terminal peptide fragment into peptide thiocarboxylic acid (PTC). Under the catalysis of N -hydroxy-2-pyridinone ester­(HOPO), the PTC can be coupled with unprotected or partially protected amino acids to extend the peptide chain by one residue. Although these two methods avoid the use of the Fmoc protecting group, they remain limited. The reactions often require longer reaction times, high-temperature coupling, and cumbersome purification and separation procedures at each step, resulting in lower synthesis efficiency. Furthermore, the poor solubility of unprotected amino acids necessitates the use of nongreen solvents such as DMF and DMSO.The quality of the crude polypeptide is the most significant factor determining the production cost. − Impurities located near the base of the product peak in HPLC are often extremely difficult to separate, which can exponentially increase production costs. Moreover, theoretically, for amino acids bearing active groups on their side chains, the absence of side-chain protection would increase the likelihood of side reactions, thereby compromising the quality of the crude peptide. Particularly, this issue is critical in the manufacturing of active pharmaceutical ingredients, where even trace impurities are unacceptable. Therefore, these two methods have not yet overcome the bottlenecks encountered in polypeptide chemical synthesis. Studies have shown that modification of Fmoc can achieve the purpose of removing Fmoc under mild conditions, such as introducing a nitro group at the 2-position of Fmoc to achieve the purpose of photolytic cleavage of Fmoc (NO 2 ) ( Figure ), but this method cannot be used to prepare amino acids with side chain protecting groups. In another approach, Stefan Kolmar and co-workers introduced a sulfonate group at the 2- and 7-positions of Fmoc-Cl to obtain Smoc-Cl (the structure of Smoc is shown in Figure ), and reacted Smoc-Cl with amino acids to obtain Smoc-AA. The resulting Smoc-protected amino acids can be used for peptide synthesis in an aqueous phase. Nevertheless, in this process, the reaction of acyl chlorides with amino acids is prone to generating impurities, such as dipeptides and polypeptides, which are extremely difficult to separate, − and the cost of aqueous-phase synthesis is much higher than that of Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis, making it difficult to achieve commercial transformation. 1 Chemical structure of Fmoc (NO 2 ). 2 Chemical structure of Smoc. Results and Discussion To address the limitations of conventional Fmoc peptide synthesis, a new protecting group, 2-alkanoyl-9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl (abbreviated as Amoc), was developed by introducing an alkanoyl substituent at the 2-position of the Fmoc. The general structure of Amoc-protected amino acids ( Figure ) is as follows: 3 Chemical structure of Amoc-Amino acid. R represents the side chain of an amino acid. R 1 and R 2 are H or methyl. R 3 is an alkyl group such as a methyl, ethyl, propyl, isopropyl, or C4–30 straight-chain or branched saturated alkyl group. From the perspective of the atomic economy, methyl for R 3 is the most reasonable. The Amoc protecting group mentioned below usually refers to the structure where R 3 is methyl. In principle, peptide synthesis using Amoc-protected amino acid (Amoc-AA) follows a straightforward strategy. However, traditional peptide synthesis technologies are strongly constrained by the synthesis carrier and coupling conditions, and previously reported protecting groups such as Fmoc­(NO 2 ) and Smoc can be easily removed under organic alkaline conditions, which may lead to the perception that Amoc-AA would not be sufficiently stable for practical peptide synthesis. The peptide synthesis workflow using Amoc-AA is largely analogous to the Fmoc strategy, except that peptide coupling reactions are preferably carried out under pH conditions around neutral. The introduction of a moderate electron-withdrawing alkanoyl group at the 2-position of Fmoc facilitates efficient deprotection of Amoc using only 0.5–2% solutions of piperidine or piperazine. The concentration of the organic base is context-dependent. 1% is typically sufficient to meet the majority of Amoc deprotection conditions. This strategy significantly alleviates several problems associated with traditional Fmoc peptide synthesis, thereby enabling green peptide synthesis, improving crude peptide quality, and reducing production costs. Amoc can also be applied to peptide purification because it can be easily removed from low-concentration alkaline solutions without damaging peptides. Amoc is retained prior to peptide cleavage; the resulting Amoc-tagged peptide exhibits a substantially different retention time from the original impurities in HPLC spectra, enabling facile separation of the Amoc-tagged peptide from most impurities. Depending on the specific properties of different peptides, Amoc with varying carbon chain lengths exhibits distinct characteristics during peptide separation. Our research team will elaborate on the application of Amoc in peptide purification in subsequent papers. The superior performance of Amoc relative to Fmoc­(NO 2 ) and Smoc can be attributed to the moderate electronic passivation of the fluorenyl group induced by the alkanoyl substituent. In Figure , when preparing fluorene-protected amino acids, both groups on either side of the carbonyl are present in the formate, leading to leaving-group competition. When X is a strong electron-withdrawing group, such as a sulfonate or nitro group, the fluorene-protected product can be obtained due to the Cl atom’s greater leaving ability. However, the OSu group is more stable than the modified fluorene group, which favors the departure of the fluorenyl group instead, preventing the formation of the desired fluorene-protected product. When X is a moderately electron-withdrawing group, such as an alkyl acyl group, both the OSu group and the Cl atom can depart competitively, yielding the 9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl-protected product. Therefore, the synthesis of Fmoc­(NO 2 )-AA and Smoc-AA is only feasible when L is Cl. However, the synthesis of protected amino acids using highly reactive acyl chlorides yields many impurities that are difficult to separate. Theoretically, X can also be F or Cl atoms, but their electron-damping effect on the aromatic ring is weak, and the modified fluorene still requires a relatively concentrated organic base to be removed. 4 When the amino group attacks the carbonyl, the groups on both sides of the carbonyl have a tendency to leave. Compared with the Fmoc peptide synthesis method, the use of Amoc-AA offers significant advantages in peptide synthesis. First, the use of low-concentration organic bases to remove Amoc reduces side reactions, thereby improving the quality of the crude product. The data above indicate three main advantages of the Amoc deprotection technique. First of all, most unknown reactions can be significantly reduced by using low-concentration bases for Amoc deprotection, thereby improving crude peptide quality universally. Meanwhile, peptide synthesis using the Amoc method in 2-MeTHF generally yields higher crude peptide quality than Fmoc chemistry. Last and most important, the Amoc strategy enabled peptide synthesis without the extensive use of hazardous solutions subject to REACH restrictions. The Amoc strategy also offers significant advantages in green chemistry. In most cases, the less hazardous and odorous piperazine can replace piperidine, which is carcinogenic and extremely malodorous. From the perspective of reaction requirements, deprotection of Amoc can be performed at room temperature using 0.5–5% piperazine or piperidine solution. Piperazine has not been applied in traditional processes mainly because 20% piperazine has poor solubility in solvents such as DMF, but 0.5%–2% piperazine is completely soluble in DMF or 2-MeTHF. Using piperazine for deprotection will significantly improve the working environment of peptide enterprises. Another important benefit relates to solvent recycling. In the process of polypeptide preparation, most of the waste is waste solvents. Even though it has been reported that EtOAc/DMSO can be used as polypeptide synthetic solvents and partial solvent recovery can be achieved, the post-treatment is troublesome, and it is uncertain whether it is applicable to the preparation of other polypeptides. When Amoc-AA is used in polypeptide synthesis, low-concentration organic bases are used to remove Amoc. A small amount of organic bases can be readily adsorbed by solid acids, such as strong cation-exchange resins. After the organic bases are removed, the solution can be distilled to achieve solvent recovery and reuse. If the currently popular Fmoc process is used, high-concentration organic bases not only cause DMF to deteriorate , but also make it particularly difficult to separate a large amount of piperidine from DMF, generating a large amount of waste liquid and causing significant environmental pressure (3). In summary, regardless of the Fmoc synthesis strategy adopted, Amoc polypeptide synthesis can facilitate the recovery and reuse of solvents. Amoc-L-AA was used to prepare H-Leu-AA-Leu-OH with DIC/Oxyma pure as the condensing agent, and the racemization of the amino acids was investigated through these tripeptides. The results showed that, except for Ser, Asp and His, the optical purity of other amino acids was not affected during the coupling and deprotection of Amoc. If Amoc-L-AA is not activated during coupling, the content of D-type amino acids will drop significantly. Except for His, which shows approximately 1% racemization, the racemization levels of Ser and Asp are both less than 0.2%. The higher racemization of Amoc-AA compared to Fmoc may be attributed to the slightly greater electron-withdrawing ability of Amoc after the introduction of the fluorenyl group into the alkyl acyl group, making amino acids more prone to form oxazolone structures during coupling. The optimization of the lower-racemization-degree coupling method is expected to address the issue of a higher racemization level in some Amoc-AA. The results of this study will be reported in a subsequent paper. From an industrialization perspective, the production cost of any commodity will drop significantly once it is mass-produced. Therefore, the production cost of Amoc-AA will not be much higher than that of Fmoc-AA. In addition, the crude product purity of peptides synthesized by the Amoc method is generally higher than that of those synthesized by the Fmoc method, and the reaction solvents can be recycled. Overall, the Amoc method offers significant cost advantages over the Fmoc method for peptide synthesis. Its strengths are particularly prominent for difficult peptides that are unsuitable for preparation using the Fmoc method. Conclusion In this study, we successfully developed a new protecting group strategy based on Amoc for peptide synthesis. Compared with the conventional Fmoc method, the Amoc peptide synthesis strategy significantly improves the purity of crude peptide products and generally reduces production costs in the peptide industry. From a sustainability perspective, the Amoc method offers several advantages that address a long-standing challenge in peptide manufacturingthe recovery and recycling of waste solvents. Using low concentrations of organic bases for deprotection facilitates solvent recycling and reduces waste solvent generation. Meanwhile, using piperazine as a deprotecting reagent avoids the use of most carcinogenic or genotoxic solvents, thereby improving the working environment and personnel safety. Nevertheless, several aspects of the Amoc method still require further optimization. First of all, more efficient or Amoc-compatible condensation reagents may need to be developed. The amoc-AA coupling reaction is preferably performed under near-neutral conditions. In addition, a small number of amino acids exhibit higher racemization levels during coupling. Another limitation arises during the preparation of fully protected peptide fragments, the combination of Amoc-AA and slightly acidic additives such as HOBt and Oxyma pure may lead to fragments falling off from the resin. This problem indicates a need for alternative linkers or optimized additives. Overall, the Amoc strategy provides a promising alternative to traditional Fmoc chemistry and serves as a platform for sustainable peptide synthesis.
