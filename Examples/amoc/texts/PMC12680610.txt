The Indian summer monsoon (ISM) is a major component of the Asian monsoon system, and exerts a critical influence on regional climate variability. However, continuous, high-resolution paleoclimate records spanning a full glacial-interglacial cycle remain limited, constraining our understanding of the ISM dynamics across different timescales. In this study, we reconstruct ISM variability from 184 − 25 ka using geochemical and environmental magnetic proxies from a lacustrine sediment core in the Heqing Basin, Southwest China. Rock magnetic results indicate that higher concentration-dependent magnetic parameters (χ, ARM, SIRM) occur during weaker monsoon (drier) phases, whereas lower values reflect stronger monsoon (wetter) phases. The proxy records reveal a pronounced dry-wet cycle throughout 184 − 25 ka: Phase I (184 − 131 ka) featured relatively weak ISM intensity and drier conditions; Phase II (131 − 69 ka) saw an overall increase in ISM intensity accompanied by higher rainfall; Phase III (69 − 57 ka) reverted to arid conditions; Phase IV (57 − 25 ka) exhibited a generally wetter climate, though punctuated by frequent major drought events. On glacial-interglacial timescales, ISM variability was primarily paced by Northern Hemisphere summer insolation (NHSI). On millennial timescales, the ISM weakened during ~ 57 − 25 ka (Phase IV, MIS 3). Weak ISM, as indicated by proxies in the PT2 core, showed consistent variations with Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC) weakening, as indicated by ²³¹Pa/²³⁰Th, and with low-latitude monsoon circulation. This pattern suggests that the region was jointly controlled by high-latitude boundary conditions and tropical ocean-atmosphere coupling processes. Therefore, variations in the ISM across different timescales are driven by multiple factors rather than solely by solar radiation. Lacustrine sediments are highly sensitive to rapid climatic shifts and preserve detailed records of past climate events. Therefore, it is considered to be a valuable basis for understanding regional and global climate-system evolution. Researchers have used lacustrine sediments to investigate vegetation dynamics, monsoon mechanisms, lake level fluctuations, and erosion patterns 3 – 6 . Southwestern China lies within a unique transition zone between the Indian and East Asian monsoon domains, and the regional climate responds directly to variations in the Indian Summer Monsoon (ISM). Tectonic basins in this area contain thick, continuous lacustrine sequences that record environmental evolution over extended periods. These lacustrine sediments are important archives for reconstructing ISM variability and local environmental history 7 – 9 . However, the driving mechanisms of ISM variability from millennial to orbital timescales remain debated 10 , 11 . For example, on millennial timescales, some studies suggest that ISM variability was paced by Northern Hemisphere summer insolation (NHSI) 12 , whereas others emphasize the influence of Southern Hemisphere temperatures 13 . Uncertainties in proxy interpretation should be nonnegligible 14 . Researchers have employed a variety of proxies to investigate ISM variability, including carbon and oxygen isotopes 15 , 16 , physical parameters 13 , 17 , biological markers 8 , 18 , and geochemical proxies 19 , 20 . Different proxies often exhibit varying sensitivities to monsoon variability 21 , 22 , and interpretations of the same proxy may differ among regions 23 . Consequently, integrating multiple types of proxies is essential to overcome the limitations of single-proxy approaches 13 . Here, we present a high-resolution paleoclimate study of the Heqing Basin based on major geochemical compositions and environmental magnetic proxies spanning 184 − 25 ka. The long, continuous lacustrine sediment record captures the region’s paleoenvironmental evolution over this interval and provides a comprehensive framework for understanding ISM dynamics. We further examine ISM variability on both glacial-interglacial and millennial timescales, and discuss the likely driving mechanisms. Sampling and material The Heqing Basin is a tectonic fault basin surrounded by high mountains. It receives ~ 900–1000 mm of annual precipitation, ~ 80–90% of which falls in summer. The climate is characterized by warm, humid summers and mild, dry winters, with peak rainfall coinciding with the highest temperatures. The Pengtun profile (26°35′24.0″N, 100°11′08.2″E, 2188 m a.s.l) lies in the central part of the Heqing Basin (Fig. 1 ). The samples were collected from core 2 in Pengtun (PT2), located ~ 2 km from Heqing County; the PT2 drill core is curated at the Continental Environmental Science Core Repository of the Institute of Earth Environment, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The PT2 drill core is 33.9 m long and consists primarily of grayish-green clay and silt. Because the soil surface is disturbed, depths are referenced from 4.5 m below ground surface. From top to bottom (Fig. 2 ), the lithology is as follows: 4.5–5 m, grayish-green clay; 5–7 m, grayish-green fine sand and silt interspersed with two lignite layers in the middle section; 7–9.2 m, a layer of grayish-green silty clay at the top with grayish-green clay at the bottom; 9.2–33.9 m, grayish-green clay containing shell fragments, with visible shell accumulations at ~ 15.2–17.1 m. The maps were generated in ArcGIS 10.2, URL: http://www.esrichina-bj.cn/softwareproduct/ArcGIS/ . Research methods Approximately 10 g of bulk sample was dried at 35 °C. Low-frequency magnetic susceptibility (χ lf ) was measured at 465 Hz and high-frequency susceptibility (χ hf ) at 4650 Hz using a Bartington Instruments MS2 magnetic susceptibility meter; values were normalized by mass. The frequency-dependent susceptibility (χ fd ), calculated as χ fd = χ lf - χ hf , is widely used as an indicator of the relative concentration of superparamagnetic (SP) particles 24 . Remanence measurements were performed on U-channel samples spaced 2 cm apart using a 2G Enterprises Model 755R cryogenic magnetometer in a magnetically shielded space (< 150 nT). Stepwise alternating field demagnetization (AFD) was performed at 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 70, and 80 mT. Subsequently, an anhysteretic remanent magnetization (ARM) was produced in a 0.05 mT DC field with a peak alternating field of 80 mT. An isothermal remanent magnetization (IRM) was obtained using a pulse magnetizer (2G model 660), and IRM imparted at 1 T was regarded as SIRM. By integrating magnetic parameters with lithological variations, we selected 19 samples from both peaks and troughs for detailed rock magnetic measurements (Fig. 2 , Sample IDs increase with depth). Temperature-dependent magnetic susceptibility (χ-T) was measured using an MFK1- FA Kappabridge system and a CS- 3 high- temperature furnace, with a temperature range of 40–700 °C. First-order reversal curve (FORC) diagrams were obtained by Model 3900 vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM). The FORC diagrams were processed with FORCinelv.1.18 25 using a smoothing factor of 3–5. All magnetic measurements were conducted at the Laboratory of Environmental Magnetism, Institute of Earth Environment, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Xi’an, China). Geochemical elements concentrations of Rb, Sr, and Ti were analyzed at 5 cm intervals using an Axios advanced wavelength dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF). AMS 14 C dating was conducted at the Radiocarbon Laboratory of the Institute of Earth Environment, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Results Magnetic characteristics of PT2 drill core sediments Terrigenous input is widely regarded as the primary source of lacustrine sediments 27 . In the Heqing Basin, the overall sediment source appears relatively simple and stable, with low eolian dust flux and limited long-range sand transport 28 , 29 . Furthermore, the FORC diagrams of representative samples are dominated by low-coercivity grains and lack features diagnostic of authigenic minerals such as greigite 30 . Surface soils in the Heqing Basin contain mainly magnetite, maghemite, and hematite 31 , and the PT2 drill core shows the same mineral suite. Consequently, we propose that the magnetic minerals preserved in the Heqing paleolake may primarily originate from detrital materials transported by fluvial transport 11 , 31 . As shown in Fig. 4 , the concentration-dependent magnetic parameters (χ, ARM, and SIRM) display consistent trends, which are also consistent with χ fd . The χ and χ fd records are provided in the Supplementary Information. ARM is sensitive to single-domain (SD) particles, and χ fd is sensitive to SP particles, highlighting the important role of fine-grained particles (SD, SP) in the lake sediments of core PT2. However, ARM/SIRM commonly varies inversely with the concentration-dependent magnetic parameters; higher values of the magnetic parameters correspond to lower ARM/SIRM, indicating relatively coarser magnetic grains during those intervals 24 , 32 . The χ-T curves for all samples show a sharp decrease at ~ 580 °C, suggesting that magnetite is the predominant magnetic mineral in the PT2 drill core 30 , 33 . These curves don’t reach a minimum near 600 °C, which may imply the presence of hematite 33 , 34 (Fig. 2 ). Additionally, some χ-T heating curves from Phases I and II show a decline between 300 and 400 °C (Fig. 2 d, e, f), consistent with the thermal transformation of maghemite to hematite 35 . FORC diagrams of representative samples reveal that high-χ samples have a broad range of magnetic grain sizes, with a predominance of vortex state (Fig. 2 l-n). These samples exhibit multidomain (MD), SD, and SP particles information. Low-χ samples show greater variability in magnetic domains: some resemble high-χ samples(Fig. 2 j, k), while others display clear SD and/or SP characteristics 24 (Fig. 2 g, h, i). Fig. 4 Down-core variations in rock magnetic and geochemical parameters of the PT2 drill core. Based on stratigraphic lithology and shifts in magnetic parameters, we divide the PT2 drill core into four phases, with Phase II further subdivided into II-a and II-b. The phases differ markedly in the mean values and ranges of magnetic and geochemical proxies. Relatively elevated magnetic concentrations in Phases I and IV, together with the strong coherence between ARM and SIRM (Fig. 5 ), highlight the significant role of single-domain (SD) particles in these phases. In Phases II and III, overall magnetic concentrations are lower, and the relationships among the magnetic parameters are more complex (Fig. 5 ). χ and SIRM exhibit similar variation patterns, as do χ fd and ARM, but the two pairs differ markedly from each other. This can imply that changes in fine-grained particle content and/or domain state are not the main control on concentration in this interval. Fig. 5 Comparison of the ARM-SIRM correlation across different phases. Reductive dissolution is widespread in the lacustrine sediments of the Heqing Basin 31 , 36 – 38 . Under water-saturated, anoxic conditions, fine-grained Fe (III) oxides dissolve more readily than coarse grains, and maghemite is more susceptible to dissolution than magnetite 30 , 37 , 39 , 40 . The relatively higher abundance of maghemite in Phase I and II, inferred from decreases in χ-T curves between 300 and 400°C 36 , therefore implies weaker reductive dissolution during this interval (Fig. 2 d-f). In contrast, the lack of diagnostic maghemite signatures in Phases III and IV indicates that it may have been completely dissolved during these phases (Fig. 2 b, c). This observation supports an overall trend of increasing reductive dissolution upward in the sequence. Geochemical characteristics of PT2 drill core sediments Titanium (Ti) is relatively immobile during chemical weathering and is widely used as a proxy for terrigenous, detrital input in sediments. In the PT2 drill core, Ti correlates strongly with magnetic susceptibility (χ) at both peaks and troughs (Fig. 4 ), highlighting the impact of terrestrial inputs on the magnetic mineral content. Higher Ti commonly reflects intensified physical erosion within the catchment and enhanced delivery of detritus to the lake 41 . In contrast, the Rb/Sr ratio is usually used to assess the intensity of chemical weathering. Lower Rb/Sr values indicate stronger chemical weathering, suggesting a link to more intense monsoon precipitation environments 42 , 43 . The consistent variation of Ti and Rb/Sr values with depth illustrates an inverse relationship between physical and chemical weathering processes. The carbonate (CaCO₃) content in lacustrine sediments is controlled by catchment hydrology, weathering, and in-lake geochemical conditions, all of which are modulated by precipitation. In the Heqing Basin, CaCO₃ contents commonly increase during wet conditions, likely reflecting a combination of enhanced delivery of dissolved and particulate carbonate from the catchment and in-lake precipitation under favorable chemical conditions 44 . In the PT2 drill core, the ARM/SIRM ratio and CaCO₃ contents exhibit coherent variations (Fig. 4 ), indicating that intervals with finer magnetic grain sizes (SD, SP particles) tend to coincide with higher carbonate content. By contrast, CaCO₃ contents vary inversely with the concentration-dependent parameters χ and SIRM (Fig. 4 ). These relationships imply that intervals of elevated magnetic concentration are characterized by reduced CaCO₃ contents and relatively coarser domain state (MD, vortex state). The climatic conditions in the catchment area likely drive this pattern. During drier intervals, reduced vegetation cover enhances surface erosion and increases the delivery of coarse detritus to the lake, yielding higher magnetic mineral concentrations and coarser magnetic grain sizes. Conversely, in humid intervals, increased catchment vegetation suppresses physical erosion and terrigenous input, while enhanced limestone dissolution and chemical weathering elevate the dissolved carbonate supply, collectively lowering magnetic concentration and shifting the magnetic grain-size distribution toward finer states. Discussion Environmental evolution in the Heqing basin from 184 to 25 ka Based on the integrated responses of magnetic and non-magnetic proxies, we divide the 184 − 25 ka interval into four phases (I-IV) from bottom to top (Fig. 4 ). These phases approximately correspond to Marine Isotope Stages (MIS) 6 − 3, respectively. In Phase I (184 − 131 ka; approximately MIS 6), the lithology is relatively uniform and dominated by grayish-green clay. Concentration-dependent magnetic parameters (χ, ARM, SIRM) are elevated, and the ARM/SIRM ratio is low, indicating higher magnetic concentration coupled with coarser average magnetic grain sizes. Higher Ti values suggest enhanced physical erosion in the catchment and more detrital influx 41 . Higher Rb/Sr ratios and lower CaCO₃ contents imply reduced chemical weathering and lower monsoon precipitation 31 , 42 , 43 . The χ-T curves show an obvious maghemite component, implying comparatively weak reductive dissolution during this interval. The evidence above indicates ISM weakening and reduced rainfall during this interval. This reduction led to sparser vegetation and enhanced physical erosion, resulting in higher magnetic mineral concentrations and coarser grain sizes. In Phase II (131 − 69 ka; corresponding approximately to MIS 5), the lithology consists primarily of grayish-green clay with occasional shell fragments. Reduced concentration-dependent magnetic parameters (χ, ARM, SIRM), lower Rb/Sr ratios, and higher CaCO₃ contents collectively indicate a more humid climate and a stronger ISM. Moreover, using 100 ka as a boundary, Phase II can be subdivided into II‑a and II‑b. In Phase II-a (131 − 100 ka), χ, SIRM, Ti, and Rb/Sr are relatively high within Phase II but their amplitudes of variability are lower than in Phase I. This pattern indicates drier conditions, stronger physical erosion and more detrital influx. In Phase II-b (100 − 69 ka), χ, SIRM, Ti, and Rb/Sr reach sequence-wide minima, whereas ARM, χ fd , and ARM/SIRM ratios are relatively high. This implies finer magnetic grains and reduced detrital influx when ISM intensified. In Phase III (69 − 57 ka; approximately MIS 4), the lithology is dominated by grayish-green clay. During this interval, χ, SIRM, Ti, and Rb/Sr increase rapidly, whereas ARM/SIRM drops abruptly. This pattern indicates drier conditions when ISM weakened, subsequently increase physical erosion, and more detrital influx into the lakes. In Phase IV (57 − 25 ka; approximately MIS 3), lithological variability is more pronounced than in earlier phases. Proxy fluctuations also reach their largest amplitudes during this interval. Relative to Phase III (Fig. 4 ), concentration-dependent magnetic parameters (χ, ARM, SIRM) remain relatively low, and Ti and Rb/Sr decrease, whereas CaCO₃ contents and ARM/SIRM increase. These trends likely reflect a generally wetter climate punctuated by high-frequency drought events. The mechanism driving in the PT2 drill core sediments on glacial-interglacial timescales To reconstruct ISM variability, we employ two complementary magnetic proxies, χ and ARM. χ tracks magnetic mineral concentration, whereas ARM preferentially records fine-grained ferrimagnetic particles 45 , 46 . The two signals may differ in both strength and amplitude within the same sedimentary sequence. In the PT2 drill core record, the larger fluctuations in ARM likely reflect the greater responsiveness of fine grains to short-term climatic forcing 47 , 48 . We therefore use χ to represent concentration and ARM to emphasize the fine-grain response, and we compare both with other paleoclimate records to evaluate ISM variability on orbital timescales and to probe its potential drivers. As shown above, the phase-based analysis demonstrates that the sediments in PT2 drill core effectively record past shifts between dry and wet climates, corresponding closely to glacial-interglacial stages and phases of weakening and strengthening of the ISM. Speleothem δ 18 O records from Xiaobailong Cave (XBL) 49 , 50 (Fig. 6 e) and Sanbao Cave (SBD) 51 (Fig. 6 d) align with our records across glacial-interglacial cycles, further confirming that records of core PT2 reliably track ISM intensity. In addition, climate proxies in core PT2 (χ, ARM) correlate positively with Northern Hemisphere Summer Insolation (NHSI) at 30°N 52 (Fig. 6 f, g), suggesting that insolation paced ISM variability on orbital timescales. Higher summer insolation enhances continental heating, strengthens monsoon circulation, and increases moisture transport into the Heqing Basin 53 , 54 . Notably, the Antarctic Temperature Stack (ATS, Fig. 6 b) 55 and records in Greenland Ice-Core Project (GRIP) ice core (Fig. 6 c) 56 have in‑phase glacial-interglacial changes, indicating consistent polar variability on orbital timescales. Their close correspondence with the records in core PT2 implies that, beyond insolation, high-latitude boundary conditions (ice volume, greenhouse gases, and associated ocean-atmosphere feedbacks) modulated ISM strength 49 , 51 . Moreover, the amplitude of variability in core PT2 most closely matches sea surface temperature (SST) reconstructions from Indian Ocean core MD90963 57 (Fig. 6 a), pointing to an important low-latitude SST control. Warmer tropical Indian Ocean SSTs enhance evaporation and lower-tropospheric moisture supply, fostering convection and moisture transport into the Asian interior 58 , 59 . Taken together, we infer that from ~ 184 to 25 ka the Heqing Basin hydroclimate was paced primarily by insolation forcing and was further modulated by high-latitude boundary conditions and tropical Indian Ocean SSTs. The ISM weakening during phase IV (MIS3, 57−25 ka) On millennial timescales, AMS 14 C dating over the past 50 ka is relatively precise 60 , so we focus on ISM variations during Phase IV (57 − 25 ka; MIS 3). Ti values and the ARM/SIRM ratio are integrated with χ and ARM to clarify interpretation of ISM variability. (Fig. 7 b, c). During the period from 57 to 25 ka (Phase IV, MIS 3), the climate proxies of core PT2 (χ, Ti, ARM) and the TCQH δ¹⁸D records 61 , along with the 64PE300‑1 δ¹³C records 62 (Fig. 7 a), exhibit consistent upward trends on a millennial scale. The TCQH δ¹⁸D values gradually increase from the most negative − 220‰ to -190‰ between 57 and 25 ka, consistent with the rise in Ti in the drill core PT2. The magnetic record from drill core PT2 is consistent with the NHSI. However, the decline during the critical phase cannot be simply explained by slow variations in NHSI and instead closely linked to the high-latitude-tropical coupling. Between the two Heinrich events H4 (~ 42 − 38 ka) and H3 (~ 33 − 29 ka), Climate proxies in core PT2 show synchronous increases (Fig. 7 b, c), chronologically coinciding with the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) weakening indicated by ²³¹Pa/²³⁰Th 63 (Fig. 7 f). The same interval corresponds to Greenland cooling (low δ¹⁸O records in the Greenland NGRIP2 ice core 64 , Fig. 7 d) and relatively warmer conditions in Antarctic (high δ¹⁸O records in the Antarctic EDML ice core 65 , Fig. 7 e). This northern-southern hemisphere temperature contrast was primarily triggered by AMOC weakening 66 , 67 . AMOC weakening reduced northward oceanic heat transport, leading to Northern Hemisphere (NH) cooling and Southern Hemisphere (SH) relative warming, which in turn amplified the trans-equatorial energy gradient. This triggered southward shifts in the Hadley circulation and Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) 68 – 70 , thereby diminishing trans-equatorial water vapor transport to South Asia and weakening the ISM. This chain of events is discernible in both lake and ocean records (Fig. 7 a-c). Thus, on millennial timescales, abrupt changes in AMOC intensity and the resulting ITCZ shift, together with changes in low-latitude monsoon circulation, were the primary mechanism modulating the ISM. The NHSI appeared to provide a slowly varying background, defining the underlying climate baseline, while specific millennial-scale jumps and oscillations were dominated by high-latitude boundary conditions and tropical ocean-atmosphere coupling processes. Notably, the negative correlation between ARM and ARM/SIRM was weaker during ~ 57 − 40 ka, potentially linked to reduced absolute ARM values due to enhanced reductive dissolution in the upper sediment. This process may locally affect particle-scale indicators but does not alter the regional-scale climate interpretation described above. Conclusions The lacustrine sediments from the PT2 drill core in the Heqing Basin provide valuable insights into the environmental and climatic evolution of the region since approximately 184 − 25 ka. The magnetic and non-magnetic results reveal four distinct phases of ISM evolution in this region: Phase I (184 − 131 ka), characterized by relatively weak ISM strength and a drier climate; Phase II (131 − 69 ka), marked by an overall increase in ISM intensity with enhanced rainfall; Phase III (69 − 57 ka), representing a renewed shift toward drier conditions; and Phase IV (57 − 25 ka), showing a generally humid climate, though frequently punctuated by significant drought events. Overall, the proxies in the PT2 drill core indicate a pattern of alternating dry and wet climatic conditions corresponding to glacial-interglacial cycles. On glacial-interglacial timescales, variations in the magnetic records were primarily influenced by NHSI and modulated by high-latitude boundary conditions and tropical Indian Ocean SSTs. On millennial timescales, the ISM weakened during 57 − 25 ka (Phase IV, MIS 3), with high-latitude boundary conditions and tropical ocean-atmosphere coupling processes jointly governing this attenuation.
