Abstract Earth’s climate has experienced abrupt millennial-scale climate variability (MCV) at least since the early Pleistocene, yet their evolution and drivers remain elusive. A 3.5-million-year decadal-to-centennial-resolved lake sediment sequence from the monsoonal Tibetan Plateau reveals a stepped evolution of MCV that differs in detail from that of North Atlantic records. Lithological and pollen changes show precursor millennial events during the interval ~ 3.5-2.7 million years ago (Ma), with the onset of multiple events at ~ 2.8 Ma. From ~ 2.7 Ma, MCV became a permanent feature of glacial periods, intensifying after ~ 1.5 Ma and extending over both glacial and interglacial periods. The embedded astronomical frequencies of MCV and their persistent linkage with precession indicate a tropical insolation origin, driven by changes in the latitudinal temperature gradient and propagated to the Tibetan Plateau via monsoonal circulation. These findings highlight that both tropical forcing and high-latitude ice-dynamic mechanisms should be considered as “dual engines” of MCV. Introduction The appearance and evolution of MCV and its interaction with orbital-scale climate variability over the course of the Plio-Pleistocene represent a fundamental, yet imperfectly understood, aspect of the great Ice Ages 1 – 6 . High-resolution records from key regions beyond the North Atlantic are critically needed to build a more complete picture of the character of MCV for different parts of the Earth System. Moreover, continuous records extending beyond the Quaternary can provide additional insights into the drivers of MCV (e.g., high- vs. low-latitude sources), as the climatic background state changed from the warm conditions of the Mid-Pliocene 7 through the intensification of Northern Hemisphere glaciation (iNHG) in the Plio-Pleistocene 8 . However, existing terrestrial records cover only discrete time windows 3 , 9 – 11 or are of insufficient time-resolution 12 (Methods), hindering the development of a long-term perspective of MCV. The Tibetan Plateau (TP) is one such key region because it affects the Asian monsoon circulation and interacts with the global climate system 13 , 14 . To date, the only long and continuous paleoclimate archive for the monsoonal TP is the previously reported Zoige paleolake record (core ZB13-C2), spanning the last ~1.7 million years 2 . Here, we extend the record to 3.5 Ma at decadal-centennial resolution and trace the evolution of MCV from the warmer Pliocene period to the latest Pleistocene. Results and discussion Core acquisition, chronology and climate proxies A new drilling (ZB19-C1) campaign was conducted in central Zoige Basin (ZB, 33°58.052′ N, 102°20.163′E, ~3442 m a.s.l; Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1 ). A 1084.67-m new core to bedrock was retrieved with 95.4% recovery. The top 583 m is nearly identical to ZB13-C2 in lithology. The newly obtained deeper part consists of grayish clay of lacustrine origin (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). Magnetostratigraphic measurements and X-ray fluorescence (XRF) scanning (~6-yr average resolution) were carried out for the entire new ZB19-C1 (Methods). Because the magnetic polarity zonation and XRF data from the top 583-m are unambiguously correlative with ZB13-C2 (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3 ), pollen (~700-yr average resolution) 15 and carbonate content (Carb%, ~500-yr average resolution) were analyzed only for the 500.00–1084.67 m interval of ZB19-C1 (Methods). The age models of ZB19-C1 were constructed following the same protocols of ZB13-C2 (ref. 2 ; Methods). Those for the lower part were developed by tuning the arboreal pollen abundance (AP%) to the combination of eccentricity, obliquity and precession (ETP) 16 and to the LR04 benthic isotope stack 17 , using geomagnetic reversals as initial age controls (Supplementary Figs. 3 , 4 ). Comparisons between this initial age model and the tuned ages aligned to ETP and the LR04 stack show close similarities (Supplementary Fig. 4b ). The ages based on the ETP correlation were used unless specified otherwise (Methods). The sedimentation rate remains generally stable, showing no large shifts. The combination of the ZB13-C2 and ZB19-C1 data provides a continuous, multi-proxy terrestrial record with decadal-to-centennial sampling resolution to study TP monsoonal climate evolution over the past 3.5 Ma (Fig. 1 ; Methods). As such, the ZB record is among the very few continuous terrestrial climate archives of similar resolution and timespan at the global scale. We use the Rb/Sr ratio, Carb% and AP% as the main climate proxies (see details in Methods). Rb/Sr at ZB primarily reflects chemical weathering intensity of the catchment and associated run-off 2 , 18 , with lower Rb/Sr values indicating stronger weathering and higher run-off (Supplementary Fig. 5 ). Carbonate mainly results from authigenic chemical precipitation and is closely related to lake-water level in the study region, with higher Carb% representing higher lake levels 2 . Both proxies are primarily associated by regional precipitation although a temperature influence cannot be entirely ruled out. AP% mainly reflects regional summer temperature, with a possible influence of drought stress during weak monsoon and glacial intervals. Recurrence analyses of the three proxies coherently reveal significant regime shifts at 2.73 Ma, 1.54 Ma and 0.62 Ma (Methods; Supplementary Fig. 6 ). We performed ensemble empirical mode decomposition (EEMD) analyses 19 to reconstruct their supra-orbital, orbital and millennial components (Fig. 1d ). These components of the three proxies are essentially comparable with subtle differences. They further confirm the three change points identified by the recurrence analysis. Hereafter, we discuss MCV features primarily based on Rb/Sr data because of its consistent multi-year resolution, which provides a comprehensive evolutionary history of millennial climate variability (Methods). Long-term and orbital-scale variation The supra-orbital components of Rb/Sr, AP% and Carb% consistently reveal a feature of stepped cooling and drying with sharp transitions at 2.73 Ma, 1.54 Ma and 0.62 Ma (Fig. 1d ). These boundaries broadly correspond to major global cooling steps of the past 3.5 Ma (Fig. 2 ) as are marked by expansions of polar ice sheets 17 , 20 , lowering of reconstructed CO 2 21 – 25 and decreases in land 12 , 26 , 27 and sea surface temperatures 28 , 29 , suggesting a causal link with global cooling and CO 2 lowering. The orbital-scale components of all the proxies show clear ~100-ka, ~40-ka, and ~20-ka cycles (Supplementary Fig. 7 ), reflecting the main astronomical frequencies 16 , 30 . These cyclicities are also evident in the proxy timeseries when using the initial age model based solely on 14 C, OSL and magnetic reversal stratigraphy (Supplementary Fig. 4c ; Methods), indicating their robustness. Their relative power varies through time with prominent shifts also occurring at ~2.7, ~1.5 and ~0.6 Ma. During the interval of ~3.5–2.7 Ma, variations in Rb/Sr, AP% and Carb% are predominantly characterized by both ~23-ka and ~19-ka cycles, reflecting the strong control of low-latitude summer insolation on the TP monsoonal climate. The strengthening of the ~40-ka signal in Rb/Sr and Carb% from ~3 Ma is consistent with the persistently low amplitude of summer insolation 16 and the intensification of Northern Hemisphere glaciation. From ~2.7 to ~1.5 Ma, the ~20-ka cycle remains predominant in AP% and continues to be distinct in Rb/Sr and Carb% when ~40-ka cycle significantly intensifies. During the interval from ~1.5 to 0 Ma, the prominent features include a noticeably weakened ~20-ka cycle, particularly for Rb/Sr, as well as the emergence (~1 Ma) and subsequent establishment (~0.62 Ma) of the ~100-ka cycle in all three proxies. The strengthening of ~40-ka and ~100-ka cycles indicate the impact of the step-wise intensification of global cooling. The consistently stronger ~20-ka signals in AP% relative to the Rb/Sr and Carb% records suggest a greater sensitivity of temperature to insolation than that of precipitation. The persistent impact of precession has also been revealed by some previous records from Asian monsoon region 27 , differing from the high-latitude region that recorded the dominant obliquity influence after iNHG 31 . The difference can be explained by the varying weighting of high-latitude versus low-latitude influences in different regions. Millennial climate variability: glacial and orbital states The MCV signals at ZB consist of three main components with the frequencies centered at ~13–9-ka, ~7–4-ka, and ~3–2-ka (Methods; Fig. 2 ; Supplementary Fig. 8 for sensitivity examination), sharing ~43%, ~26%, and ~18% of the total millennial variance in Rb/Sr respectively (Supplementary Data 2 ). A ~ 2–1-ka quasi-periodicity is also identified. These multi-frequencies consistently show stepped intensifications at ~2.7 Ma and ~1.5 Ma, with the pronounced transition occurring at ~1.5 Ma (Fig. 2 ). The relationships of the MCV amplitude to the orbital and glacial cycles also vary across these boundaries. During the interval of ~3.5–2.7 Ma, MCV signals, mostly coupled with low values in the precession parameter (northern summer insolation highs), are observed in either glacial or interglacial stages but with weak intensity in interglacials (Fig. 3 ). The amplitude is considerably lower than in subsequent intervals; however, the MCV remains visible, as supported by the observed precursor events (Fig. 4 ) and by EEMD analysis that reveals MCV accounts for ~7% of the total variability of Rb/Sr during this period (Supplementary Data 2 ). Relatively high-amplitude MCV is detected only in some colder glacial stages, such as MIS-MG4, M2, KM4, G22, G18 and G10, suggesting that sufficiently cold conditions are critical for MCV. MIS G10 marks the onset of prominent multiple millennial-scale events. However, MCV is weak during the cold glacials of KM2 and G20, which instead coincide with precession maxima, suggesting its non-linear relationship with glacial strength. What emerges is that high-amplitude MCV at ZB systematically occurs during the low-obliquity glacials that are coupled with low precession. From ~2.7 to ~1.5 Ma, high-amplitude MCV becomes a persistent feature of glacial periods but remains subdued during interglacial stages (Fig. 3 ; Supplementary Fig 9a ). Since glacial-interglacial changes of this interval are dominated by ~40-ka obliquity cycles 17 , high-amplitude MCV mostly aligns with low obliquity. This yields the ~40-ka amplitude modulations (Fig. 2c ; Supplementary Fig. 10 ). MCV signals are significantly stronger within colder glacial stages associated with precession minima, including MIS-G6, MIS−104, 100, 96, 92, 88, 82, 78, 76, 74, 64, 62, 60, 58, 54 and 52 (Fig. 3 ). Within these cold glacial stages, high-amplitude MCV signals, with the dominant frequencies centered at ~10-ka and ~5-ka, yield a “square-wave” structure of multiple millennial events (Fig. 5 ). However, such features become much less prominent when low obliquity coincides with high precession: this is the case for MIS-G2, MIS−104, 98, 86, and 70 (Fig. 3 ), despite MIS-98 being among the coldest glacial stages. Again, this underlines their non-linear relationship with glacial strength, pointing to the fundamental role of precession. Intensified MCV since ~2.7-2.6 Ma was also noted in a loess grain-size record covering the interval of 3.7−1.9 Ma 9 and a 3.6-Ma grain size stack from North China 27 (Fig. 4 ) reflecting the Asian winter monsoon strength, although their exact relationship with the orbital parameters was not resolved. However, these detailed “square-wave” multiple events revealed by the multi-year resolved Rb/Sr at ZB have not yet captured by previous monsoonal records with coarser resolution. From ~1.5 Ma onwards, strong MCV becomes a persistent feature for both glacial and interglacial stages (Figs. 3 , 6 ). High MCV amplitude occurred during certain interglacial peaks (defined by refs. 32 , 33 ), e.g., MIS stages 7e, 11c, 15a, 15e, 17, 19c, 21 31, 35 and 43 (Figs. 3 , 6 ). Strong MCV also occurred at some continued interglacials (referring to ref. 34 ), the preceding glacial maximum/termination and subsequent glacial descent/interglacial demise. Notably, the last two glacials (MIS-2-3-4 and MIS-6) exhibited stronger MCV compared to the adjacent interglacials (MIS-5e and 7c). Nonetheless, high-amplitude MCV no longer systematically aligns with low obliquity, but near-systematically matches precession minima whose amplitudes are largely modulated by the ~100-ka eccentricity cycle (Supplementary Fig. 11 ). Bispectral analyses of Zoige Rb/Sr (Methods) further reveal that ~87% of the variance in the half-precession MCV signals over the last ~0.6 Ma is transferred from precession variations (Supplementary Fig. 12 ). These findings systematically revealed their precession modulation feature, extending beyond previous observations about their general relationships with glacial states and precession 27 , 35 . Collectively, these MCV-orbital relationships significantly differ from those observed in the ~3.5–1.5 Ma interval, suggesting a fundamental dynamic change. Among the longer records of MCV, only a few continuous records from the North Atlantic extend back to the Pliocene 20 , 31 , 36 – 40 (Fig. 7 ) while others are mostly restricted to the last 1.5 Ma 1 , 3 (Fig. 6 ). ZB shares some common features with these older records, including the sub-precession (~11 and ~5.5-ka) signals in the mid-late Quaternary 41 , the ~40-ka amplitude modulation of MCV in the early Quaternary, the substantial increase in the MCV intensity at ~2.7 Ma 31 and ~1.5 Ma 20 , and the clearer precession-modulation of MCV amplitude since the MPT 1 . These similarities suggest that the MCV signals in the monsoon zone and North Atlantic are dynamically linked. ZB, however, shows several major differences from the North Atlantic records, indicating that the expressions of MCV are regionally variable despite being dynamically linked. First, ZB provides robust evidence of the MCV presence during the interval of ~3.5-2.73 Ma, an intensified MCV at MIS G10, and multiple millennial events occurring during MIS G10, G6, G4, and 104, while these signals are essentially absent in most North Atlantic records 20 , 40 . Only the higher latitude sites north of 50°N, e.g., ODP 984, U1314 and ODP 611 show millennial-scale variability related to small-scale ice-rafting debris (IRD) events during glacial stages of the late Pliocene 36 , 38 , 39 . Multiple IRD events did not begin until MIS 100 38 , 40 . The recently published centennial-resolution record from North Atlantic site U1385 revealed the onset of MCV at ~2.7 Ma heralded by isolated precursor events and the first occurrence of multiple millennial events during MIS 100 (ref. 31 ) (Fig. 4 ). These findings, derived from high-resolution data, strongly corroborate earlier observations. Second, the number and geometric structures of “square-wave” multiple millennial events differ between ZB and site U1385. ZB captures a greater number of multiple events, especially during the transitions into and out of glacial states (Supplementary Fig. 13 ), and generally exhibits a sinusoidal structure for individual events (Supplementary Fig. 14 ) that closely resembles that of insolation. In contrast, U1385 displays a more asymmetric shape for stadial-interstadials—likely associated with ice-rafted debris. Third, the strongest MCV signals for the ~2.7–1.5 Ma interval at ZB appear in those glacial stages that are characterized by low precession and obliquity values (Fig. 3 ), while in the North Atlantic sites MCV occurs during all glacials when global benthic δ¹⁸O values exceed a certain threshold 31 , irrespective of the sign of precession 20 , 31 , 36 . In particular, MCV amplitude in cold MIS 98 is not subdued in North Atlantic records 20 , 31 . Fourth, over the past ~1.5 Ma, prominent MCV at ZB occurs in both glacials and interglacials, while high-latitude marine records (e.g., U1385, U1308) 1 , 20 , 31 , 36 , 42 show pronounced MCV only for glacial inception and continued ice growth periods under conditions of declining insolation forced mainly by obliquity and secondarily by precession 1 , although interglacial MCV started to emerge since the MPT in some mid-latitude North Atlantic 1 , 43 , and terrestrial records 3 , 44 (Fig. 6 ). Origin and dynamics of millennial climate variability Internal climate processes, such as the high-latitude Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) 45 , ice-related freshwater fluxes 46 and sea ice 47 , orbitally induced insolation changes 48 , 49 and atmospheric CO 2 50 , have been proposed to explain the origin of MCV in the climate system. In terms of orbital forcing, the double maximum and the maximum seasonal contrast of daily irradiation received in tropical latitudes over the course of the year can generate ~11-ka, ~5.5-ka, and ~2.5-ka cycles in equatorial insolation 48 . Interactions derived from anti-phased low-latitude insolation changes between the Northern and Southern Hemispheres may also generate half-precession signals 5 . The changes in suborbital-millennial signals of tropical insolation (MTI) may play an active role in the response of the climate system to astronomical forcing 48 . These precession harmonics has been recorded in tropical thermocline temperature 51 , low-mid-latitude climate 10 and in polar ice cores 52 . Since MTI arises from precession harmonics, their amplitudes are inherently modulated by eccentricity, with stronger signals during precession-lows and eccentricity-highs. The discrepancies in MCV features between ZB and North Atlantics, specifically regarding the onset of MCV, the detailed structure of multiple millennial events, and their relationship with glacial and precession, suggest that the primary MCV signals at ZB was not directly sourced from the high-latitude, ice-associated processes. The main frequencies of MCV at ZB are highly correlative with MTI (Fig. 7 ), and hence, are strongly indicative of a tropical origin. This is consistent with the prevailing monsoon circulation at ZB, which is extremely sensitive to tropical insolation changes. The persistent linkage of MCV at ZB with precession minima (strong MTI), along with the enhanced MCV that are phase-coupled with eccentricity-highs (high MTI amplitude) over the past 1.5 Ma (Supplementary Fig. 11 ), strongly supports this interpretation. This is further corroborated by bicoherence analyses of ZB Rb/Sr (Methods) showing that ~10-ka, ~5 ka and 2.5-ka signals could be produced through nonlinear interactions of precession and sub-precession oscillations (Supplementary Fig. 15 ; Supplementary Data 3 ). It is also in line with an earlier study showing that ~76% and ~40% of the half-precession signals in the tropical and mid-latitude North Atlantic, respectively, were nonlinearly transferred from precession-band variations during the Mid-late Pleistocene 41 . Similar energy transfers from astronomical periodicities to multi-millennial variability (12.5–2.5 ka bandwidth) have also been identified in Greenland records 53 . A fundamental driver of low-latitude insolation forcing is further confirmed by the simulated summer precipitation changes in the Zoige Basin region over the last 800 ka, based on transient climate simulations performed with LOVECLIM1.3 (Methods). The ~10-ka, ~5 ka and 2.5-ka signals in summer precipitation can be robustly reproduced driven by orbital forcing alone (Fig. 8 ). Moreover, the half-precession signal in the simulated summer precipitation is clearly in phase with bi-hemisphere maximum insolation, supporting a dynamical connection. However, the stepwise intensification of MCV at ~2.7 Ma, and ~1.5 Ma is not directly linked to MTI changes, as insolation lacks any long-term shifts 16 . Instead, these transitions coincide with times of major global cooling 17 , 28 , suggesting that the propagation of MTI signals to higher latitudes and its amplification by shifts in global ice-boundary conditions also play a critical role in exerting a pervasive influence in the climate system. Climate models 54 – 56 have demonstrated that monsoon circulation (of tropical origin) and ocean circulation (in particular AMOC across the tropics) are the primary mechanisms transmitting low-latitude climate signals to high-latitudes. Both data and simulations support that the general circulation in the atmosphere and ocean can be fueled by the energy gradient between low and high latitudes, through changing the Hadley Cell, westerlies, subtropical high, ITCZ, and monsoon extent 57 – 59 . For example, a higher gradient would likely shift the monsoon system equatorward, amplifying MCV, especially along its poleward margins 57 . Reduced obliquity strengthens the latitudinal insolation gradient/temperature gradient, thereby increasing the meridional heat and moisture flux to higher latitudes 59 . The long-term and orbital-scale latitudinal temperature gradient is also strongly determined by the ice-boundary conditions 17 and atmospheric CO 2 concentration 21 – 25 . The close correlation between the MCV intensity changes at ZB and the tropical-extratropical temperature gradient (Fig. 7a ; Supplementary Fig. 9b ) in the past 3.5 Ma further confirm the important role of the temperature gradient in propagating the tropical climate signals to higher latitudes. These mechanisms can therefore explain the MCV temporal evolution at ZB and its key differences from the high-latitude records. During the warm Pliocene interval when the latitudinal temperature gradient was generally small, MTI-induced tropical climate signals that were stronger during precession-lows, were substantially propagated to ZB only during some cold glacial stages with low obliquity and relatively higher gradient. However, the warmer climate context, the general suppression of MCV in the North Atlantic sites (Fig. 7 ), and low latitudinal temperature gradient meant that the abrupt change signals were not propagated to higher latitudes (Supplementary Fig. 9c ). From ~2.7 to 1.5 Ma, the iNHG at ~2.7 Ma, as well as a drop in CO 2 concentrations (Figs. 2 b, 4 ), substantially enhanced the temperature gradient of glacial stages (Fig. 7 ). Thus, tropical signals could be largely propagated to ZB during glacial periods. Given their divergence from North Atlantic records before ~1.5 Ma, most MCV signals at ZB were likely derived directly from the tropics through the Asian summer monsoon circulation, though the influence of AMOC could not be entirely ruled out, particularly during the coldest Quaternary glacials. This propagation mechanism further advances previous insights concerning the influence of monsoon system and Northern Hemisphere cryosphere on the MCV amplitude transition at ~2.7-2.6 Ma 9 , 27 . The substantially enhanced MCV at ZB since ~1.5 Ma is unambiguously coupled with a further intensified latitudinal temperature gradient driven by a major cooling step (Fig. 7 ). The gradient became sufficiently great to cause the propagation of MTI signals to ZB for both glacial and interglacial stages. This pattern is also recorded by the increase of interglacial MCV amplitude since the MPT in some mid-latitude terrestrial records (Fig. 6 ). Another alternating lacustrine-deltaic sand record from monsoonal China revealed a persistent interglacial MCV in the last 1.9 Ma, but the glacial MCV signal in its sediments is mostly muted 11 . Enhanced signal transmission from the tropics to higher latitudes is additionally supported by marine records 55 , which indicate that tropical semi-precession cycles (with periods of about 11.5 ka) started to propagate to higher latitudes since ~1.5 Ma. The stronger MCV at ZB during either interglacial peaks, continued interglacials or at glacial-interglacial transitions over the past 1.5 Ma can be reasonably attributed to the 100-ka eccentricity cycle modulation of MTI signals, which intensifies when precession amplitudes are high (Supplementary Fig. 11 ). The stronger ~10-ka, ~5 ka and ~2.5-ka signals generally corresponding to large eccentricity in the simulated summer precipitation at ZB driven by orbital forcing alone (Fig. 8 ) further support this explanation. This suggests that at least a large part of the MCV signal at ZB was directly sourced from the tropics via the summer monsoon. Because the presence of strong MCV may prevent an ice sheet from reaching its maximum size 20 , the enhanced northwards transmission of tropical MTI signals modulated by eccentricity since ~1.5 Ma would also favor in sharpening the late Quaternary ~100-ka glacial cycles. A previous study 60 proposed that orbital-scale, low-latitude interhemispheric insolation gradients could also shape global ice volume dynamics via monsoon dynamics, highlighting the possible tropical role on different timescales in influencing the glacial-interglacial variability. However, part of the glacial MCV signal of the last 1.5 Ma at ZB would have resulted from both the high-latitude feedback to transmitted MTI signals and the ice-associated origins through AMOC (Supplementary Fig. 9c ), given the strong MCV signals observed in the North Atlantic. This is supported by the enhanced ~1.5-ka-periodicity variabilities at ZB (Fig. 2a ), which may reflect a “fast” Dansgaard-Oeschger mode associated with AMOC dynamics 53 . This interpretation aligns with the geological evidence 3 , 35 and model outputs 56 , both confirming AMOC’s impacts on the Asian monsoon. In this case, AMOC would have acted as an amplifier of MCV signals and their pervasive influence since ~1.5 Ma. Specifically, the strong MCV during MIS-2-3-4 and MIS-6 is likely attributable to high-latitude feedback through AMOC, as is concurrent with Antarctica ice core and most of the MCV records spanning the last two glacial cycles 61 – 63 . Taken together, the decadal-to-centennial resolved ZB record represents a rare terrestrial archive from the Tibetan Plateau, a region of key global significance. It provides an independent and detailed history of MCV for the Asian monsoon area that diverges from the high-latitude MCV evolution over the past 3.5 Ma. Its specific location offers an opportunity for addressing the origin and dynamics of MCV in the climate system. We found strong evidence that MCV is initially triggered by orbital forcing and, in particular, by precession-related tropical insolation changes. Our results indicate that changes in latitudinal temperature gradient and obliquity have been responsible for signal propagation to the Tibetan Plateau. The monsoon circulation has played a critical role in this propagation. The combined mechanism of tropical origin and signal propagation provides an integrative framework for understanding MCV evolution in Asian monsoon region. These findings underscore that a comprehensive understanding of MCV requires integrating both tropical forcing and high-latitude ice-dynamic mechanisms. This study also carries broad implications for understanding future abrupt climate change, cross-scale climate dynamics, ecosystem responses, and early hominid evolution. Methods Regional settings The Zoige Basin is a low-relief tectonic basin (32°10′‒34°10′N, 101°45′‒103°25′ E, ca. 3350‒3450 m a.s.l) on the eastern Tibetan Plateau (TP) (Fig. 1 ). The surrounding mountains exceed 4000 m in elevation. At present, the Yellow River flows into the western part of the basin. However, a huge lake occupied the basin at least since the early Pleistocene, until it was finally drained about 30‒20 ka ago when the Yellow River cut through the mountain barrier to the east 2 , 64 . Mean annual temperature (MAT) at the nearby Zoige meteorological station at an elevation of 3439 m a.s.l is ca. 1.4 °C, with a mean July temperature of ca. 11 °C and a mean January temperature of ca. −9 °C. Mean annual precipitation (MAP) is about 650 mm (Supplementary Fig. 1 ) 2 . Most precipitation falls as rain during the summer months (June–September), due to the influence of the Asian summer monsoon (Fig. 1 ). The Zoige Basin is currently covered by subalpine meadows. A distinct elevational vegetation gradient occurs in the mountains surrounding the Zoige Basin 2 , 65 . The modern vegetation distribution in this alpine forest-meadow ecotonal region is primarily controlled by temperature, given the relatively high precipitation and low evaporation 2 , 15 . Seismic survey, drilling operation, and sediment lithology A seismic survey was conducted in the sedimentary centre of the paleolake using Tromino 3 G seismometers 2 . Seismic reflection profiles were used to select the site and portray an undisturbed sedimentary section that was not impacted by erosion. Scientific deep drilling in the sedimentary center of the paleolake of the Zoige Basin was performed in June‒August 2019. A 1084.67-m core (ZB19-C1: 33°58.052′ N, 102°20.163′E, 3442 m a.s.l.), ~400 m southeast of the previous ZB13-C2 core 2 , reaching the basal rock was retrieved, with a recovery of 95.4%. No significant hiatuses were detected except a sampling hiatus from 2964 to 2934 ka. The depth was calibrated from the initial drilling after considering errors caused by sediment expansion in some sections, loss of samples, and drilling deviation. The PVC tubes were cut into two halves, one half for XRF scanning and laboratory analysis, and the other for archiving. The lithology of the sediment core mainly consists of clay, silty-clay, and clayey-silt, except for two fluvial sandy layers (10.18 m and 9.14 m thick) (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). The top 583 m of the core is identical to the previously drilled core ZB13-C2 (~400 m away from ZB19-C1) covering the last ~1.7 Ma 2 , and the bottom section is dominated by fine bluish-grey clay of lacustrine origin. The persistent presence of aquatic pollen and fresh-water Ostracoda taxa 15 confirm the fresh-water nature of the lake deposits. The high recovery rate of core drilling, the predominance of lacustrine-originated clay sediments, straightforward correlation with orbital parameters, and the stable sedimentation rate collectively indicate the near-continuous nature of the sediments, suggesting the absence of significant hiatuses 15 . Laboratory analyses Paleomagnetic measurements 3877 samples of 2-cm-edge cubes from core ZB19-C1 were used for paleomagnetic measurements 15 . Before demagnetization, anisotropy of magnetic susceptibility (AMS) measurements were performed using a KLY-4s Kappabridge (Agico Ltd., Brno) in the Paleomagnetism and Geochronology Laboratory (PGL) of Institute of Geology and Geophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. AMS results indicate that the sediments have remained undisturbed since their deposition and are suitable for paleomagnetic analysis. Rock magnetic investigations show that the main magnetic carrier is low coercivity ferrimagnetic mineral dominated by magnetite, suggesting that stepwise alternating field (AF) demagnetization is a suitable approach in this study. Remanence measurements were then made using a 2G-760 cryogenic superconducting magnetometer in a magnetically shielded space (<300 nT) at PGL. All the 3877 samples were subjected to stepwise AF demagnetization up to 75 mT at 2.5−10 mT intervals. A total of 2372 samples gave reliable characteristic remanent magnetization (ChRM) directions. For the samples whose AF demagnetization results were not satisfactory, thermal demagnetization was further measured using a TD-48 thermal demagnetizer. They were stepwise heated to 680 °C at 10–50 °C increments. Both AF and thermal demagnetizations can effectively remove viscous components of magnetization after 15–20 mT or 250 °C, respectively. Seventy-two percent of the samples yielded stable and reliable ChRM directions. The recognized magnetic polarities were then correlated with the geomagnetic polarity time scale (GPTS) 66 (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2 ). Two main geomagnetic reversals, the Matuyama-Brunhes boundary and the Gauss-Matuyama boundary, were clearly identified at 275.07 m and 923.7 m. The subchrons in the Matuyama reverse chron, including Jaramillo (346.94–369.55 m), Cobb Mountain (398.31–403.89 m), Olduvai (610.81−672.35 m), and Reunion (740.65–742.41 m), and the reverse subchrons in the Gauss chron, including the Kaena (1023.45−1034.89 m) and the Mammoth (1050.03−1067.55 m) were also clearly identified. The geomagnetic polarity pattern of the upper part of ZB19-C1 are consistent with those of ZB13-C2, which was constrained by paleomagnetic measurements for 3018 samples 2 . Rb/Sr ratio and CIA estimates Scanning-X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis was performed with the same instrument and protocol as were applied to ZB13-C2 (ref. 2 ). It was performed on split core surfaces at 2-mm increments and 10-s dwell time using an ITRAX XRF core scanner, equipped with a Mo-tube that was set to 30 kV and 30 mA. Measured element counts was calibrated in Q-Spec (ITRAX, Cox Ltd., Sweden), based on a fitting procedure for the actual spectrum to the mathematical models of characteristic x-ray lines and spectral background. Peak area numbers, which could be a direct measure of the elemental concentration, were evaluated by a fitting procedure based on the nonlinear set of coefficients to minimize a chi-square quantity (MSE) with Q-Spec. The energy scale and peak width parameters were adjusted to ensure the optimal fitting of the actual spectrum to the mathematical models of characteristic X-ray lines and spectral background. Rb/Sr ratio was estimated based on the calibrated element counts. To evaluate the climatic proxy indication of XRF-scanning-derived Rb/Sr ratios and the relationship between Rb/Sr and the chemical index of alteration (CIA), an indicator of chemical weathering, the elemental concentrations (Al, K, Na, Ca, Rb, Sr) of 1035 bulk sediment samples from cores ZB13-C2 (ref. 2 ) and ZB19-C1 were measured on an Axios advanced wavelength dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (WD-XRF; PANalytical, Ea Almelo, The Netherlands). Sample preparation involved air-drying followed by grinding and homogenization in an agate mortar. The carbonate fraction was then removed by treating the homogenized samples with 2 mol/L HCl, after which they were thoroughly rinsed four times with deionized water and dried. For analysis, 5 g of each prepared sample was pressed into a 32 mm diameter disc under a force of 30 tonnes (tf). The CIA, expressed as the molecular ratio, was calculated using the formula: CIA = 100 *Al 2 O 3 /(Al 2 O 3 + Na 2 O + K 2 O + CaO). X-ray diffraction (XRD) mineral analysis Two grams of sediment from 80 samples through the combined core of ZB13-C2 and ZB19-C1 were used for XRD mineral analysis. They were first thoroughly ground to a fine powder (approximately 325 mesh) in an agate mortar. The powder was then packed and leveled into the cavity of a glass sample holder using a clean glass slide to ensure a flat, uniform surface. Phase identification and semi-quantitative analysis were performed using an X’Pert Pro X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA). Data were collected in a 2 θ range with a step size of 0.017° and a counting time of 42.545 s per step. Mineral types were identified, and their relative percentages were estimated using the instrument’s accompanying X’Pert HighScore Plus software. Loss-on-ignition (LOI) measurements Volumetric subsamples of ~2 cm 3 of 3648 samples from core ZB19-C1 were used for LOI analysis. The samples were weighed and oven-dried at 105 °C to estimate moisture content. LOI at 500 °C was then used to estimate organic matter content. The weight lost at 950 °C was multiplied by 1.36 to derive the carbonate content (Carb%) 67 . The combined core of ZB19-C1 and ZB13-C2 (ref. 2 ) consists of 6921 samples for carbonate content estimate. Pollen analysis A total of 5000 pollen subsamples of ~2 cm 3 in volume were analyzed for the combined cores of ZB19-C1 and ZB13-C2. All the samples were processed following standard procedures 68 , including HCl, KOH, HF, heavy liquid flotation, and acetolysis treatments, as well as fine sieving to remove clay-sized particles. At least 300 terrestrial pollen grains were counted for each sample. The pollen percentages were calculated based on the sum of the terrestrial pollen taxa 69 . Only arboreal pollen abundance (AP%) was used for the data analysis and discussion, because it represents the principal component axis 1 of the pollen assemblages 15 . The pollen results have been presented in ref. 15 ., but only for discussion about vegetation regime transformation. Establishment of age-depth model and composite record First-order tie points are provided by radiocarbon and luminescence dates 2 and geomagnetic reversals. As discussed in ref. 2 , spectral and wavelet analysis of AP% of ZB13-C2 show clear ~33 m, 12 m, and 7 m cycles, suggesting possible eccentricity (E), tilt (T), and precession (P) powers according to the average sedimentation rate. AP% (with reference to Rb/Sr and Carb%) were then tuned to the ETP, that is generated by normalizing and averaging variations in orbital eccentricity, tilt, and reversed precession 16 , and to the LR04 benthic oxygen isotope stack record 17 (Supplementary Fig. 3b-c ). Comparison of the orbital tuning and land-sea correlation age models shows close similarities (Supplementary Fig. 4 ). The final age was established based on the ETP correlation control points, as ETP persistently exhibits all the consistently strong cyclicities of ~20-ka, ~40-ka and ~100-ka. Land-sea correlation age was only referenced in examining the subtle relationships between the MCV amplitude and glacial-interglacial cycles in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 9 a, b. The basal ages for both models are ca. 3.5 Ma. The sedimentation rate remains generally stable (averagely ~3.2 ka/m), with no significant changes. The strong agreement between the filtered orbital signals (at the band of 350−15 ka) of AP% and ETP confirms the robustness of the alignments (Supplementary Fig. 4a ). As a sensitivity test, wavelet analysis was also performed for AP% timeseries using an untuned age model (initial age) based only on 14 C, OSL and magnetic reversal stratigraphy (Supplementary Fig. 4c ). The results reveal distinct ~100-ka, ~40-ka and ~20-ka cyclicities. Comparisons between this initial age model and the tuned ages aligned to ETP and the LR04 stack show only subtle differences (Supplementary Fig. 4b ). The exceptional quality of the material—continuous lacustrine sediments with a generally stable sedimentation rate—has enabled robust age tuning. These tests confirm that the orbital-scale signals in the proxies are not artifacts of the tuning process, even though tuning enhances chronological accuracy. The AP% and Carb% values for the top 500 m of core ZB19-C1 were aligned with those of core ZB13-C2, based on the strong correlation of Rb/Sr ratios between the two cores (Supplementary Fig. 3a ) and the alignment of AP% and Carb% values in the overlapping section (Supplementary Data 1 ). Note that the bottom age of ZB13-C2 was adjusted from 1.74 to 1.72 Ma due to the newly obtained Olduvai reversal at the depths of 610.81–672.35 m in ZB19-C1. Combination of the ZB13-C2 and ZB19-C1 data provides a unique multiproxy record (with resolution of ~6-yr for XRF scanning Rb/Sr, average ~500-yr for carbonate estimation, and average ~700-yr for pollen data) of supra-orbital, orbital and millennial scale climate variability for the past 3.5 Ma (Fig. 1 ), except for two 10 m-thick fluvial layers in the top section of the core and the sampling hiatus at 2964–2934 ka. The analysis resolution remains relatively consistent throughout the core due to the generally stable sedimentation rate, although up to ~100–200-year variabilities exist in the Carb% and AP% data between sections. The ultra-high-resolved Rb/Sr ratio was used as the primary proxy for exploring millennial climate variability, with Carb% and AP% providing supplementary proxy support. Proxy interpretation The vegetation in the eastern Tibetan Plateau is strongly influenced by the Asian summer monsoon. A stronger monsoon leads to warmer and moister climate, and causes the expansion of tree populations. However, in the alpine Zoige region, the density and elevational limits of forests are primarily controlled by temperature, as moisture availability is relatively plentiful 2 . This is confirmed by the distinct elevational distribution of modern vegetation 65 , the good match of AP% and axis 1 of a principal components analysis (PCA) of the pollen data 15 , and the close relationship between summer temperature and PCA axis 1 in the significance test of the quantitative temperature reconstructions for core ZB13-C2 (ref. 2 ). Thus, on long timescales, variations of AP% mainly reflects summer temperature although drought stress on tree populations during weak monsoon or glacial intervals cannot be entirely excluded. Previous studies from core RH 64 nearby ZB19-C1 indicate that Carb% mainly represents authigenic chemical precipitation, as both detrital carbonate content and shell carbonate are in trace amounts. The measured Carb% in the core should mainly reflect processes of chemical precipitation in the lake, which are largely related to summer temperature and precipitation. High temperature can increase carbonate precipitation through changing the precipitation-dissolution equilibrium and photosynthesis process. Precipitation can also elevate carbonate content by delivering more Ca 2+ and HCO 3 - into the lake through chemical weathering 2 . Additionally, in-lake biological activities, such as shell formation by mollusks and calcification by certain algae, can contribute to the accumulation of biogenic carbonate. In the meantime, during low-lake-level periods, the input and deposition of detrital minerals (e.g., silicates) would dilute carbonate, thereby influencing Carb% values 70 . Lacustrine carbonate precipitation thus can either be driven by evaporation in arid environments or occur under warm and humid conditions 71 . Consequently, there is no single interpretation for carbonate content in the lake sediments. However, multiple proxy indicators and the specific geological context of the coring site support that the high Carb% measured in the Zoige core primarily reflect wetter climatic conditions in the region, rather than the opposite. Our initial hypothesis linking proxy Carb% to lake level arose from Zoige core observations. Mollusc shells, which indicate shallow-water conditions, are found intermittently in the upper sediments but are completely absent below a depth of 490.52 m, where carbonate content is higher. This interpretation is supported by a previous study in this region, which documents a positive correlation between carbonate content and the hydrogen index, which is a proxy for lake water depth 64 . Furthermore, the measurements of Carb% in surface mud samples, collected along water-depth transects from four lakes near the ZB19-C1 core site, show that Carb% generally increases with water depth in each lake 2 . This carbonate vs. lake-level relationship has also been observed in other regions, including arid environments 70 , 72 . Collectively, these lines of evidence indicate that high carbonate values correspond to high lake levels. Lake level in this high-altitude setting is controlled by the balance between precipitation and temperature-dependent evaporation but closely tracks precipitation 73 . Specifically, in the Zoige region, evaporation is relatively low due to cool temperatures and sufficient precipitation (mean annual temperature ~1.4 °C, summer ~10 °C; annual precipitation ~650-700 mm, falling mostly in summer). Therefore, high Carb% measured in Zoige core are primarily related to high summer precipitation. The interpretation of Carb% is further supported by their coherent changes with pollen data, with high AP% and pollen concentration corresponding with high Carb%. In this high-latitude region, high (low) tree pollen abundance (AP%) reflects warm (cold) climate, while high (low) pollen concentration is associated with dense (sparse) vegetation cover under moist (dry) climate 2 , 65 . Moreover, the relatively high abundance of pollen of dryland plants Artemisia and Chenopodiaceae occurred in the low-carbonate periods (Supplementary Fig. 5d ). It is also noteworthy that the overall percentages of these dryland pollens are low, even during the glacials 15 , suggesting that conditions did not reach extremes of aridity. Rb/Sr ratio is widely used as an indicator of watershed chemical weathering or strength of summer monsoon and associated run-off 18 . Rubidium (Rb) is incorporated mainly in potassium (K)-bearing silicates and is an immobile element. When weathering/runoff intensifies, increased Sr is transported into the lake, resulting in a lower Rb/Sr ratio. Sr contained in carbonate can influence Rb/Sr ratios from bulk scanning data, potentially rendering climate interpretations less reliable. However, the Rb/Sr ratios measured by WD-XRF on decarbonated bulk samples of the Zoige core show coherent changes with the high-resolution XRF scanning data (Supplementary Fig. 5a ) 2 . The XRD mineral analysis on 80 samples further reveals that the carbonate in the core is predominantly calcite, with negligible contributions from dolomite and aragonite (Supplementary Fig. 5c ). Since dolomite (Mg-rich) readily incorporates Sr, whereas calcite—especially the Mg-poor calcite typical of freshwater environments—incorporates far less 74 , the impact of carbonate on the Zoige Rb/Sr values should be limited. This interpretation is consistent with the sedimentological and biological evidence indicating that Zoige paleolake was a large freshwater lake, as shown by its clay sediments and the persistent presence of aquatic pollen and freshwater ostracods 15 . Consequently, the scanning-XRF-derived Rb/Sr data in the Zoige core are not strongly distorted by carbonate overprint. While minor influences cannot be entirely ruled out, they do not affect the conclusions of this study. Furthermore, the WD-XRF measured Rb/Sr ratios show positive relationship with the chemical index of alteration (CIA) (Supplementary Fig. 5b ), supporting the weathering interpretation. The coherent changes of Rb/Sr with Carb% and pollen concentration (Supplementary Fig. 5d ) further suggest the impact of summer precipitation. Taken together, the variabilities of Rb/Sr can serve as a robust indicator of summer monsoon precipitation and silicate weathering. Dataset requirements for MCV investigation To retrieve millennial-scale climate variabilities (MCV), the ideal archives must provide continuous deposition with minimal hiatuses and changes in sediment types, and be sensitive to climatic parameters. The chronological control requires robust, independent age and/or orbitally tuned age models. Critically, the decadal-to-centennial-resolved continuous records are essential for capturing the a full-spectrum picture of abrupt millennial-scale climatic events and their evolution through time. For example, a resolution coarser than ~200-yr resolution may be insufficient for identifying classic Dansgaard–Oeschger (DO)-style variability, as Herbert 75 and Weedon 76 suggested that a minimum of eight samples per oscillation is required to correctly characterize the waveform morphology. Continuous records that meet this resolution criteria are largely confined to the last ~1.5 Ma (e.g., refs. 1 , 3 , 44 ), although some coarser-resolved marine and loess records covering the Pliocene-Pleistocene have provided some framework insights into MCV dynamics (e.g., refs. 20 , 27 ). The scarcity of consistent sub-~200-year-resolution datasets since the Pliocene greatly hinders the development of a long-term perspective on MCV evolution and its underlying mechanisms. The recently published North Atlantic record from IODP Site U1385 (refs. 1 , 31 ) represents an important contribution toward filling a critical high-resolution data gap in global MCV research. It provides an important comparative record to assess similarities and differences in the evolution of MCV between the North Atlantic and monsoonal Tibetan Plateau. The Zoige record from the monsoonal Tibetan Plateau is among the very few continuous terrestrial climate archives of similar resolution and timespan on a global scale. The consistent lacustrine sediments, robust age control provide an important prerequisite for reconstructing millennial-scale climate changes. A further strength of this dataset lies in its high-resolved multiple proxies, including elemental Rb/Sr ratio, Carb% and pollen data. Despite being obtained through distinct analytical techniques of XRF scanning, loss-on-ignition and pollen counting, they display strong coherence (Fig. 1 ; Supplementary Fig. 3 ), lending support to the authenticity of the observed climate signals. Particularly, pollen data can straightforwardly reflect vegetation changes in response to climatic shifts. Therefore, these cross-validation among multi-proxies from Zoige enables clear proxy indication, particularly for the primary proxy—the ultra-high-resolved Rb/Sr (averagely ~6 years; data at the interval of 2 cm, roughly 60-year, were shown in Supplementary Data 1 ) used for MCV evolution investigation. Rb/Sr thus provides an unprecedentedly complete picture of MCV evolution for the last ~3.5 Ma, offering insights into MCV dynamics from a long-term perspective. Data analyses Recurrence analysis We conducted Recurrence analysis (RA) in Matlab’s CRP Toolbox 77 in the time domain to identify change points between different climate regime states. Analysis was performed on the un-detrended proxy records. To better quantify the recurrence plot, “Determinism” (DET) 78 was introduced to assess the fraction of recurrence points that form diagonal lines with respect to all recurrence points. This parameter quantifies the predictability of dynamics in a system’s state. Predictability estimates the stochastic (unpredictable) versus the deterministic (predictable) nature. DET values near zero correspond to unpredictable dynamics, whereas large values indicate predictable dynamics. Changes in DET of the climate proxies can thus reveal transitions between fundamentally different climate regimes in the Zoige Basin (Supplementary Fig. 6 ). Time-series analyses Prior to analysis, Rb/Sr, Carb% and AP% were resampled at equal spaces of 0.2-ka, 0.5-ka and 1-ka intervals, respectively. Ensemble empirical mode decomposition (EEMD) 19 was firstly performed for all the proxies to decompose the signals of long-term, orbital and sub-orbital scales. Singular Spectral Analyses (SSA) using the kSpectra software 79 yield similar results. Consequently, only EEMD results are used for discussion (Fig. 1d ; Supplementary Data 2 ). A Taner-Hilbert band-pass filter (at band of 13−1 ka) was also applied by using the Acycle v2.8 software 80 in order to retrieve millennial-scale components and their amplitude modulations. The retrieved millennial signals are almost identical to the EEMD analysis results. The amplitude data derived from Taner-Hilbert filtering was then normalized to 0−1. The median value of 0.2 was then applied as threshold for identifying high and low MCV amplitude. Evolutive power spectral analysis and multitaper spectral analysis (MTM) were applied to examining the orbital (Supplementary Fig. 7 ) and millennial-scale cyclicities (Supplementary Fig. 15 ) using robust red-noise models to ascertain the mean, 90%, 95% and 99% confidence levels of the spectra 81 . All analyses were performed using tuning ages derived from correlations with the ETP and LR04 stack. The similar spectral results from both methods indicate they are independent of the age models. Continuous wavelet transform was performed using the MATLAB code developed by Mélice and Servain 82 , 83 to track the time-varying amplitude of millennial signals (MCV). Morlet wavelets were used in the continuous wavelet transformation. The statistical significance of the wavelet power was assessed relative to a red-noise background power spectrum. To examine the orbital and glacial modulation of the amplitude of MCV, cross-wavelet analyses of the MCV amplitude of Rb/Sr versus the ETP, LR04 stack (Supplementary Fig. 10 ) and tropical insolation (Fig. 7 ) were conducted within a Monte Carlo framework using published code 84 . Tropical insolation was represented by seasonal insolation contrast and bi-hemisphere maximum insolation 85 . The tropical seasonal insolation contrast refers to the largest amplitude in the seasonal cycle, defined by the amplitude of the 24-h mean irradiation at the equator between spring equinox and summer solstice, spring equinox and winter solstice, autumn equinox and summer solstice, and autumn equinox and winter solstice. The bi-hemisphere insolation is the maximum of summer insolation at 30°N (June) and 30°S (December). The Acycle v2.8 software can be obtained at: https://acycle.org/ . The code for continuous wavelet transform and cross-wavelet spectra is available for download at https://grinsted.github.io/wavelet-coherence/ . Sensitivity test of MCV cyclicities and amplitude To avoid circular problem, only the orbital signals of AP% (rather than any MCV signals) were tuned to ETP (rather than insolation dominated by precession cycles). As ETP is generated by normalizing and averaging variations in orbital eccentricity, tilt, and reversed precession, all the 20-ka, 40-ka and 100-ka cyclicities in it are strong. Moreover, while tuning may potentially affect expression of cyclicities, it cannot alter the amplitude of MCV relative to astronomical configuration. In particular, the persistent linkage of Rb/Sr MCV amplitude with precession is largely independent of the tuning approach. To test the sensitivity of MCV cyclicities, we have performed wavelet and spectral analysis on the Rb/Sr timeseries based on both depth and untuned age model (initial age) solely using 14 C, OSL and magnetic reversal stratigraphy. In the depth domain, Rb/Sr shows the presence of ~4.5-3-m, ~2.2−1.7-m and ~1-0.8-m cycles (Supplementary Fig. 8a ). Given an average sedimentation rate of ~3.2 ka/m, these roughly correspond periodicities of ~12-ka, ~5-ka. ~3-2.5-ka respectively. Analysis of Rb/Sr data based on initial age further exhibit approximately ~1/2-, ~1/4- and ~1/8-precession periodicities (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c ). Additionally, the consistent sedimentation rate validates that millennial-scale frequency variations throughout the past 3.5 Ma represent actual climate feature rather than depositional artifacts. These lines of evidence suggest that the tropical-induced cyclic signals are not artifacts produced by the age-tuning approach, and the timing of MCV amplitude relative to astronomical configurations is robust. It is noteworthy that though the amplitude of the MCV during ~3.5-2.7 Ma is considerably lower than in other intervals, the variations remain evident (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3c ). EEMD analysis quantitatively demonstrates that the MCV accounts for ~7% of the total variance of Rb/Sr (Supplementary Data 2 ). The relationship between MCV amplitude and precession along with glacial condition are still visible and robust (Fig. 3a ). Bispectral and bicoherence analysis Bispectral analysis, a high-order spectral analysis technique based on the Fourier transform method, was used to identify and quantify the strength of nonlinear coupling and interaction and energy transfer between different climate cycles in the Zoige Basin Rb/Sr record. The bispectrum was defined by Eq. (1): B f 1 , f 2 = E [ C f 1 C f 2 C f 1 + f 2 * ] , where E […] is the ensemble average of the triple product of complex Fourier coefficients C at the difference frequencies f1 , f2 , and their sum f3 ( f3 = f1 + f2 ), and the * indicates complex conjugation 86 . To obtain conservative net energy transfers per frequency and hence to extract the total gain or loss in energy per climate cycle over a specific time interval, we integrated over the imaginary part of the bispectrum and multiplied it by a coupling coefficient W (f1 , f2 ) = ( f1 + f2 ) 87 . We express an integration over the positive quadrant of the bispectrum alone, by integrating diagonally over the entire imaginary part of the bispectrum, and over specific zones for further examination 88 , 89 . To obtain more insight into the energy transfers among eccentricity, obliquity, precession and half-procession cycles, we integrated the bispectrum over separate zones (Supplementary Fig. 12a ), the central frequencies of the climate cycle zones were defined as 10.0, 25.0, 50.0, and 100.0 Ma − ¹, with a bandwidth corresponding to 20% of each frequency. We recombined (i.e. summed) the individual zones, to better highlight the roles of all nonlinear interactions involving eccentricity, obliquity, precession or half-procession cycles. Based on the conservative nature of the energy transfer calculation (Supplementary Fig. 12 ), we further approximately estimated the energy weighting of precession transferring to half-precession only for the last ~0.6 Ma. All the MATLAB codes are from ref. 87 . Bicoherence analysis was also used to evaluate potential nonlinear interactions between observed frequencies 90 . We employed the bicoherence approach following Sullivan et al. 90 , which uses Welch’s Overlapped Segment Averaging (WOSA) 91 that includes improvements to the standard approach that reduce the potential for spurious bicoherence peaks. Bicoherence is reported at a lower-frequency resolution than the power spectral results as its estimates require a substantial increase in bandwidth resolution (“smoothing”) (Supplementary Fig. 15 ). We implemented this method using the R-package Astrochron 92 . WOSA power spectra were also included for reference to the bicoherence estimates. Climate model simulations The simulated summer precipitation in the Zoige Basin region (Fig. 8 ) is extracted from the Orb simulation performed in refs. 49 , 93 . It is a transient simulation covering the last 800 ka performed with LOVECLIM1.3 (ref. 94 ), an Earth system Model of Intermediate Complexity (EMIC). In our study, the atmosphere (ECBilt), the ocean and sea ice (CLIO) and the terrestrial biosphere (VECODE) are interactively coupled. ECBilt is a spectral atmospheric model with truncation T21, which corresponds approximately to a horizontal resolution of 5.625° × 5.625°. It has three vertical layers, and its dynamics are governed by the quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity. VECODE is a two-dimensional dynamical terrestrial vegetation model including two plant functional types: tree and grass, and its resolution is the same as ECBilt. CLIO is a global free-surface ocean general circulation model coupled to a thermodynamic-dynamic sea ice model. The horizontal resolution of CLIO is 3° in longitude and latitude, and there are 20 unevenly spaced levels along vertical in the ocean. In the Orb simulation, only astronomical parameters 85 vary with time, with the greenhouse gases (GHG) and ice sheets being fixed to their pre-industry conditions, in order to investigate how the tropical insolation alone could introduce MCV signals to the Zoige. Given that MCV of the Tibetan Plateau over the past 800 ka is more strongly influenced by direct high-latitude ice dynamics than in earlier periods, comparing the Orb simulation results with Zoige records for this elected interval would provide robust evidence for the fundamental role of tropical insolation forcing. As an EMIC model, the reduced complexity of LOVECLIM1.3 allows that the 800,000-year-long transient simulation could be performed without using the orbital acceleration technique. Acceleration approach has been widely used in long (several glacial cycles) transient simulations performed with General Circulation Models (GCMs), but it could strongly affect the simulations of abrupt and high-frequency climate changes 95 . Non-acceleration approach is the advantage of LOCECLIM1.3. However, its intermediate complexity makes it difficult to directly perform one single transient simulation running from 800 ka BP to the present. Therefore, we split the simulation into nine segments and run them in parallel, with an overlap of 5000-year between each segment. The initial condition of each segment is provided by a 2000-year equilibrium experiment with the pre-industrial GHG and ice sheets, and astronomical parameters at the starting date of the simulated period. We finally obtain the continuous transient simulation covering the entire past 800 ka by concatenating the data from each segment and by applying a time-sliding linear interpolation for the overlap periods. More details about the model and the experiment setup can be found in refs. 49 , 93 . The summer (JJA) precipitation is extracted from the model grid cell corresponding to the ZB19-C1 core site. Due to the relatively rough spatial resolution of LOVECLIM1.3, the orographical detail on the Tibetan Plateau (TP) may not be very well represented, although an altitude in the model (~3655 m) for the study region is close to observation (~3422 m). This weakness could potentially affect local precipitation simulation, and it is actually also a challenge for most state-of-the-art CMIP GCMs 96 . However, the robustness of LOVECLIM in reproducing large-scale circulations and teleconnections has been demonstrated in previous studies 97 , 98 and has been confirmed by different GCMs, for example by ARPEGE 99 , 100 and by HadCM3 101 . In particular, it has been demonstrated that LOVECLIM was able to reproduce the major characteristics of the eastern Asian 97 , 98 and southern Asian 102 monsoons’ responses to changes in insolation and ice sheets. Moreover, LOVECLIM1.3 has been identified as one of robust models in simulating multi-year ENSO variability 103 . On balance, given the current state of model development, the non-accelerated transient simulation using LOVECLIM1.3 represents a reasonable and realistic compromise for conducting long-term transient simulations. The code of LOVECLIM1.3 is available at https://www.elic.ucl.ac.be/index.php?id=289 .
