Abstract Various proxy data agree on a shallow Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) during the last glacial maximum (LGM), extending down to 2000 m − 2500 m depth, which is similar to the AMOC geometry projected by climate models over the 21 st century with rising atmospheric CO 2 . The AMOC strength in the two climate states is insufficiently constrained. Here the Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW) and North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) properties from proxy data and climate models are used to investigate their influences on the AMOC. It is primarily the AABW’s potential density that determines the AMOC depth in both the LGM and warming climate. Changes in the potential density of the AABW, and the density contrast between NADW and AABW affect the pathways via which NADW returns to the surface and provide a constraint on AMOC strength. This study emphasizes the importance of the Southern Ocean in the AMOC’s depth and strength across climate states. Introduction The AMOC is part of the global Meridional Overturning Circulation (MOC), which is a major component of the climate system. In a zonally averaged view, the MOC can be divided according to the region of deep-water formation into the North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) cell characterised by northward flow in the upper ocean and southward flow in the deep ocean 1 , and the Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW) cell with northward flowing water in the abyssal ocean below the NADW cell. Changes in the MOC have a major influence on global and regional climate variability over a wide range of time scales by storing and transporting heat, freshwater, carbon and nutrients 2 – 6 . The Last Glacial Maximum (LGM, ~21,000 years before present) is one of the best documented periods by paleoclimate archives and helpful for understanding past climate change and carbon cycle 7 . During the LGM, the AMOC was shallower relative to the present-day AMOC by about 1000–1500 m, which is a robust feature inferred from various types of tracers such as δ 13 C, Cd/Ca, PO 4 and εNd 8 – 13 . The AMOC strength derived from proxy data, however, exhibits a large uncertainty, inhibiting a complete picture of the AMOC during the LGM. It remains highly controversial as to whether the glacial AMOC was stronger 14 , comparable 15 , 16 or weaker 17 – 22 relative to the present-day AMOC. Paleoclimate modelling during the last several years attempted to reproduce the AMOC during the LGM constrained by proxy data 23 – 26 . Climate models generally agree on a stronger glacial AMOC relative to the present-day AMOC, yet disagree widely on the depth of the overturning cell 26 (Supplementary Fig. 1 ) due to misrepresentations of various processes associated with surface-buoyancy forcing and mixing in the ocean interior 27 – 29 . These deficiencies lower the confidence in the climate models and contribute to the uncertainty regarding the AMOC projections over the 21 st century with rising atmospheric CO 2 30 . In this study, we evaluate the simulated properties of the NADW and AABW in climate model simulations against glacial reconstructions through direct model-data comparison. We also explore the influences of an improved AABW in the models on the AMOC geometry through a set of dedicated numerical experiments. As changes in the density of deep waters potentially affect the pathways via which deep water returns to the surface, this set of experiments permits to assess the dependence of AMOC strength on various overturning pathways across climate states, eventually allowing to constrain AMOC strength with observation-based estimates of the pathways in both the LGM and warming climate. Results Changes in the deep ocean revealed by proxies Changes in the properties of the NADW and AABW influence the AMOC depth and strength 31 – 34 and therefore became a central topic of paleoclimate modelling 35 . The potential density of seawater, which is a function of temperature, salinity and pressure, is a direct indicator for the properties of the NADW and AABW and hence could provide information about their influences on the AMOC 36 . We first discuss the mean temperature and salinity, and the climate-model biases relative to observations at Feni Drift, Bermuda Rise and Shona Rise (Fig. 1a ) in pre-industrial simulations with the Kiel Climate Model (KCM, “Methods”) and climate models participating in the Paleoclimate Modelling Intercomparison Project (PMIP). Temperature and salinity at these locations reveal the approximate properties of the NADW and AABW 36 . Two pre-industrial simulations were conducted with the KCM: one (PI) is performed with the standard version and the other (PI res ) with salinity in the deep Southern Ocean (south of 60°S and below 3250 m) relaxed to the Levitus’ salinity climatology (“Methods”, Supplementary Fig. 2 ). All simulations (KCM and PMIP) presented in this study are integrated for at least a few hundred years to reduce the effects of spin-up. There are noticeable warm biases in the pre-industrial control integrations of a number of climate models amounting up to about 3 °C in the temperatures at Feni Drift, Bermuda Rise and Shona Rise relative to the EN4.2.2 and WOA18 datasets (Fig. 1b–d ). Yet the climatological mean salinity at the three sites is generally in good agreement with the observations (Fig. 1b–d ). Overall, most PMIP models and the KCM reproduce reasonably well the potential density σ 2 (contours in Fig. 1b–d ), which is referenced to 2000 m depth, in the deep North Atlantic and Southern Ocean in the pre-industrial control integrations. Following the PMIP protocol, the LGM simulations with the KCM and the PMIP models are performed by implementing glacial boundary conditions including among others continental ice sheets, greenhouse gas concentrations and orbital parameters (Methods). The potential density σ 2 in the LGM simulations is significantly underestimated compared to the proxy data at the three sites (Fig. 1e–g ). At Feni Drift and Bermuda Rise, the bias in σ 2 can be mainly attributed to a warm temperature and to a lesser extent fresh salinity bias. The biased potential density at Shona Rise is largely related to fresh salinity biases in the deep Southern Ocean. Fresh biases there exceed 1 practical salinity unit (psu) in the LGM simulations performed with the standard KCM (Methods) and the PMIP models. We note that the salinity bias at Shona Rise in FGOLAS-g2 and MIROC-ESM exceeds 2 psu. This is likely because these models do not fully implement the PMIP protocol and do not add 1 psu to the oceanic initial conditions of the LGM simulations. The two CESM2 models show relatively dense waters at Shona Rise, which may be due to the parameterisation of density-driven overflow that moves dense water from the shelves to the deep ocean without cascading 37 , 38 . Southern Ocean controls the geometry of the AMOC The σ 2 of the NADW and AABW at their source regions is suggested to be a major factor controlling the AMOC in the LGM and modern climate 26 , 28 , 39 , 40 . We examine the relationship of the AMOC in the region 20°S-10°N to the σ 2 of the NADW and AABW. Here the AMOC depth is defined as the depth at which the overturning streamfunction is zero. The density of the NADW and AABW is represented by the vertical and area-weighted mean from 800 m to 2800 m over 50°N-55°N and 50°W-10°W in the North Atlantic, and below 3000 m over 50°S-55°S and 50°W-10°E in the South Atlantic, respectively (yellow rectangles in Fig. 1a ). The AMOC depth exhibits a statistically significant correlation of 0.70 at the 95% confidence level with the σ 2 contrast between NADW and AABW in the LGM simulations (Fig. 2a ). The significance of correlation coefficient is tested using the p value approach. It is primarily the σ 2 of the AABW that exhibits a strong relationship with the AMOC depth, with a correlation of −0.71 (Fig. 2b ). The NADW hardly influences the AMOC depth, as illustrated by its small and insignificant correlation with σ 2 (now shown). Clearly, the shoaling of the AMOC in the LGM relative to the pre-industrial simulations is linked to the enhanced stratification due to the rather dense AABW in the deep ocean 36 . Similar to the LGM, the density contrast between the NADW and AABW exhibits a statistically significant correlation with the AMOC depth under global warming, which, however, is smaller amounting to 0.46 (Fig. 2c ). As in many other climate models, the AABW in the KCM is formed by open-ocean convection and not over the continental shelf. Previous studies suggest that reducing warm temperature biases in the deep Southern Ocean improves the simulated ocean stratification and the AMOC 43 , 44 . We correct the σ 2 bias in the deep Southern Ocean by relaxing the KCM’s salinity below 3250 m south of 60°S towards the palaeoceanographic salinity reconstructions (Methods). This approach has been proven useful to reproduce the vertical stratification of the glacial ocean 45 . The initial conditions for the sensitivity experiments are the temperature and salinity climatology calculated from the LGM simulation with the standard KCM, to which either 1.0 psu, 1.5 psu or 2.0 psu is added (Methods). Each salinity-restoring experiment is integrated for 1600 years, with the monthly output from the last 300 years analysed here (Fig. 3a, b ). The σ 2 biases at Shona Rise are relatively small in the salinity-restoring experiment, and the AMOC shoals considerably (Fig. 3b ). In particular, the temperature and salinity in LGM res20 lie within the uncertainty of the reconstructions (Fig. 1c ). The AMOC shoals by about 800 m relative to PI res to a depth of 2050 m in LGM res20 (Fig. 3c, d ), which agrees well with various proxy data 8 – 14 . The AMOC strength reduces from 16.8 Sv in the standard LGM simulation to 13.8 Sv in the LGM res20 experiment, which is still slightly larger with respect to the pre-industrial state 14 (12.7 Sv). Moreover, the dominant role of the AABW on the AMOC depth is also found in the salinity-restoring experiments (Fig. 2b ), whereas the density contrast between the NADW and AABW appears to be slightly less important (Fig. 2a ). Overall, the restoring experiments support the argument that strong ocean stratification drives the shoaling of the AMOC during the LGM by limiting the deep convection in the North Atlantic 14 , 35 . Improved North Atlantic surface climate due to reduced salinity bias in the Southern Ocean The KCM suffers from systematic model biases in the North Atlantic as other PMIP models (Supplementary Fig. 4 ). To alleviate the impact of systematic biases, we compute the simulated SST responses in the LGM simulations relative to the pre-industrial experiment. The KCM’s response of sea surface temperature (SST) over the North Atlantic benefits from the reduced σ 2 bias in the Southern Ocean. The late-winter mean (January-March, JFM) SST changes during the LGM from MARGO 46 , which is a proxy-based reconstruction for the glacial ocean conditions, depict a cooling pattern relative to the modern climate over the eastern North Atlantic, which extends from 60°N to 40°N (Fig. 4a ). In comparison to MARGO, the standard KCM simulates a weaker response of cooling in the eastern North Atlantic that is restricted to north of 50°N from 35°W to 5°W (Fig. 4b ). Correcting the salinity bias in the deep Southern Ocean in the experiment LGM res20 , not only improves the AMOC but also broadens the meridional extent of the cooling in the eastern North Atlantic (Fig. 4c ). Relative to MARGO, the root-mean-square-error averaged over 40°N-60°N and 35°W-5°W reduces from 5.32 °C to 3.87 °C in the restoring experiment. Consistently, the restoring experiment exhibits improvements in the simulated JFM mean SST compared to MARGO (Supplementary Fig. 5 ). We note that there are still some significant discrepancies in the SST-cooling response between MARGO and the salinity-restoring experiment, which may partly arise from the mean-state biases over the North Atlantic associated with deficiencies in the dynamical processes in the pre-industrial simulation with the KCM 47 . There is an eastward expansion of the sea ice associated with the cooling over the North Atlantic in LGM res20 (Fig. 4d–f ), which reduces the local surface-heat flux and wind-driven oceanic salt transport 29 . As a result, the deep oceanic convection, as measured by the JFM mean mixed layer depth, is weakened south of Iceland in LGM res20 relative to the standard LGM simulation (Fig. 4g–i ). We note that LGM res10 and LGM res15 show similar but smaller changes as LGM res20 . Dependence of changes in the glacial AMOC on the overturning pathways One remaining issue is the strength of the glacial AMOC. As proxy data disagree on the changes in the AMOC strength during the LGM, we seek to constrain the AMOC changes using present-day observations, which is similar to the concept of emergent constraints 48 . This approach assumes that despite major differences among the climate models, relationships between the present-day and glacial climate are implicit within the model solutions of the partial differential equations governing the physical processes and parameterisations. Thus, the relationship between the present-day constraint variable ( x ) and glacial change in AMOC ( y ) can be expressed as y = f x + ε where ε represents a negligible deviation from function f . The function f can be obtained through analysis of a diverse model ensemble with varying resolution and parameterisations, such as the PMIP/CMIP ensembles. This allows us to establish a relationship between the glacial AMOC changes and the pre-industrial overturning pathways, where the pathways are quantified by the overturning streamfunction, which stands for the zonally integrated volume transport that returns the NADW to the surface (Methods). In latitude-depth space, the AMOC can be regarded as a superposition of the shallower adiabatic cell and the deeper diabatic cell separated in the Atlantic and the Indo-Pacific basin. An acceleration (deceleration) of either cell strengthens (weakens) the AMOC. As suggested by Cessi (2019) 49 , about half of the volume of the modern NADW returns adiabatically to the surface through the wind-driven upwelling pathway in the Southern Ocean 50 (SO_Wind total ). The remaining half of the NADW completes its circulation diabetically via diffusive upwelling in the Atlantic (At diffu ) and Indo-Pacific 51 (IP diffu ). There is compelling evidence from modern hydrography that a geostrophic inter-basin transport connects the Atlantic with the Indo-Pacific, resulting from differences in the isopycnal depth on the order of 100 m between the two basins 52 . Specifically, analysis of water mass properties shows that the NADW rising to the surface of the Southern Ocean is transformed into AABW and then transported to the bottom of the Indo-Pacific. These dense waters are upwelled through diapycnal mixing and eventually return to the Atlantic 1 . Each overturning pathway exhibits a large spread among the PMIP and CMIP models. Motivated by the influence of deep waters on the AMOC, we investigate relationships of the overturning pathways with the potential density of the NADW and AABW (Fig. 5 ). The Southern Ocean wind-driven pathway is positively correlated to the potential density contrast between the NADW and AABW in the LGM simulations (Fig. 5a ). The correlation coefficient amounts to 0.62, which is significant at the 95% confidence level. Consistent with the LGM simulations, the potential density contrast between the NADW and AABW is associated with the magnitude of the Southern Ocean wind-driven pathway under global warming in CMIP simulations, with a correlation of 0.42 that is significant at the 95% confidence level (Fig. 5c ). The Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway exhibits no significant dependence on the potential density of the NADW and AABW in the LGM simulations with the PMIP models (blue lines Fig. 5b ). Salinity-restoring experiments with the KCM suggest that enhanced potential density in the Atlantic basin tends to weaken the Indo-Pacific pathway (green lines in Fig. 5b ). In contrast, global warming simulations with the CMIP6 models exhibits a positive correlation of the Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway with the potential density of the AABW, amounting to 0.53 which is significant at the 99% confidence level (Fig. 5d ). Differences in the influence of the AABW on the Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway across the glacial and warming climate states are likely associated with the boundary conditions, particularly in the Northern Hemisphere. In summary, the two overturning pathways exhibit a significant relationship to the potential density of the AABW and NADW in the models across climate states. Then each of the two pathways is examined with respect to the response of the AMOC strength, defined as the change in the LGM simulation relative to the pre-industrial simulation. Models with strong pre-industrial Southern Ocean wind-driven pathway tend to exhibit strengthened glacial AMOC driven by intensified westerly winds (Supplementary Fig 6 ), as indicated by the positive correlation ( r = 0.58), explaining about one third of the total variance among the models (PMIP and KCM, Fig. 6a ). PMIP models with a stronger pre-industrial Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway are sensitive to the reduced inter-basin transport, which is affected by the glacial ocean stratification and depict an opposite relationship with the glacial AMOC response ( r = −0.54; PMIP and KCM, Fig. 6b ). Furthermore, the Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway is highly correlated with the glacial AMOC response ( r = 0.8, PMIP and KCM; Supplementary Fig 7c ), consistent with previous studies 53 – 55 . The above results suggest that the Southern Ocean wind-driven and the Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway can be used as a constraint on the glacial AMOC response. This hypothesis is tested using 110 global warming simulations from 12 CMIP5 (Table 2 , Supplementary Fig 8 ) and 25 CMIP6 ensembles (Table 1 , Supplementary Fig 3 ) employing the SSP5-8.5 scenario. The ensemble mean is computed using all available members and the model data are averaged over the years 2071–2100. Consistent with the LGM simulations, the weakening of the AMOC under global warming is dependent on the magnitude of the Southern Ocean wind-driven and Indo-Pacific diffusive pathways in the pre-industrial simulations (Fig. 6c, d ). In particular, the Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway plays a dominant role in the AMOC response to global warming, explaining about 50% of the variance among the CMIP models (Fig. 6d ). The weakening of the AMOC under global warming is largely attributed to the restricted Indian Ocean inflow induced by the shoaling of the AMOC 56 and compensated by the Southern Ocean pathway driven by the intensified westerly winds (Supplementary Fig 6 ). We note that the CMIP6 models exhibit a stronger dependence of the AMOC response on the pre-industrial pathways than the CMIP5 models. This could be due to improvements in ocean stratification and abyssal circulation in the CMIP6 models 57 . Discussion We have investigated in climate models the factors influencing the geometry and strength of the AMOC in the LGM and modern climate, and under global warming. It is found that the potential density in the deep Southern Ocean is crucial to realistically simulate the AMOC depth. A long-lasting, controversial question is what controls the changes in the glacial AMOC strength. By examining the dependence of the AMOC changes on the pre-industrial pathways that return deep waters to the surface, we find that the Southern Ocean wind-driven and Indo-Pacific diffusive pathways exhibit statistically significant correlations with the AMOC changes, with each explaining about 30% of the variance among the models. The combination of observation-based reanalysis datasets and climate model-derived constraint relationships reveals a stronger glacial AMOC relative to the pre-industrial state, with a large uncertainty regarding the magnitude of the strengthening, while keeping in mind that the applied methodology is debatable. The density of the AABW can affect the AMOC geometry through its influence on vertical stratification. Strongly increased salinity in the glacial deep waters, overwhelming the effect of their temperatures, leads to higher density and enhanced stratification and by extension of the volume of the AABW to AMOC shoaling 35 , 36 . Chen et al. 65 argue that strong glacial ocean stratification, independent of changes in the vertical mixing induced by tidal dissipation, was primarily responsible for the shallower AMOC during the LGM. Heuzé et al. 57 report that CMIP6 models that form dense water via shelf processes and with parameterised overflows around the Antarctic are the most accurate in capturing the properties of the present-day AABW. A salty deep ocean is also found to be a prerequisite for glacial termination 66 . Our findings reinforce that improving the representations of shelf processes and density-driven overflow in climate models is crucial to understand past and future changes in the deep waters and hence the AMOC geometry. Since reconstructions and modelling studies 67 – 70 of the glacial AMOC strength are subject to large uncertainties, we constrain the changes in the AMOC strength using the present-day overturning pathways. The constraint relationship is derived from the correlations between the climate models’ AMOC responses to LGM forcing and the magnitude of the major present-day overturning pathways. The Southern Ocean wind-driven and Indo-Pacific diffusive pathways exhibit not only statistically significant correlations with the AMOC changes during the LGM but also with the AMOC responses to global warming. One of the likely links between changes in the glacial AMOC and the overturning pathways is the potential density of the AABW and NADW. The Southern Ocean wind-driven pathway can be influenced by multiple processes, such as the Southern Ocean surface buoyance flux, the maximum and latitudinal position of westerly wind stress driving upwelling and Ekman transport 71 , which may also alter the potential density of deep waters. The AABW properties influence the Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway by modulating the inter-basin transport 56 associated with the vertical and horizontal mixing and parameterised mesoscale eddies. Consequently, the constraint relationship on the glacial AMOC responses can be sensitive to the simulated deep water properties. Most models with large biases in these properties exhibit stronger glacial AMOC, while models with relatively small biases in the AABW properties tend to exhibit a slightly stronger or comparable glacial AMOC relative to its present-day magnitude. Our approach is primarily based on the hypothesis that climate models fully capture the dynamical processes underlying the response of the overturning pathways to external forcing across climate states. Additional uncertainties may arise due to missing processes in the models. Furthermore, it is generally accepted that nonlinear dynamics, such as ice-sheet retreat and glacial isostatic adjustment, play a pivotal role in the climate evolution during the deglaciation 72 , 73 . The development of nonlinear constraint approaches may provide more accurate estimates for the glacial AMOC strength. It is worth noting that the relative contributions of the two pathways to the AMOC changes vary across climate states, which result from differences in the westerly winds and Southern Ocean stratification. Forced by intensified westerly winds (Supplementary Fig 6 ), the Southern Ocean wind-driven pathway tends to strengthen the AMOC in both glacial and warm climates. The upper ocean stratification enhances due to surface warming and freshening in response to global warming, as evident from the shallower AMOC (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 6 ), leading to a substantially weaker Indo-Pacific pathway associated with reduced inter-basin transport. The weakening AMOC under global warming is thus mainly attributed to the reduced Indo-Pacific diffusive pathway and compensated by the Southern Ocean pathway. By contrast, the upper ocean stratification during the LGM is determined by the opposing changes induced by cooling and salinification, leading to a highly variable Indo-Pacific pathway associated with the inter-basin transport. The glacial AMOC strength is hence subject to large uncertainties. However, the two pathways in the models insufficiently account for the impacts of mesoscale processes in the Southern Ocean, which are suggested to play a crucial role in the inter-basin transport and the AMOC via the salt-advection feedback 74 , 75 . Relative to the CMIP5 models, the larger correlations of the AMOC changes with the Indo-Pacific pathway in the CMIP6 models is possibly due to improvements in the parameterisations of mixing processes and mesoscale eddies 38 , 76 , and/or from increased model resolution 77 , 78 . Of particular importance to understand the spread of the glacial AMOC strength across models may be the parameters affecting vertical mixing. Mikolajewicz et al. 73 managed to simulate stronger, comparable and slightly weaker glacial AMOC relative to its present-day intensity in an ensemble of eight simulations. The glacial AMOC is influenced by the prescribed model parameters, particularly the vertical background mixing in the ocean. This may account for the large spread of simulated glacial AMOC across studies. In conclusion, our findings reinforce the essential role of the Southern Ocean for the AMOC across varying climate states. Methods Observational datasets and proxy reconstruction To quantify the temperature and salinity biases of NADW and AABW in modern and glacial simulations, model results are compared to the present-day observations and to palaeoceanographic reconstructions, respectively. To obtain the mean-state salinity and temperature biases in pre-industrial simulations, the monthly version of EN4.2.2 objectively analysed temperature and salinity with bias corrections using the method of Cowley et al. 79 is used as reference. Cowley et al. analyses and compares the ocean temperature profiles measured by some 4100 side-by-side deployed expendable bathythermographs and conductivity–temperature–depths such that the measurement biases in ocean temperature can be separated from those in depth. Biases in ocean temperature and salinity relative to the climatology from Volume I and Volume II of the World Ocean Atlas 80 , 81 (WOA18) are considered as well. The temperature and salinity climatology of EN4.2.2 and WOA18 are both averaged over the period 1975–2004. The temperature and salinity biases during the LGM are mainly estimated by comparing the model data to proxy reconstructions at two sites from Ocean Drilling Program by Adkins et al. 36 . Two reanalysis datasets, ECCO v4 and GloRanV14, are used here. Forced by the ERA-Interim data over the period 1992–2015, ECCO v4 uses a general circulation model (MITgcm) and assimilates most of available satellite and in-situ data. The data include satellite sea surface height, ocean bottom pressure, sea surface temperature, sea surface salinity, in-situ temperature and salinity and sea-ice measurements from satellite and in-situ observations. The state estimation uses the adjoint method to iteratively minimise the squared sum of weighted model data misfits and control adjustments. The horizontal resolution varies from 22 km to 110 km, with the highest resolution in high latitudes and lowest resolution in midlatitudes. GloRanV14 uses the eddy-permitting (1/4° horizontal resolution) Nucleus for European Modelling of the Ocean (NEMO) model, driven with atmospheric forcing from ERA-Interim over the period 1993–2021. Satellite sea surface height, sea surface temperature, sea-ice concentrations and in situ temperature and salinity are assimilated. We choose the Multiproxy Approach for the Reconstruction of the Glacial Ocean Surface Project (MARGO) to estimate the sea surface temperature biases over the North Atlantic during the LGM 46 . The MARGO synthesis compiles and integrates 696 reconstructions of SSTs based on four microfossil (i.e. planktonic foraminifera, diatom, dinoflagellate cyst and radiolarian abundances) and two geochemical (alkenones and planktonic foraminifera Mg/Ca) proxies for the LGM. The geological age is rigorously defined as the period between 23 and 19 thousand years before present. This multiproxy approach yields the least biased representation for the glacial ocean conditions. The MARGO data are mapped on a global 5° × 5° grid. The spatial coverage of proxies is especially dense in the North Atlantic, the Southern Ocean and the tropics. The samples in the subtropical gyres, especially in the Pacific Ocean, however, remain rare. Kiel Climate Model and experimental setup The simulations are carried out with a fully coupled atmosphere-ocean-sea ice general circulation model, the Kiel Climate Model 82 (KCM). The version of the KCM used here consists of the ECHAM5 83 atmosphere general circulation model with a horizontal resolution of T42 (2.8° × 2.8°), and with 19 vertical levels reaching up to 10 hPa. The atmosphere model is coupled to the NEMO-LIM ocean–sea ice model 84 through the OASIS3 coupler 85 . The horizontal resolution of NEMO is approximately 2° (ORCA2 grid), with a meridional refinement of ~0.5° near the equator and 31 vertical levels. The KCM suffers from biases in the abyssal ocean which are common to many climate models (Supplementary Fig 9a – c ). Yet our aim is to investigate the influences of the abyssal biases on the AMOC, but not their causes. Here we investigate the impact of correcting the AABW salinity biases on the geometry and strength of the glacial AMOC. This is achieved by restoring the AABW salinity towards the palaeoceanographic salinity reconstructed by Adkins et al. 36 . The experimental strategy is as follows: we perform two sets of experiments. The first set is performed with the standard KCM, which consists of two experiments: one 5300-year-long pre-industrial control run (PI) that is initialised with the Levitus climatology of temperature and salinity. Pre-industrial greenhouse gases, modern land-sea mask and continental ice-sheet geometry are employed in PI. In the other 5750-year-long simulation (LGM), the boundary conditions including greenhouse gas concentrations and orbital parameters are implemented in accordance with the PMIP4 protocol for the LGM experiments 86 . The LGM is initialised from the Levitus temperature climatology and salinity climatology to which 1 psu has been added to account for the 116-m drop of global mean sea level during the LGM. The simulated interactions between the tropical Pacific and the North Atlantic, and the multidecadal variability of the AMOC in LGM have been addressed in previous studies 87 , 88 . For the second set of experiments, we apply a three-dimensional relaxation sponge in the Southern Ocean, south of 60°S (Supplementary Fig 2 ) following the configuration used by Patara and Böning 89 . As the KCM features strong mixing due to its coarse horizontal and vertical resolution, a relatively strong restoring has to be applied with a relaxation time set to 60 days. To avoid spurious sources of vorticity, the sponge area is restricted away from the Antarctic coast and its northern boundary. The relaxation weakens exponentially in the vicinity of rigid boundaries using the default smoothing function in NEMO. The first experiment (PI res ) is a 1100-year-long pre-industrial run starting from the Levitus climatology, yet the salinity below approximately 3250-m in the Southern Ocean (south of 60°S) is restored to monthly values from the Levitus climatology. Notably, the salinity relaxation induces a small deficit in the freshwater budget. We therefore compute the seasonal cycle of the deficit using the initial 300-year monthly data and add the freshwater flux at the surface south of 60°S (Supplementary Fig 10 ). The zonal mean salinity bias in PI res slightly reduces relative to PI (Supplementary Fig 9 ). Yet the simulated mean climate and AMOC in PI res remain quite similar to those in PI (not shown). Three additional experiments under the LGM boundary conditions are performed. The salinity in the deep Southern Ocean is restored to monthly salinity climatology from the LGM simulation, to which 1.0 psu (LGM res10 ), 1.5 psu (LGM res15 ) or 2.0 psu (LGM res10 ) is added. Each simulation is integrated for 1600 years, such that the simulated mean climate and circulation reach a quasi-equilibrium state. The last 300-year monthly output from the two sets of simulations are used in the analysis shown here. Calculation of overturning pathways As palaeoceanographic reconstructions do not agree on the strength of the glacial AMOC, we seek for alternate ways, such as wind-driven and vertical diffusive processes, to constrain changes in the AMOC strength 50 , 51 , 60 . In the present-day overturning circulation, NADW returns to the ocean surface via either upwelling in the Southern Ocean forced by the westerly winds, or upwelling in the Atlantic and the Indo-Pacific Oceans through vertical diapycnal mixing. In particular, diffusive upwelling in the Indo-Pacific Ocean may significantly impact the global overturning circulation and hence, largely determines the responses of the AMOC strength to anthropogenic global warming in CMIP6 simulations 60 . Similarly, we estimate the wind- and diffusion-driven overturning pathways to assess changes in the AMOC during the LGM. Following Baker et al. 60 , we use streamfunction, which represents the zonally integrated volume transport by Eulerian-mean and parameterised eddy velocity, as a measure for the overturning pathways. The pathways are defined by the processes and regions associated with this upwelling. In CMIP6 models they can be estimated as follows, 1 A t d i f f u = A M O C max − A M O C min 2 S O − W i n d t o t a l = ψ max ∣ ϕ = 34.5 ∘ S To conserve the total volume of NADW, the diffusive pathway in the Indo-Pacific Ocean can be computed as the residual, 3 I P d i f f u = A M O C max − A t d i f f u − S O − W i n d t o t a l , where A M O C max represents the total volume of NADW and is defined as the maximum streamfunction in the North Atlantic. A M O C min represents the NADW entering the Southern Ocean, which is quantified as the minimum streamfunction located between 34.5°S and the latitude of maximum streamfunction in the Atlantic basin. The difference between A M O C max and A M O C min thus represents the upwelled NADW via diffusive upwelling in the Atlantic. The total Southern Ocean wind-driven pathway ( S O − W i n d t o t a l ) is defined as the maximum global overturning streamfunciton located at approximately 34.5°S. The Indo-Pacific pathway is the residual arising from volume conservation.
