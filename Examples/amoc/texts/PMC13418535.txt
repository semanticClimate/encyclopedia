Abstract Coccolithophores are vital to marine ecosystems and play a central role in the global carbon cycle. Although abundant in the temperate North Atlantic and Barents Sea, their long-term bloom dynamics remain poorly understood. Using two decades of satellite observations, we reveal a substantial decline in coccolithophore blooms between 2003 and 2022, with a 36.1% reduction in bloom area and a 51.2% decrease in bloom frequency. The decline is most pronounced in the temperate North Atlantic and western Barents Sea, while the eastern Barents Sea shows notable increases. Decreasing sea surface temperatures (SSTs) in the North Atlantic and warming in the eastern Barents Sea are identified as key drivers of these trends. However, SST changes alone do not explain the asymmetric patterns across the Barents Sea, suggesting additional environmental drivers and phytoplankton competition. Our study provides the most spatially and temporally resolved assessment of coccolithophore bloom trends in this region to date, with broader implications for marine ecosystems and carbon cycling. INTRODUCTION Marine phytoplankton play a pivotal role in mitigating global climate change by absorbing and sequestering atmospheric carbon dioxide ( 1 , 2 ). However, climate change has been increasingly altering marine environments, including but not limited to reshaping phytoplankton communities and stressing their associated ecological functions ( 3 – 7 ). Coccolithophores, a group of calcifying phytoplankton, are globally distributed and produce calcium carbonate plates known as coccoliths. These coccoliths are a key component of the ocean’s particulate inorganic carbon (PIC) pool, contributing approximately half of the PIC flux to the seafloor ( 8 ). Through primary production and calcification, coccolithophores contribute to both the biological and carbonate pumps, playing a complex and poorly understood dual role in ocean carbon sequestration—enhancing organic carbon export via calcite ballasting while simultaneously increasing the partial pressure of CO 2 ( P co 2 ) at the sea surface through the calcification process ( 8 ). However, climate change is profoundly affecting the physiology and distribution of coccolithophores, driving species extinctions ( 9 ) and migrations ( 10 – 13 ). Thus, monitoring the spatial and temporal shifts of coccolithophores is crucial for understanding the ocean carbon cycle. The area extending from the temperate North Atlantic to the Barents Sea is a key region for extensive coccolithophore proliferation. Large-scale blooms of coccolithophores have been recorded since the late 20th century ( 14 , 15 ), with Gephyrocapsa huxleyi ( G. hux ) formerly known as Emiliania huxleyi being the dominant species ( 16 ). In recent years, G. hux has been observed advancing poleward into the Barents Sea, one of the fastest-warming areas in the world’s oceans ( 17 , 18 ). This poleward shift has been attributed to bioadvection ( 10 ) and the northward progression of the polar front ( 11 ) associated with ocean warming. Alongside coccolithophores, noncalcifying phytoplankton biomass has also substantially increased at higher latitudes, driven by an influx of nutrient-rich Atlantic waters—a process commonly referred to as “Atlantification” ( 19 – 22 ). The blooms of calcifying and noncalcifying phytoplankton typically occur in succession throughout the year ( 23 ), reflecting the preferences of different temperature, light, nutrient availability, and stratification conditions ( 21 , 24 , 25 ). This natural cycle prompts a critical question: Is there a relationship between the recent changes in calcifying and noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms? To address this question, the first step is to obtain detailed spatial and temporal characteristics of coccolithophore blooms. The in situ continuous plankton recorder (CPR) provides over half a century of plankton sampling data in the North Atlantic, with one study indicating that the occurrence of coccolithophores increased from 2 to 20% in this region from 1965 to 2010 ( 26 ). However, these insights are primarily based on data from the latter half of the 20th century, and since the CPR operates on fixed ship routes, its capacity to capture the full extent of phytoplankton dynamics across the North Atlantic and Barents Sea is limited. Satellite remote sensing offers extensive spatial coverage and long-term observations, complementing in situ measurements to alleviate data limitations. A commonly used method to estimate coccolithophore abundance is through remotely sensed PIC concentrations. This approach has been widely applied in global and regional studies of coccolithophore bloom dynamics ( 21 , 24 , 27 ). However, PIC algorithms can yield false positives in sediment-dominated shelf seas or areas with high concentrations of noncalcifying phytoplankton (see Materials and Methods), introducing considerable uncertainty in quantifying coccolithophore trends. Moreover, while most recent studies focus on the poleward expansion of coccolithophores in polar waters ( 10 – 13 ), less attention is given to the ongoing changes within the ecological continuum from the temperate to the polar seas (e.g., the temperate North Atlantic to the Barents Sea). In this study, we leveraged two decades of satellite imagery to address two critical questions regarding coccolithophores in this region: (i) Have coccolithophore blooms in the temperate North Atlantic and Barents Sea increased or decreased over the past 20 years? And (ii) what were the potential drivers behind these changes? We generated a 1-km resolution daily coccolithophore bloom dataset using all available NASA Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) images from 2003 to 2022. We developed a practical coccolithophore detection algorithm based on MODIS imagery, which accurately identified the highly reflective G. hux blooms and effectively minimized the false positives caused by noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms or suspended sediments. Validation using in situ data showed the advantage of our method compared to the PIC algorithm in avoiding false positives (see Materials and Methods). We quantified bloom frequency by integrating the bloom counts (that is, the number of pixels with coccolithophore blooms detected) and the bloom area (that is, the areas with coccolithophore blooms detected) within a 0.1° by 0.1° grid, using this metric to characterize bloom intensity. RESULTS Decreasing coccolithophore blooms Widespread coccolithophore blooms were identified across our study region from the temperate North Atlantic to the Barents Sea, with the total bloom area covering 8.25 million km 2 . Bloom hotspots include the southern Barents Sea, coastal Iceland, the North Sea, and the Celtic-Biscay Shelf ( Fig. 1A ). Temporally, regions with the highest coccolithophore bloom frequency progressively shift northward from June to August (fig. S1). By September, these blooms gradually dissipate from the Barents Sea. This spatial progression over seasons is linked to the changes in temperature, light, and the dynamics of noncalcifying phytoplankton, as coccolithophore blooms typically occur under warm, stratified conditions, often following the depletion of surface nutrients caused by preceding noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms ( 23 , 25 ). Between 2003 and 2022, coccolithophore blooms experienced a substantial decline across almost the entire studied region. Over that interval, the annual bloom area decreased by 1.24 million km 2 (a 36.1% reduction), while bloom frequency dropped by 51.2%; the linear trends for these reductions are estimated at 71,700 km 2 year −1 and −2.57% year −1 , respectively ( Fig. 1C ). Areas experiencing significant declines ( P < 0.05) accounted for 6% of the examined area (0.51 million km 2 ). By contrast, significant increasing trends were mainly confined to the eastern Barents Sea, the central North Sea, and the eastern Iceland basin ( Fig. 1B ), with the total area of these regions (0.05 million km 2 ) being approximately an order of magnitude smaller than the areas with significant decreases. Dividing the study area into the North Atlantic and the Barents Sea, we found that the North Atlantic has experienced significant declines ( P < 0.05) in annual bloom area (−3.41% year −1 ; Fig. 1B ) and annual bloom frequency (−3.68% year −1 ; Fig. 1D ) over the past two decades. This finding contrasts with in situ CPR data from 1965 to 2010, which suggested an increase in coccolithophore presence across the North Atlantic ( 26 ). In our study, rapid increases in coccolithophore blooms were observed in the eastern Barents Sea, aligning with previous research indicating an eastward shift in these blooms ( 10 , 11 ). Notably, our findings revealed a pronounced dipole pattern across the 32°E meridian, with bloom frequency decreasing to the west but increasing to the east ( Fig. 1B ), resulting in no significant overall trend for the Barents Sea as a whole ( Fig. 1E ). Dominant role of sea surface temperature Previous laboratory studies have shown that G. hux growth rates increase within a temperature range of ∼2° to 25°C but decline at temperatures exceeding 25°C ( 28 – 31 ). Our findings highlight the extensive influence of sea surface temperature (SST) on coccolithophore blooms across high-latitude oceans. This influence is evident not only in the spatial succession observed on a monthly scale ( Fig. 2, A to D ; as reflected by the northward shift of bloom hotspots from June to August) but also in broader spatial patterns. Our further analysis revealed that the spatial patterns of coccolithophore bloom trends largely align with SST trends for all summer months ( Fig. 2, E to L ), with strong correlations observed in all regions where bloom frequency is high ( Fig. 2, M to P ). The massive decline in bloom frequency in the North Atlantic aligns with the cooling trend in SST across all summer months over the past two decades ( Fig. 2, I to L ). This cooling, often referred to as the “warming hole,” is linked with the recent slowdown of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), which has reduced northward heat transport ( 32 , 33 ). The eastward shift of coccolithophore blooms in the eastern Barents Sea is also influenced by changes in the SST, although in the opposite direction. We not only found significant correlations between rising SST and increased coccolithophore bloom frequency within this region ( Fig. 2, M to P ) but also documented synchronous movement between the leading edge of annual coccolithophore blooms and the location of the thermal front ( r = 0.57, P < 0.05; Fig. 3 and fig. S2). This indicates that as the Barents Sea warmed, blooms expanded poleward, following the shift of the thermal front, consistent with earlier work by Neukermans et al. ( 11 ). However, this northward shifting pattern appears to have reversed after 2016 ( Fig. 3B ), coinciding with recent cooling trends and a rebound in sea ice extent ( 34 ). Fig. 3. The strongest warming has been observed in the eastern Barents Sea region ( Fig. 2, J and K ), which corresponds to a marked increase in coccolithophore blooms ( Fig. 2, F and G ). Unexpectedly, despite the lack of a clear SST trend in the western Barents Sea over the past two decades, coccolithophore blooms have been declining, although strong correlations between bloom frequency and SST were observed in this region ( Fig. 2, N and O ). This suggests that while SST changes do influence coccolithophore growth, the declining bloom trends in the western Barents Sea may be driven by additional factors beyond SST alone. Regime-shift in the Barents Sea Our further examination of interannual variations in coccolithophore blooms reveals that the dipole trend pattern observed in the Barents Sea was primarily driven by abrupt changes around 2011 ( Fig. 4 ). In the eastern Barents Sea, SST increased sharply in 2011 and then stabilized, with the 2011–2022 mean SST 1.6°C higher than 2003–2010 mean SST ( P < 0.05; Fig. 4G ). This temperature increase was strongly correlated with a significant rise in coccolithophore bloom frequency after 2011 ( r = 0.81, P < 0.05; Fig. 4F ). A similar high correlation was observed between coccolithophore bloom frequency and SST in the western Barents Sea before 2011 ( r = 0.84, P < 0.05; Fig. 4D ), but this relationship became nonsignificant afterward ( r = 0.33, P > 0.05). Despite the SST increase post-2011, coccolithophore bloom frequency demonstrated decreasing trends in both subperiods, highlighting the potential decoupling of coccolithophore bloom dynamics from SST in the western Barents Sea after 2011. The decline in coccolithophore blooms in the western Barents Sea since 2011 may be linked to shifts in noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms ( Fig. 4E ), as suggested by the clear upward trend in noncalcifying phytoplankton and the temporal succession between the two ( 21 , 24 , 25 ). Phenological analysis reveals that before 2011, coccolithophore blooms commence in late June and peak by the end of July (fig. S3A), aligning with earlier observations ( 18 ). However, since 2011, the bloom peak has shifted to mid-August (fig. S3B), likely driven by the prolonged spring bloom of noncalcifying phytoplankton. This extended bloom period is likely fueled by increased nutrient input and enhanced mixing of Atlantic waters through the Barents Sea Opening ( 27 ), narrowing both the spatial extent and seasonal window conducive to coccolithophore growth in the western Barents Sea. The competitive dynamics between coccolithophores and noncalcifying phytoplankton in the Barents Sea have shifted also over the past two decades. In the western Barents Sea, bloom frequencies of coccolithophores and noncalcifying phytoplankton exhibited a significant negative correlation before 2011 ( r = −0.60, P < 0.05; Fig. 4E ). However, following 2011, the rise in noncalcifying phytoplankton was no longer statistically linked to the low coccolithophore bloom frequency. In contrast, the eastern Barents Sea showed no correlation between coccolithophores and noncalcifying phytoplankton before 2011, but a positive correlation emerged thereafter ( r = 0.53, P < 0.05; Fig. 4H ). This shift does not contradict the earlier out-of-phase relationship observed in the western Barents Sea from 2003 to 2011. The pronounced warming and reduction in sea ice likely created new habitats conducive to the proliferation of various phytoplankton species ( 21 , 35 , 36 ); as both coccolithophores and noncalcifying phytoplankton began from low bloom frequencies, their populations increased synchronously in response to rising SSTs in the eastern Barents Sea post-2011 ( Fig. 4, F and H ). DISCUSSION Our mapping of coccolithophore blooms across the temperate North Atlantic and Barents Sea provides a highly detailed spatial and temporal characterization of coccolithophore trends in the 21st century in this region. The findings reveal that, despite confirming a polar shift in coccolithophore blooms in the Barents Sea driven by the movement of thermal front with ocean warming ( 11 , 21 ), overall coccolithophore blooms across the Barents Sea have not increased. This is primarily due to intensified spring blooms of noncalcifying phytoplankton, which may have encroached on the ecological niche of coccolithophores in the western Barents Sea. The massive decreasing trend in coccolithophore blooms observed in the subpolar North Atlantic contrasts with the reported increases in coccolithophore abundance based on in situ CPR data from 1965 to 2010 ( 26 ). However, this discrepancy should not be interpreted as a direct contradiction. In addition to a difference in the study interval, with only 10 years of overlap with ours, this discrepancy may also reflect differences in the coccolithophore species detected by satellites versus the CPR. Satellite observations primarily capture blooms of G. hux , recognizable by its distinct milky-white features due to prolific coccolith production, while the CPR’s capture of G. hux is limited. First, the CPR’s 270-μm mesh is not well suited to retain smaller coccolithophores, which typically range from 5 to 40 μm in size ( 37 , 38 ). Coccolithophores may only be retained in the CPR mesh if they become entangled in its filaments or if clogging by larger organisms reduces the filtration pore size ( 39 – 41 ). This setup makes CPR samples of smaller coccolithophores susceptible to interference from larger noncalcifying blooms. In addition, the CPR classifies coccolithophores simply as cells larger than 10 μm with calcium carbonate plates without finer taxonomic distinctions ( 39 ). As a result, compared to remote sensing, the CPR is more likely to detect larger coccolithophore species such as Coccolithus pelagicus ( 39 ), while smaller species such as G. hux , which typically have diameters under 10 μm ( 42 ) and are key contributors to large-scale blooms in our study region, are underrepresented. Last, differences in sampling methods likely compound the observed discrepancies: The CPR uses transect-based sampling with direct cell counts, whereas satellite observations offer daily, spatially extensive measurements of bloom occurrences; these variations in sampling frequency, coverage, and species sensitivity further explain the divergent coccolithophore trends reported in each dataset. Our analysis reveals SST as an important driver of coccolithophore blooms across the study region, reinforcing previous laboratory findings that G. hux growth rates increase with rising temperatures and decrease as temperatures fall ( 28 – 30 ). The substantial reduction in coccolithophore bloom frequency in the subpolar Atlantic is linked to declining SST, driven by the weakening of the AMOC. Evidence indicates that AMOC began weakening in the late 1950s, experienced a partial recovery during the 1990s to mid-2000s, and subsequently entered a renewed phase of decline ( 43 , 44 )—a period that coincides with our study time frame. However, the limited temporal coverage of satellite observations prevents an evaluation of how coccolithophore blooms in this region responded to AMOC (or SST) changes before 2003. Looking forward, global warming is projected to further weaken the AMOC through intensified hydrological cycles, accelerated sea ice loss, and increased meltwater input from the Greenland Ice Sheet ( 45 ). The implications of these changes for coccolithophores and noncalcifying phytoplankton warrant continued investigation. In the Barents Sea, extreme climate events exerted additional influence on bloom frequency, particularly in years characterized by Arctic Oscillation (AO) and North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) anomalies post-2011 ( Fig. 4B ). The summer of 2016 witnessed peak coccolithophore frequency, coinciding with record high temperatures, while an abrupt decline in 2014 correlated with anomalously low temperatures. Although previous research, including our own, has implicated accelerating Atlantic inflows in the eastward migration of coccolithophores in the Barents Sea ( 10 ), our current analysis found no significant correlation between coccolithophore trends and surface current velocities across the entire region, despite increased Atlantic current velocities (table S1). Notably, coccolithophore blooms also showed no significant correlations with mixed layer depth or sea surface salinity. In the eastern Barents Sea, meridional wind stress exhibited a significant positive correlation with coccolithophore blooms (table S1). This relationship may be attributed to fertilization effects driven by regionally enhanced uptake of atmospheric CO 2 , as suggested by previous studies ( 24 ). While increased CO 2 levels have been associated with the proliferation of coccolithophores in the North Atlantic ( 26 , 27 ), we also found a significant negative correlation between pH and the increased coccolithophore blooms in the eastern Barents Sea (table S1). However, this mechanism fails to account for the recent decline in coccolithophore populations observed in the North Atlantic region. Ocean acidification, known to affect coccolithophore calcification ( 46 , 47 ), presents a complex picture with both positive and negative effects, rendering its net contribution to observed trends challenging to quantify ( 48 ). Furthermore, viral infections have been proposed as a potential factor in the decline of mesoscale coccolithophore communities ( 49 , 50 ). However, assessing their impact on large-scale, long-term trends remains a challenge, mainly due to a lack of observations. Our comprehensive mapping of daily coccolithophore blooms across the North Atlantic–Barents Sea continuum provides a critical baseline for understanding phenological shifts and species succession of phytoplankton since the start of the 21st century. Our work has substantial implications for marine ecosystem dynamics and global climate processes. Coccolithophores play a pivotal role in marine biogeochemistry, contributing substantially to sediment formation, modulating P co 2 , and facilitating the export of organic matter to the deep ocean ( 51 ). Hence, our findings provide valuable inputs for improving ocean carbon flux models. Furthermore, given the high sensitivity of coccolithophores to SST, their spatial and temporal distribution may serve as a critical indicator of climate change. This offers a nuanced lens through which to observe and interpret climatic shifts, providing important insights into understanding and responding to future climate challenges. MATERIALS AND METHODS Data sources Satellite data We used daily 1-km resolution MODIS Aqua satellite imagery to monitor coccolithophore blooms in the temperate North Atlantic and the Barents Sea. MODIS Aqua Level 1A images were obtained from the Ocean Biology Distributed Active Archive Center and processed into Rayleigh-corrected reflectance ( R rc ; dimensionless) using NASA’s recommended software, SeaDAS (version 7.5) ( www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools/seadas ). Pixels affected by unfavorable observational conditions—such as straylight, clouds, land interference, high sunglint, elevated solar zenith angle, and high sensor zenith angle—were excluded using the quality flag (l2_flag) before coccolithophore bloom detection. For comparison, NASA’s standard Level 2 PIC products from MODIS were downloaded from the NASA Ocean Color Website ( https://oceancolor.gsfc.nasa.gov ) to evaluate the differences between coccolithophore blooms identified through our algorithm and the standard PIC algorithm. Physical oceanography parameters To assess the influence of SST on coccolithophore bloom trends, we used 1/4° daily Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST V2.1) data from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) ( www.ncei.noaa.gov ). To investigate the influence of the polar temperature front on coccolithophore distribution in the Barents Sea, we delineated its position during summer months. The SST gradient was estimated using the method outlined by Martínez-Moreno et al. ( 52 ), and the polar front was identified from these gradients via the probability density function approach developed by Oziel et al. ( 53 ). Sea surface salinity, meridional and zonal wind stress, mixed layer depth, and ocean surface currents were analyzed to assess their influence on coccolithophore trends. The first three parameters were sourced from the monthly global ocean and sea ice reanalysis (Ocean Reanalysis System 5) dataset produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts ( https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-oras5 ). pH data obtained from the Global Ocean Surface Carbon dataset published by the EU Copernicus Marine Services ( https://marine.copernicus.eu/user-corner/product-roadmap/transition-information?product=MULTIOBS_GLO_BIO_CARBON_SURFACE_MYNRT_015_008 ). Geostrophic currents, used to approximate ocean surface currents, were derived from satellite altimetry products. The absolute geostrophic velocity components (meridional and zonal) were obtained from the Copernicus Marine and Environment Monitoring Service’s Sea Level Version DT2021 dataset ( https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/satellite-sea-level-global ). Satellite-mapped noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms The global 1-km daily phytoplankton bloom dataset ( 54 ) was used to investigate the competitive relationships between coccolithophores and noncalcifying phytoplankton species. This dataset includes key bloom-forming species such as diatoms and dinoflagellates in coastal waters while excluding large algae (e.g., Sargassum and Ulva ) and coccolithophores. The dataset is publicly available at ( https://zenodo.org/records/7359262 ). Climate extremes To assess the potential impacts of climate extremes on coccolithophore trends in the Barents Sea, we used four key indicators: the NAO, AO, Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO), and El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). The NAO and AO indices were obtained from the National Weather Service Climate Prediction Center ( www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov ) The Multivariate ENSO Index (MEI), used for ENSO, and the AMO index were obtained from the NOAA Physical Sciences Laboratory ( https://psl.noaa.gov ). In situ data To evaluate the performance of our algorithm, we compiled in situ observations of both coccolithophore blooms and noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms. The coccolithophore data were obtained from two sources: (i) published literature and (ii) the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, www.gbif.org ), by retrieving records for the species E. huxleyi . Although the definition of a “bloom” can vary, a threshold of 1 × 10 6 cells liter −1 is commonly recommended ( 55 ). This criterion was used to select bloom events from previous studies. However, only a limited number of records with precise geolocation during our study period met this threshold. To expand the sample set and test whether our algorithm could detect weaker blooms, we adopted a more inclusive criterion for the GBIF dataset, identifying bloom occurrences as samples with cell counts exceeding 1 × 10 5 cells liter −1 —an abundance level also frequently associated with bloom conditions ( 55 – 57 ). For noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms, in situ data were obtained from the Harmful Algal Bloom Events ( https://haedat.iode.org ) database, which documents numerous bloom events caused by noncalcifying species. We retained records with accurate geolocation and aggregated those occurring on the same day within a 1° by 1° grid to avoid duplication. In total, we obtained 51 coccolithophore bloom records and 206 noncalcifying bloom records with corresponding satellite coverage. Development of an improved coccolithophore bloom detection algorithm The temperate North Atlantic and Barents Sea are among the most concentrated regions for coccolithophores, with G. hux being the dominant species. Blooms of G. hux can occur on an immense scale in this region, and calcified G. hux cells, or large numbers of detached coccoliths during bloom, cause substantial backscattering. This backscattering creates distinct bright, milky-turquoise water coloration, making these blooms highly detectable by remote sensing satellites (fig. S4B) ( 58 ). A common method for identifying coccolithophore blooms is through remotely sensed PIC concentrations ( 59 ). Since coccolithophore blooms substantial increase PIC levels, a threshold is often used to classify bloom and nonbloom pixels ( 11 , 24 , 60 ). The estimation of PIC concentration was initially based on an empirical relationship between the backscattering coefficient at 546 nm [𝑏 bc (546)] and PIC concentration ( 61 ). This was further developed into a switching algorithm that uses two bands (443 and 555 nm) ( 62 ) and three bands (670, 765, and 865 nm) ( 63 ) to derive estimates across different water regions. However, the standard PIC concentration algorithm has several limitations. Since it relies on absolute water-leaving radiance, it is sensitive to atmospheric correction errors ( 64 ). In addition, suspended sediments and high concentrations of diatoms can cause similar high reflectance in the green wavelength range, leading to misclassification of high PIC concentrations. This also makes it challenging to set a unified threshold for identifying coccolithophores based on PIC. For example, thresholds of 2, 4, and 6 mmol m −3 have been used to delineate coccolithophore blooms in the Barents Sea ( 11 , 24 , 60 ). Despite attempts to improve the classification methods for coccolithophore blooms ( 64 – 69 ), current algorithms still tend to produce substantial false positives in high-reflectance waters, particularly in shelf seas and coastal areas. We developed an improved algorithm to classify coccolithophore blooms based on their spectral characteristics using the MODIS Aqua R rc dataset. Our approach uses the R rc difference between 555 and 645 nm ( R rc 555 − 645 ) as the primary indicator for detecting coccolithophores, which is similar to the CI2 algorithm of Mitchell et al. ( 64 ). Coccolithophores exhibit high reflectance in the blue-green bands, and the 555-nm band was chosen because it minimizes the influence of chlorophyll absorption near 443 and 667 nm. Many coccolithophore detection algorithms focus on this wavelength ( 62 , 64 , 65 , 70 ). The 645-nm band was selected because it demonstrates much lower and more stable reflectance compared to 555 nm, due to higher water absorption, making it ideal for constructing a reliable difference algorithm. To minimize the influence of suspended sediments or high concentrations of diatoms—both of which can cause high reflectance values similar to coccolithophores in R rc 555 − 645 , we used an additional band difference: 469 to 555 nm ( R rc 469 − 555 ). Coccolithophore spectral characteristics vary at different stages of bloom development ( 71 ). In the later stage of coccolithophore blooms, when coccoliths have mostly detached from the cells, the bloom waters exhibit a reflectance peak in the blue band (fig. S4B). In contrast, noncalcifying phytoplankton and sediment-rich waters typically peak in the green band (fig. S4, C and D). During the later stages, R rc 469 − 555 in coccolithophore blooms is usually positive, whereas in noncalcifying phytoplankton and sediment-rich waters, it is usually negative. In the early stages of coccolithophore blooms, when most coccoliths are still attached to the cells, the reflectance peak is in the green band (fig. S4B). At this stage, the R rc 469 − 555 of coccolithophore blooms, the noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms, and the sediment-rich waters will be similar, usually close to zero or negative (fig. S4, B and D), but the R rc 555 − 645 of the coccolithophore blooms at this stage will be much higher than the other two, usually higher than at earlier stages, making it easier to distinguish coccolithophore blooms from noncalcifying phytoplankton and sediment-rich waters. Consequently, coccolithophore blooms exhibit a negative correlation between R rc 469 − 555 and R rc 555 − 645 , with one of these two metrics always being higher than those of noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms or sediment-rich waters. This dual-band difference approach improves the accuracy of coccolithophore bloom detection by leveraging spectral differences at different bloom stages. To demonstrate the capability of the dual-band difference method to distinguish between bloom and nonbloom, we manually selected coccolithophore bloom pixels and nonbloom pixels. The process for determining the pixels can be summarized in the following three steps: Step 1: Selection of candidate images from the MODIS R rc dataset. We began by compiling coccolithophore bloom events reported in previous studies (table S2). Initially, 10 MODIS images containing confirmed bloom occurrences were selected; however, these scenes provided limited spatial representation, often capturing isolated blooms without the presence of co-occurring noncalcifying phytoplankton or sediment-laden waters. Furthermore, they failed to encompass the full phenological spectrum of coccolithophore blooms across the North Atlantic to Barents Sea continuum. To address these limitations, we manually screened additional MODIS imagery. Coccolithophore blooms are visually distinctive in true-color composites, exhibiting bright, milky-white patterns with seasonal consistency in specific regions—such as the Celtic Sea in April to May, the North Sea in June to July, and the Barents Sea in July to August. Guided by these spatiotemporal patterns, we expanded our dataset to 35 MODIS scenes with unambiguous coccolithophore bloom signatures. Step 2: Initial identification of candidate patches from imagery. We first identified candidate regions within each image based on their distinct visual characteristics, including color, texture, and geographic context. These features were used to delineate approximate patch locations for different water types, as summarized below: 1) Coccolithophore blooms (fig. S5, A to C): Milky-white or turquoise waters, typically occurring in open ocean or shelf regions, often forming patchy or swirling structures. 2) Noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms (fig. S5, D to F): Bright-green to dark-green waters, distributed in offshore or coastal regions, typically exhibiting patchy or swirling structures. 3) Sediment-rich waters (fig. S5, G to I): Yellow-brown or grayish waters, usually located in coastal or estuarine regions, often forming plume-like, banded, or diffuse structures. Step 3: Spectral confirmation and pixel selection. Following the initial identification of candidate patches, pixels were further evaluated on the basis of their spectral characteristics. The classification criteria were developed from previously published in situ spectral observations ( 58 , 71 , 72 ) and were used to guide the selection of representative training samples by comparing satellite-derived spectra with known in situ signatures. The decision rules are summarized below: 1) Coccolithophore blooms (fig. S5, A to C): Characterized by high reflectance in the blue bands (mean R rc412,443,469,488 > 0.01) and a rapid decrease toward the red bands. The blue-band reflectance exceeds that in the red bands (mean R rc645,667,678 ) by 0.005. Spectral peaks occur at R rc488 or R rc531 , and no distinct fluorescence signal is observed at R rc678 . 2) Noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms (fig. S5, D to F): Characterized by relatively low blue-band reflectance (mean R rc412,443,469,488 < 0.01), a peak in the green bands ( R rc547 or R rc555 ), and a distinct fluorescence signal at R rc678 , with R rc678 exceeding R rc667 . 3) Sediment-rich waters (fig. S5, G to I): Characterized by elevated red-band reflectance and suppressed blue reflectance, with a blue-red reflectance difference (mean R rc412,443,469,488 to mean R rc645,667,678 ) of <0.005. Spectral peaks occur in the green or red bands, and no observable fluorescence signal is present. Pixels were assigned to a category only when their spectral characteristics were consistent with the defining features of that class. Those that did not satisfy these criteria were considered ambiguous and excluded from the training dataset. Last, a total of 99,550 bloom pixels were selected from 35 different MODIS Rrc images, covering various sea areas and different stages of coccolithophore blooms. In addition, 23,830 pixels associated with suspended sediment and 17,930 pixels representing noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms—both of which are often misclassified as coccolithophore blooms using standard PIC algorithms—were also identified. When plotting R rc 469 − 555 against R rc 555 − 645 , the coccolithophore bloom pixels show clear separation from those of suspended sediment and noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms, with a clear boundary between the two categories (fig. S4A). This boundary can be expressed as (1) R rc 555 − 645 = − 1.5205 × R rc 469 − 555 + 0.0081 The classification of coccolithophore blooms was performed using the dual-band difference algorithm through the following steps: Step 1: Input data preparation: For each pixel, obtain the R rc values at the required wavelengths (469, 555, and 645 nm) from the MODIS satellite image. Step 2: Calculation of band differences: Compute the two spectral differences R rc 555 − 645 and R rc 469 − 555 . Step 3: Evaluation relative to decision boundary: Compare the calculated values ( R rc 469 − 555 and R rc 555 − 645 ) with the decision boundary defined by Eq. 1 . Step 4: Pixel classification. If R rc 555 − 645 > − 1.5205 × R rc 469 − 555 + 0.0081 , then the pixel is classified as a coccolithophore bloom. Otherwise, the pixel is classified as a nonbloom pixel. Assessments of the algorithm performance To demonstrate the accuracy of our coccolithophore bloom detection algorithm, we conducted validation through the following three aspects. First, we evaluated the performance of our algorithm, as well as the PIC algorithm, using in situ observations of coccolithophore and noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms. A 7-day time window centered on each in situ sampling date was used for validation. For coccolithophore samples, successful detection (or true positive) was defined as the presence of at least one coccolithophore pixel identified by the algorithm on any day within the validation window. Conversely, if any coccolithophore pixels were detected during the same time window for a noncalcifying phytoplankton bloom sample, then it was considered a false positive. The validation results (table S3) show that our algorithm achieved a moderate detection rate (61%) for coccolithophore blooms while maintaining a low false-positive rate (27%) for noncalcifying blooms. In contrast, the PIC algorithm failed to simultaneously ensure both moderate detection accuracy and low error rates across a range of commonly used thresholds (2, 4, and 6 mmol m −3 ) for classifying coccolithophore blooms ( 11 , 24 , 60 ). It is worth noting that the overall true positive rate for coccolithophore blooms remains moderate (∼60%). This is likely due to several factors. First, many samples have relatively low cell abundances (1.0 × 10 5 to 1.0 × 10 6 cells liter −1 ), which, although still associated with bloom conditions, are more difficult to detect via satellite. Second, despite the use of a 7-day temporal window, satellite detection may still be limited by cloud cover and data availability, further reducing the effective detection rate. Nonetheless, this comparison demonstrates that our algorithm offers a more balanced performance—successfully identifying true coccolithophore bloom pixels while substantially reducing false detections from noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms. Second, we compared the spatial and temporal patterns of coccolithophore blooms identified by our algorithm with those detected by the PIC method, using images from the North Atlantic to the Barents Sea (fig. S6). These included various bloom stages, as well as waters dominated by diatoms and suspended sediments. Visual comparisons of RGB (red-green-blue) imagery, PIC concentration maps, and reflectance spectra revealed that our method reliably captured coccolithophore bloom patterns while avoiding false positives in sediment-laden or diatom-rich regions—areas where the PIC product often shows elevated values and may misclassify blooms when using a threshold-based approach. To further quantify this comparison, we evaluated the spatial distributions of coccolithophore bloom frequency derived from our algorithm and those classified using PIC concentrations from 2003 to 2022, along with their respective trends over the past two decades. Specifically, we calculated the mean annual bloom frequency based on our method and compared it to a 20-year annual maximum composite of the PIC product (fig. S7). We chose to use the maximum-value composite rather than annual averages because satellite observations in polar regions are largely limited to summer months, which coincide with the peak coccolithophore bloom season. In contrast, lower-latitude regions have year-round satellite coverage, meaning that annual averages often include many nonbloom periods, thereby diluting the overall PIC signal and underrepresenting true bloom coverage. The spatial distribution and temporal trend patterns of both products showed broad agreement in certain regions, such as the Barents Sea and coastal Iceland (fig. S7, A to D). Using a PIC threshold of >2 mmol m −3 —identified as yielding the highest classification accuracy (table S3)—we found a 75.6% spatial overlap between our algorithm and the PIC product (fig. S7E). Of the remaining nonoverlapping areas, 19.3% were exclusive to the PIC product, primarily located in the Baltic Sea, White Sea, and Arctic Ocean—regions often dominated by noncalcifying phytoplankton species ( 73 – 75 ). In contrast, only 5.1% of the total bloom area is identified only by our method. Notably, the PIC product exhibited anomalously high concentrations in estuarine and coastal areas, such as the English Channel and eastern Greenland, likely reflecting misclassification of high sediment loads as coccolithophores. Elevated PIC values north of 80°N may result from floating sea ice or ice-edge algal communities, as coccolithophores rarely bloom in these low-temperature environments ( 24 ). Similarly, high PIC values were detected in the Baltic Sea, likely due to strong signals from frequent cyanobacteria blooms rather than coccolithophores ( 73 , 76 ). Last, using the same pixel sampling steps as in the algorithm development, we identified an additional 65,000 bloom pixels and 30,000 nonbloom pixels from 30 different MODIS images covering various times and regions. By comparing the algorithm-classified results with the manually delineated pixels, we found that our coccolithophore bloom detection algorithm achieved similar producer accuracy to the PIC method, with over 95% of coccolithophore bloom waters correctly classified. However, when it came to excluding false positives—such as sediment-rich waters or noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms, which can be erroneously identified as coccolithophore blooms—our algorithm demonstrated a notable advantage. Exploring patterns and trends in coccolithophores We applied the developed coccolithophores bloom detection algorithm to all available MODIS Aqua Level 2 R rc images from 2003 to 2022, generating 1-km daily composite coccolithophore bloom products. The number of blooms for each year was obtained by summing the daily blooms within the same year. Coccolithophore bloom frequency was defined following the method of Dai et al. ( 54 ), which can be expressed as (2) Bloom frequency = n N ∑ i = 1 n M i where M i is the bloom count of each 1-km resolution pixel within a 0.1° by 0.1° grid cell over a year, n represents the number of relevant pixels affected by coccolithophore blooms within the same grid cell (number of pixels with M i > 0 ), and N is the total number of 1-km MODIS pixels in that grid cell. We calculated annual bloom frequency from 2003 to 2022 and determined the long-term trend in the North Atlantic to Barents Sea region using linear least-squares regression ( Fig. 1 ). Similar calculations were conducted at a monthly scale, enabling us to estimate trends for individual months ( Fig. 3 ). Investigating the drivers of coccolithophore bloom trends To explore the influence of SST on coccolithophore bloom trends, we obtained monthly SST grid data at a 0.25° by 0.25° resolution and calculated the monthly coccolithophore bloom frequency using Eq. 2 at the same resolution. We used the Pearson correlation coefficient to assess the relationship between SST and coccolithophore bloom frequency for each pixel over the past 20 years. In addition, we examined the correlation between coccolithophore bloom frequency and the bloom frequency of noncalcifying phytoplankton. To further investigate the eastward migration of coccolithophores in the Barents Sea, we first generated composite images of summer (July, August, and September) coccolithophore bloom frequencies. We then calculated the mean values for grid cells within the eastern and western regions to assess interannual variability and trends in coccolithophore bloom frequency in both areas. Using the same method, we also analyzed the annual variation in noncalcifying phytoplankton blooms and various marine physical parameters, examining their correlation with coccolithophore bloom frequency over different time periods using the Pearson correlation coefficient. In the Barents Sea, the thermal front (also referred to as the polar front) in its western region is bathymetry-driven and remains stable year round, while the eastern front shows considerable variability ( 50 ). We calculated the annual average latitude of the eastern polar front between 30° and 55°E. To assess shifts in coccolithophore blooms, we manually traced the northern boundary of the blooms in the eastern Barents Sea, defined as the northernmost occurrence of blooms each year, and calculated the average latitude annually.
