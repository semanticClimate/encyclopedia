Abstract Climate models show considerable discrepancies in their future projections around the Atlantic, mainly due to uncertainties in the fate of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC). Climate models suggest a reduction in AMOC strength of 32 ± 37% by 2100 (90% probability, Shared Socioeconomic Pathways 2-4.5 scenario, Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6). To refine this estimate and reduce its uncertainty, we use four different observational constraint methods. The best one, which provides the lowest leave-one-out error, integrates a large set of observable variables using ridge-regularized linear regression—a method unusual in climate science. It gives an estimate of the AMOC slowdown of 51 ± 8% (90% probability), i.e., a weakening ∼ 60% stronger than suggested by the multimodel mean. This refinement mainly results from correcting a bias in South Atlantic surface salinity, consistent with recent studies emphasizing its role in the proximity to an AMOC tipping point. This more substantial AMOC weakening has key implications for future adaptation strategies. Combining observations and climate models suggests a 60% stronger weakening of the Atlantic circulation than using models alone. status released display-pdf yes is-olf no is-manuscript no is-preprint no is-journal-matter no is-scanned no is-retracted no Received 2025 Mar 13; Accepted 2026 Mar 12; Collection date 2026 Apr 17. INTRODUCTION Projections of the future climate are essential to support decision-makers in planing effective adaptation strategies ( 1 , 2 ). These projections are provided by climate models for different socioeconomic scenarios involving greenhouse gas and aerosol emissions, as well as land use. However, climate projections can be very different depending on the chosen climate model and socioeconomic scenario, making the future climate very uncertain ( 1 , 2 ). The uncertainty due to differences between climate models is called model uncertainty. The uncertainty due to differences between socioeconomic scenarios is called scenario uncertainty. A third source of uncertainty ( 3 ) is the internal variability, i.e., the natural climate variability unrelated to human activities. The relative importance of the source of uncertainty depends on the process considered ( 3 ). Model uncertainty is due to the differences in the choices of physical processes included in the model, parameterization, and resolution ( 4 ). This results in substantial differences in the simulation of several complex processes between climate models. One such process is the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), which has a global impact ( 5 ) and shows a wide range of weakening amplitude in the future ( 6 ). In turn, this strongly influences the projection of many climate variables (e.g., temperature, precipitation, and jet stream position) in the Atlantic regions and beyond through climate teleconnections ( 7 ). The AMOC is a system of ocean currents that transports warm and salty water into the North Atlantic via upper-ocean currents ( 8 ). In winter, these salty waters release heat to the atmosphere, cooling them and making them very dense ( 9 , 10 ). They eventually form deep waters in the Irminger, Labrador, and Nordic Seas through convection processes ( 11 ). These newly formed deep waters are then transported southward by deep currents ( 8 ). Changes to the AMOC will strongly influence future climates in the Atlantic and beyond ( 5 ). A key consequence of an AMOC slowdown, among many others, is the equatorward shift of the intertropical convergence zone ( 12 ), which will lead to the drying out of the Sahel region and put pressure on its agriculture and food security ( 13 ). The climate models from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) agree that the AMOC will very likely decline in the 21st century ( 14 ), but they disagree on the exact magnitude of such a decline, mainly due to model uncertainty ( 6 ). Reintges et al. ( 15 ) highlighted that, in CMIP5, the total AMOC uncertainty by the end of the 21st century is mainly due to the climate model uncertainty rather than the scenario uncertainty or the internal variability. This fact is very unusual for climate variables, whose total uncertainty by the end of the 21st century is usually more related to anthropogenic emission scenarios [e.g., global temperature change ( 16 , 17 )]. This paper will examine whether this CMIP5 result holds in CMIP6 models. Methods called emergent or observational constraint (OC) ( 6 , 18 – 28 ) have been developed to reduce the model uncertainty of a future climate variable of interest, hereafter called projected variable. These methods constrain the estimate and model uncertainty of the projected variable using the real-world observations of one or more observable variables. This results in a constrained model uncertainty that is smaller than unconstrained one. However, there is a wide variety of approaches to achieving OC in the literature. Methods based on the approach hereafter referred to as “weighted average” are averaging the climate model projections, with weights that depend on the model’s ability to reproduce selected real-world observations ( 18 – 20 ). OC methods based on the approach hereafter referred to as “unregularized linear regression” learn a linear relationship between the observable variable(s) and the projected variable using climate models. They use real-world observations through this relationship to predict the value of the projected variable ( 6 , 21 – 23 ). Some approaches use a nonlinear relationship ( 24 – 26 ), mainly based on “random forests.” Other OC methods are based on a Bayesian theory, estimating the distribution of the predicted variable given the real-world observations ( 27 , 28 ). There are three important comments regarding this literature. (i) Each OC method makes different assumptions and quantifies the constrained model uncertainty differently. Therefore, there is a need to compare OC methods on a common basis. (ii) Few OCs have been applied to the AMOC ( 6 , 29 , 30 ), so it is worth evaluating which method is the best for reducing the model uncertainty in the AMOC by 2100. (iii) Most papers use only one observable variable ( 6 , 18 , 19 , 21 , 22 , 27 , 29 – 34 ), chosen in the light of a known physical process. Others suggest using multiple observable variables ( 35 ). It remains unclear which approach is more robust. Given the complexity of the AMOC and the different drivers of its future weakening, it seems necessary to consider multiple observable variables to constrain the estimate and the model uncertainty of the future AMOC. However, the few OC methods that constrained the future AMOC so far are using a single observable variable, which may explain their poor efficiency. For example, Weijer et al. ( 6 ) used the mean AMOC over the period covered by the RAPID observations; Baker et al. ( 29 ) used the mean over the historical period for a metric representing the Indo-Pacific diffusion; and Bonan et al. ( 30 ) used the change in AMOC strength predicted by the thermal wind relationship. Salinity and temperature drive buoyancy, which is a key parameter in water mass transformation and deep water formation ( 36 ) and consequently in AMOC strength ( 37 ). Therefore, the sea surface temperature (SST) and sea surface salinity (SSS) are expected to play a crucial role in the AMOC slowdown simulated by climate models. This is demonstrated by the substantial impact of surface fluxes on AMOC weakening and model uncertainty in CMIP5 ( 38 ) and CMIP6 ( 39 ). Different subregions of the Atlantic Ocean may play a role: the deep water formation regions (Labrador, Irminger, and Nordic Seas) ( 40 ), the Subtropical North Atlantic ( 41 ), the South Atlantic ( 42 ), etc. Even other basins may play a role, as illustrated by the effect of Indian Ocean SST on North Atlantic wind stress ( 43 ) or from the El Niño region ( 44 ). The use of both SST and SSS in these regions as observable variables therefore seems relevant to constrain the estimate and the model uncertainty of future AMOC weakening. However, this has not been done up to now. This paper proposes a comparison of the multimodel mean (MMM) and OC methods applied to the future AMOC based on four different approaches: the weighted average, unregularized linear regression, nonlinear regression (random forest), and ridge-regularized linear regression. Ridge-regularized linear regression is a method designed to address multiple correlated variables in a multivariate linear regression. It is often used in fields where a process depends on many others, requiring multivariate regression, such as health or the economy ( 45 ). It is rarely used in OC methods for climate based on linear regression, as most of these methods are univariate. However, the complexity of the climate system makes multivariate linear regression an attractive option, and ridge-regularized linear regression could be a powerful tool in this context. Furthermore, two cases are compared in the article: one using only one observable variable (the past AMOC; the univariate approach) and one using a set of observable variables (the past observed AMOC and past SST and past SSS from different regions; the multivariate approach). The regions of the latter case are defined in Materials and Methods (see fig. S1). They roughly represent key ocean areas, which are thought to potentially influence the ongoing and future fate of the AMOC. These areas include the classical regions in the Atlantic (Tropical, Subtropical, Subpolar, etc.), the Indian Ocean ( 43 ), and one in the Equatorial Pacific Niño region ( 44 ). This article compares the MMM and OC methods in their performances using a metric called leave-one-out error. The leave-one-out method (also known as the “perfect model” approach; see Materials and Methods for further details) leaves one climate model out and uses the value of the observable variable simulated by the isolated model as the real-world observation in the OC method. The projected value estimated by the OC method is then compared to the projected value simulated by the isolated model to calculate the error. This process is repeated for each climate model to calculate the total error called leave-one-out error. The method with the lowest leave-one-out error is considered the most effective for predicting future data based on past observations and an ensemble of climate models. This article presents AMOC projections using all CMIP6 models that provide an AMOC projection for different socioeconomic emission scenarios. The total uncertainty in the future AMOC is then decomposed into three sources of variance (model uncertainty, scenario uncertainty, and internal variability), which highlights the dominant role of model uncertainty. To reduce this uncertainty, the performance of different OC methods is compared. This results in a best estimate of the future AMOC with a stronger weakening than the MMM by about 60% (Shared Socio-economic Pathways 2-4.5). This estimate of the future AMOC is then physically interpreted in terms of salinity and temperature biases in climate models and explained using existing literature. Last, the robustness of these OC methods with respect to the considered scenario and the choice of variable definitions is tested, producing consistent results, which highlight a stronger AMOC weakening by the end of the century when constrained by available key observations. This may be due to the real AMOC being closer to a tipping point than a number of models suggest. The model uncertainty of the AMOC anomalies ( Fig. 1B ) is close to zero in the reference period (by construction) and increases over time. Therefore, differences in AMOC forcing factors —e.g., the increase in precipitation in the high latitudes of the North Atlantic, which depends on the warming level—might also increase over time. Whether to treat the AMOC as an anomaly is an important choice, as it has a major impact on the differences between AMOC time series from climate models. Here, we argue that it makes more sense to use absolute (rather than relative) values of the AMOC because two climate models with the same relative AMOC weakening can produce two very different absolute values in 2100, depending on their initial values. Every sverdrup of the absolute AMOC in 2100 is important, as it represents ~0.05 PW of the northward heat transport ( 49 ). Almost all CMIP6 climate models project a decline in the AMOC by 2100 under all SSP scenarios, ranging from 3 to 72% depending on the climate model and scenario. Only one climate model projects an increase (of 6%) under the SSP1-1.9 scenario (the scenario with the lowest greenhouse gas emissions). The AMOC in the MMM slows down more under stronger emission scenarios, with decreases of 23 ± 45, 32 ± 37, 37 ± 33, and 43 ± 34% (90% probability) under the scenarios SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5, respectively (see Table 1 ). Throughout the article, the results for the SSP1-1.9 scenario are not shown, as only five climate models are available. The differences in AMOC projections between scenarios are small compared to the differences between climate models; the time series of the MMM for each scenario lies within one multimodel SD of the model uncertainty for scenarios SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0 (see Fig. 1 ). This suggests that most of the total uncertainty in the future AMOC is due to differences between climate models (model uncertainty), as will be demonstrated in the next section. This article compares four different OC methods: weighted average, unregularized linear regression, random forest regression, and ridge-regularized linear regression. The first three represent the main types of existing approaches used in climate science. Figure 3 also compares the results of these OC approaches with the unconstrained results, i.e., the MMM results. This comparison of methods is performed in two different cases. The first case uses only one observable variable in the OC method, namely, the past AMOC (2005 to 2023 mean) as observed by the RAPID array ( 46 ). The second case uses a set of 19 observable variables, the past AMOC (2005 to 2023 mean), and the past SST and SSS (1900 to 2020 means), averaged over nine classical oceanographic regions of the world (see fig. S1 in the Supplementary Materials). Each OC method exhibits a lower estimate of the future AMOC magnitude than the MMM, as shown in Fig. 3A , and an improvement in the leave-one-out error compared to the MMM, as shown in Fig. 3B . When multiple observable variables are used, this correction to the estimate and reduction in the leave-one-out error are even stronger. This may be due to the additional knowledge gained from the extra observable variables. When only one observable variable is used, we illustrate the principles and results of the weighted average and unregularized linear regression methods in fig. S2. The results of the different OC methods are presented below in order of increasing leave-one-out performance: (i) weighted average, (ii) random forest, (iii) unregularized linear regression, and (iv) ridge-regularized linear regression. (i) Compared to the MMM, the weighted average only reduces the leave-one-out error by 5%, whether one or multiple observable variables are used. This can be explained by the fact that the weighted average method gives more weight to the models that are close to the real-world observations. When using only one observable variable—here, the 2005-to-2023 mean AMOC—the MMM appears to be close to the real-world observation (with a difference of only 0.04 sverdrup). This is the case for this variable but not necessarily for others. Therefore, most models centered around the MMM are unaffected, resulting in minimal change to the average and error. In multivariate (including the past SST and SSS), the weighted average estimate is also close to the MMM. This indicates that the climate models that perform better (the closest to the observations; see Eq. 14 ) are centered around the MMM. (ii) The random forest method shows a leave-one-out error reduction of 35% compared to the MMM, whether one or multiple observable variables are used. However, this does not outperform the linear regression methods (see below). This suggests that the nonlinearities in the random forest method may overfit the data, i.e., learn incorrect relationships between the variables. We therefore recommend the use of far more samples (in this case, climate models) to make the random forest method more robust. (iii) The unregularized linear regression method shows a strong reduction of leave-one-out error of 44%, thanks to strong intermodel relationships between the observable variables and the future AMOC. This reduction in leave-one-out error is similar whether one or multiple observable variables are used. We would have expected a stronger reduction in leave-one-out error in the case with multiple observable variables. The inclusion of more information seems to have an effect only on the reduction of model uncertainty. The high leave-one-out error suggests that there is some overfitting, a known risk in unregularized linear regression when having a number of samples (climate models) of the same magnitude, or less, than the number of variables. To limit this risk of overfitting, and thus improve the robustness measured by the leave-one-out error, we decided to also use a ridge-regularized linear regression, as described in (iv). (iv) Ridge-regularized linear regression is the best method in terms of leave-one-out error when using multiple observable variables. It reduces the leave-one-out error by 60% compared to the MMM. Its leave-one-out error is thus 58% lower than the weighted average, 39% lower than the random forest and 29% lower than the unregularized linear regression. When using a single observable variable, this regularization is unnecessary, as it is designed for multiple variables. As this method has the lowest leave-one-out error, it provides our best estimate of future AMOC. It predicts a 2091-to-2100 mean AMOC of 8.1 ± 1.4 sverdrup compared to the MMM estimate of 12.0 ± 6.5 sverdrup. Model uncertainty is thus reduced by 79%. To calculate the percentage change in AMOC, we need to use a reference. We constrained the 1850-to-1900 mean AMOC using the same method (ridge-regularized linear regression), resulting in a constrained reference of 16.4 sverdrup; see Table 1 . Using this reference, the AMOC weakening estimated by the ridge-regularized linear regression method is 51 ± 8%, while the MMM method estimates a weakening of 32 ± 37% (see Table 1 ). This OC method predicts a decline that is stronger than that estimated by the MMM by about 20% or an additional 60% decline compared to the one estimated by the MMM and cited in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) reports ( 14 ). This is a key result with implications for the future climate of the Atlantic and beyond. The physical interpretation of this result is now discussed in the next section. Interpretation of the best estimate of the AMOC future According to the leave-one-out evaluation metric, the best method is ridge-regularized linear regression using multiple observable variables. This method estimates the 2091-to-2100 averaged AMOC to be f ˆ ( x 0 ) = 8.1 sverdrup, as opposed to the MMM estimate of μ ˆ Y = 12.0 sverdrup. As described in Materials and Methods, x 0 is the real-world observation, f ˆ is the linear regression function estimated on the ensemble of climate models, and μ ˆ Y is the MMM of Y . Vectors are noted in bold, while scalars are not, e.g., x 0 = ( x 0 1 , … , x 0 p ) with p = 19 being the number of observable variables. The correction of −3.9 sverdrup of the estimate can be explained by Eq. 18 (see Materials and Methods) from the ridge-regularized linear regression: f ˆ ( x 0 ) = μ ˆ Y − β ˆ T ( μ ˆ X − x 0 ) , where the vector β ˆ stands for the estimated coefficients of the ridge-regularized linear regression and the vector μ ˆ X is the MMM of X . The correction between the unconstrained estimate ( μ ˆ Y ) and the constrained estimate [ f ˆ ( x 0 ) ] depends on two terms: the coefficients β ˆ and the term ( μ ˆ X − x 0 ) , which is the difference between the MMM and the real-world observation. The latter represents what is hereafter called the multimodel bias (of X ). Each observable variable j ∈ [ 1 , … , p ] influences the correction depending on its coefficient β ˆ j and its MMM bias ( μ ˆ X j − x 0 j ) . Thus, thanks to this approach, we developed a way to interpret a linear OC method by decomposing the impact of each observable variable on the correction to the estimate compared to the MMM. The values of the different terms are given in table S3 for each observable variable. Figure 4 shows the correction induced by each observable variable j , expressed by β ˆ j ( μ ˆ X j − x 0 j ) . This figure does not show the contribution of the past AMOC observable variable, as the latter is almost zero; see table S3. This allows us to identify the role of each observable variable in correcting the future AMOC by −3.9 sverdrup. Some observed variables may induce almost no change due to a small linear coefficient caused by the ridge regularization or due to a small MMM bias. Fig. 4. Impact of each observable variable of SSS and SST on the constrained estimate of the future AMOC. ( A ) SSS. ( B ) SST. The constraint based on ridge-regularized linear regression corrects the unconstrained estimate of the future AMOC. This correction is decomposed for each of the observable variables and shown here in color. The correction induced by the past AMOC is not shown as it is very small. The exact values are given in the fourth column of table S3. The two most important groups of observable variables are salinity in the South Atlantic and temperature in the subtropical region. The 2005-to-2023 average AMOC has an intermodel correlation of 0.84 with the projected AMOC (2091 to 2100 average), which might explain the positive high β ˆ j coefficient (the highest coefficient compared to other observable variables; see table S3). This strong correlation has the effect to strongly reduce the model uncertainty of the future AMOC by reducing the error of the linear regression. This is in line with Lin et al. ( 50 ), which argues that a good representation of the mean AMOC state is very important to get a good representation of the future AMOC state. However, the multimodel bias of the past AMOC is very small; therefore, the past AMOC has almost no influence on correcting the future AMOC estimate (see table S3). The majority (83%) of the correction to the future AMOC is induced by two groups of variables: (i) the SSS in the two South Atlantic regions (47%) and (ii) the SST in three North Atlantic regions (36%) (see table S3). While a detailed analysis of the processes that could explain our results is beyond the scope of this paper, we can propose some physical interpretations based on the existing literature. (i) As shown in table S3, the South Atlantic SSS exhibits a negative linear coefficient β ˆ for both the Atlantic South and Tropical South regions and a negative multimodel bias. This indicates that the greater the simulated SSS in the South Atlantic over the observed period, the weaker the projected AMOC will be by the end of this century. As the MMM exhibits a strong fresh bias in the South Atlantic, it consequently underestimates the projected AMOC weakening in the future. This result is consistent with van Westen and Dijkstra ( 42 ) and van Westen et al. ( 51 ). van Westen and Dijkstra ( 42 ) state that CMIP6 climate models have too low a South Atlantic SSS, resulting in too weak a salt-advection feedback. van Westen et al. ( 51 ) argue that, due to this underestimation of the salt-advection feedback—defined here as the freshwater transport at 34°S, usually called F OV , as an indicator of AMOC stability—in a number of CMIP6 climate models, there might produce, on average, a too stable AMOC in the projections. The correction induced by the SSS observations, leading to a stronger AMOC weakening, is consistent with this interpretation. (ii) The group of observable variables with the second largest impact on the future AMOC estimate are SSTs in three regions of the North Atlantic: the subtropical region, the subpolar East, and the Nordic Seas. These variables have a negative linear coefficient and a negative multimodel bias. This cold bias is observed in all North Atlantic regions (Subtropical, Labrador, Subpolar East, Nordic Seas, and Tropical North). This negative relationship indicates that climate models with warmer North Atlantic SSTs tend to predict a lower future AMOC. In other words, the negative SST bias in a number of CMIP6 models in the North Atlantic may result in an AMOC that is too stable in response to perturbations. We do not find any simple physical explanation to explain such a relationship, but it may be related to lower sensitivity to changes in surface heat fluxes ( 52 ), which are known to play a big role in AMOC’s response to global warming ( 38 ). There may also be a potential link with northward heat transport, which is a known negative feedback of the AMOC ( 53 , 54 ). However, the physical link between the strength of this feedback and the negative SST bias in the North Atlantic in a number of models is far from straightforward and is left for future analysis. When combining the multimodel bias highlighted from (i) and (ii), it shows that the multimodel ensemble is overestimating the meridional density gradient in the past. This might lead them to overestimate the future gradient, leading to a too strong AMOC in the future. This is therefore constituting another interpretation of why climate models overestimate future AMOC strength, consistently with the results of this article. The remaining 20% of the correction is mainly induced by SSS in the Tropical North, SSS in the Labrador Sea, and SSS in the Eastern Subpolar. Many processes are driving SSS in in the Tropical North SSS region. The bias and regression coefficient may be related to the intertropical convergence zone, upwelling processes (e.g., the Atlantic equatorial cold tongue and vertical diffusion parameterization), how the coastal ocean currents are represented (e.g., the location of the Angola-Benguela front), or equatorial winds. Therefore, it is difficult to identify the cause of this salinity bias and its relationship to the fate of the AMOC. Regarding SSS in the Labrador, the negative coefficient associated with this observable variable is consistent with Jackson et al. ( 55 ), who found a negative intermodel correlation between the SSS in the Labrador Sea and the AMOC trend. As it is explained in ( 55 ), the higher the Labrador SSS, the greater the AMOC weakening, due to stronger deep convection. As shown in table S3, the CMIP6 models underestimate the SSS in the Labrador Sea (negative multimodel bias). Consequently, they also underestimate the AMOC slowdown, which explains the negative correction to the future AMOC. Regarding the SSS in the Eastern Subpolar, the CMIP6 ensemble has a negative bias (being too fresh) and a positive relationship with the future AMOC; the greater the past salinity, the stronger the future AMOC. This may be related to increased deep water formation caused by higher salinity in this region, which stabilizes the AMOC. Overall, our work is consistent with Jackson et al. ( 55 ) and allows the identification of key model biases affecting the estimate of the future AMOC. These biases are important to understand and reduce to refine estimates of the AMOC’s future behavior. Robustness of the results This section examines the sensitivity of different OC methods to the choice of scenario and variables of interest. Two sensitivity tests are performed: changing the socioeconomic scenario used and changing the definition of the observable variable X or the projected variable Y . Sensibility to the scenario Figure S3 shows how sensitive the OC methods are to a change in the emission scenario. When constrained only by the past AMOC for the method using unregularized linear regression, the OC are very similar for all different scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5); see fig. S3 (A, C, E, and G). This is because there is approximately the same intermodel correlation between the past and future AMOC (correlations of 0.86, 0.84, 0.83, and 0.78, respectively). This leads to very similar leave-one-out errors for all different scenarios; see fig. S3 (B, D, F, and H). When comparing all OC methods, the best methods in terms of leave-one-out error are, from the worst to the best, as follows: weighted average, random forest, unregularized linear regression, and ridge-regularized linear regression, for almost all scenarios. However, there is an important exception for the SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0 scenarios. Few climate models are available for these scenarios (18 models in total), so linear regression methods are more sensitive to overfitting when considering multiple observed variables. This can greatly increase the leave-one-out error. This highlights the importance of having as many climate models as possible to correctly constrain a projected variable. Apart from the differences due to the different number of models used per scenario, the results are robust to changes in the anthropogenic emissions scenario (see fig. S3). When constrained by past AMOC, SST, and SSS (see Table 1 ), the additional weakening of future AMOC estimates compared to the unconstrained estimates are −0.8 sverdrup (SSP1-2.6), −3.9 sverdrup (SSP2-4.5), −3.1 sverdrup (SSP3-7.0), and −2.6 sverdrup (SSP5-8.5). These differences are partly due to the different number of climate models used for each scenario. Using the same 17 climate models available for all scenarios, the additional weakening becomes −0.6 sverdrup (SSP1-2.6), −4.6 sverdrup (SSP2-4.5), −3.7 sverdrup (SSP3-7.0), and −4.4 sverdrup (SSP5-8.5). In this case, the differences between scenarios are smaller except for the scenario SSP1-2.6. The remaining differences are due to variations in greenhouse gas and aerosol emissions between the scenarios, which influence the activation of nonlinear feedback from the AMOC or the climate system. This might explain why the SSP1-2.6 scenario is an exception, as the additional weakening is low in any case because these feedback are not activated. The fact that the corrections induced by the OC are very close for SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5 may show that the OC corrects similar biases present in climate models, independently of the scenario. Sensibility to the definition of the variables Here, X and Y are defined using time averages. However, OC methods have also been designed to use emerging trends from anthropogenic forcing in the recent past to evaluate models’ ability to simulate them ( 56 ). Furthermore, some studies have suggested that AMOC trends associated with SST cooling in the subpolar gyre may already be occurring ( 34 ). Consequently, we attempted to replace the time averages by time trends. (i) We conducted a test involving changing the definition of X to use a time trend (over 1975 to 2020 for SST and SSS and 2005 to 2023 for AMOC) instead of a time mean. (ii) We changed how Y is defined, using the mean AMOC anomaly for the period 2091 to 2100 (relative to 1850 to 1900) instead of its absolute value. (iii) We tested changing the time period considered. Figure S4 evaluates the sensitivity of OC methods to changes in the choice of X or Y metric. In both cases, the leave-one-out performance of all OC methods decreases significantly, highlighting the poor added value of the trends for refining future AMOC estimates. This may imply that no robust intermodel relationships can be found between the ongoing observed trends in SSS and SST and the AMOC. (i) The poor leave-one-out performance when using time trends may indicate that the anthropogenic forcing signal on the AMOC is still too weak to identify a robust emergent relationship and is likely obscured by internal variability, consistent with recent publications [e.g., ( 57 )]. (ii) The poor leave-one-out performance when using anomalies rather than absolute values for the future AMOC highlights that the future AMOC anomaly and the absolute value are two distinct variables, as the former has removed the past mean bias in the models, as seen in Fig. 1 . Constraining these variables therefore leads to distinct performances of the OCs. For example, considering anomalies involves treating a model starting at 10 sverdrup—whose reduction cannot be more than 10 sverdrup, corresponding to collapse—the same as a model starting at 30 sverdrup, which can have a weakening of 20 sverdrup (twice larger than the former one), without any full collapse. Therefore, we argue that it makes sense to use absolute values rather than anomalies for the AMOC. (iii) Furthermore, we tested changes in the time period considered using for example a more recent period. Using the period 1980 to 2020 instead of 1900 to 2020 for the SST and SSS means, the constrained estimate of the future AMOC is 8.0 ± 1.4 sverdrup instead of 8.1 ± 1.4 sverdrup, respectively. The results are therefore almost the same. In conclusion, the leave-one-out performance of the OC methods can depend heavily on the definition of the observable and projected variables, highlighting the importance of identifying the most suitable observable variables. DISCUSSION Climate models from the CMIP6 show a declining AMOC by 2100 but with a large uncertainty in the amplitude of this decline. As this paper shows, this uncertainty is mainly due to differences between climate models and, to a much lesser extent, to internal variability and, more unexpectedly, to the emission scenario of greenhouse gasses and aerosols considered. The MMM of the CMIP6 models estimates an AMOC slowdown of 32 ± 37% (90% probability) in the last decade of the century, compared to the 1850–1900 period, for the SSP2-4.5 scenario. To reduce the large model uncertainty, this paper applies OC methods to the projected AMOC. Different OCs based on four state-of-the-art approaches are compared: weighted average, unregularized linear regression, ridge-regularized linear regression, and random forest. In addition, the article compares performance using either one observed variable (the 2005-to-2023 mean AMOC) or a set of 19 observable variables—the latter comprising the 1900-to-2020 mean SST and SSS in nine regions. Using multiple observable variables leads to a greater reduction in model uncertainty than using a single observable variable, due to the gain in information. However, there is a risk of overfitting—learning incorrect relationships between the considered variables—when having a number of climate models of the same magnitude or less than the number of variables. To assess this risk, the leave-one-out metric is used. This evaluates the performance of the methods in accurately predicting future AMOC changes. This article uses ridge-regularized linear regression, which is designed to limit overfitting. Using the 19 observed variables with this method leads to the best (leave-one-out) performance of all four OC methods. This method constrains the SSP2-4.5 AMOC slowdown to 51 ± 8%, providing a more confident and stronger AMOC slowdown than the unconstrained estimate of 32 ± 37%. The article also provides the unconstrained and constrained estimates of the AMOC fate for different emission scenarios in Table 1 . These well-informed estimates are key for updating projections of future climate. According to a recent IPCC report ( 5 ), a slowdown of more than 50% can be called a “substantial weakening of the AMOC.” This substantial weakening could have important implications for future adaptation plans in various regions affected by the AMOC, around the Atlantic and in teleconnected regions. This refinement toward stronger AMOC decline is mainly due to SSS fresh biases in the South Atlantic regions and SST cold biases in the North Atlantic regions in the CMIP6 MMM. This highlights the importance of biases in climate models for their ability to represent future AMOC fate. Therefore, this result encourages the modeling community to focus on reducing these biases. Furthermore, based on this comparison of different OC methods, we recommend using simple methods (e.g., linear regression) when the number of samples (i.e., climate models) is small rather than complex methods (e.g., random forest), which require more samples to be effective. We also recommend using multiple constraining observable variables with appropriate testing methods to estimate the risk of overfitting to obtain robust constrained estimates. Nevertheless, the quantification of constrained model uncertainty used here should be treated with caution. This quantification relies on strong, yet necessary, assumptions for method comparison (summarized in the “Assumption” section of Materials and Methods). However, the conclusions of this article remain robust when the scenario is changed, using SSP1-2.6, SSP3-7.0, or SSP5-8.5 rather than SSP2-4.5. When the definitions of the projected and observable variables are changed, OC methods do not reduce model uncertainty much; therefore, the choice of variables to be constrained is crucial. This does not mean that our main results are not robust with respect to the observable variables used; rather, when variables that do not explain the spread of the future AMOC are used, the improvements in future estimates are very small compared to MMM. Here, we have examined a limited number of observable variables. Others that may provide strong constraints on the projected AMOC are not necessarily included in the CMIP6 climate models, e.g., the melting of Greenland ( 58 , 59 ). An interesting approach would be to scan as many variables and regions as possible rather than choosing the observable variables per se. We suggest using dimensional reduction methods (e.g., principal component regression and clustering) to objectively identify the optimal set of variables that best explain model uncertainty from a very large ensemble of variables. Further research into the underlying processes will also be needed to better explore and validate the processes suggested here, based on existing literature. In this respect, our method provides useful tools for understanding model uncertainty and identifying priority areas for improvement. Overall, the best OC method in terms of leave-one-out error, using the information from multiple observable variables, suggests an AMOC slowdown that is 60% stronger than estimated by the MMM. This could result in significant modifications to the climate change projections for various regions worldwide and introduce additional risks that stakeholders must consider, such as the potential for extensive drying in the Sahel region. MATERIALS AND METHODS Definition of the data The AMOC is defined as the maximum (along the depth) of the volume transport averaged at 26°N latitude. Its strength is measured in sverdrup (1 sverdrup = 10 6 m 3 s – 1 ). The projected variable, denoted Y , is the AMOC averaged over the last decade of this century (2091 to 2100). The observable variable, denoted X , is composed of p variables. This article proposes two cases: the univariate case ( p = 1) and the multivariate case ( p = 19). In the univariate case, X is the AMOC averaged over the period 2005 to 2023. In the multivariate case, X is composed of 19 observable variables: the AMOC averaged in time between 2005 and 2023 (one observable variable) and the SST and SSS averaged in time between 1900 and 2020 and averaged in space over nine different regions (the other 9 × 2 observable variables). These nine regions are defined in fig. S1. These regions are key areas of the Atlantic Ocean ( 11 , 41 , 42 ), in addition to two regions believed to play a role for the AMOC weakening: the Indian Ocean ( 43 ) and the Equatorial Pacific Niño region ( 44 ). The period 1900 to 2020 is the largest available in the observational dataset and was chosen to minimize internal variability and focus primarily on model uncertainty. The observed values of X are obtained from the RAPID array ( 46 ) for the AMOC and from Hadley EN.4.2.2 ( 60 ) for the SSS and SST [g10 bias corrections used ( 61 , 62 )]. EN.4.2.2 data were obtained from www.metoffice.gov.uk/hadobs/en4/ and are British Crown Copyright, Met Office, provided under a Non-Commercial Government Licence ( www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/doc/non-commercial-government-licence/version/2/ ). The simulated values of X and Y are obtained from CMIP6 climate models from the IPSL-ESGF (Pierre Simon Laplace Institute-Earth System Grid Federation) node. Each climate model provides between 1 and 50 members, which are models run with different initial conditions, models physics, or forcing. Here, each climate model is represented by the average of its members to reduce the effect of internal variability as much as possible and better estimate the forced signal. The names of the climate models and the number of members used for each SSP scenario can be found in tables S1 and S2. The article compares the results with the scenarios SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. The scenario SSP1-1.9 is not used in the OCs, as only five climate models are available for both the AMOC, SSS, and SST. The main results of the article are obtained using the SSP2-4.5 emission scenario because this is a median (plausible) scenario that maximizes the number of climate models available. Total variance decomposition Reintges et al. ( 15 ) have applied the total variance decomposition proposed by Hawkins and Sutton ( 3 ) to the projection of the AMOC using climate models from CMIP3 and CMIP5. We used the same approach with CMIP6 to produce Fig. 2 . The method decomposes the total variance T ( t ) into three sources: the model variance MOD ( t ), the scenario variance S ( t ), and the internal variability variance IV , as described in Eq. 1 (1) T ( t ) = MOD ( t ) + S ( t ) + IV Model variance is the variance between the projections from different climate models. Scenario variance is the variance between the projections of the different socioeconomic scenarios. Internal variability represents natural variability and is a constant in this framework. We used the CMIP6 time series from the historical period (1850 to 2014) and the projected period (2015 to 2100) with N S = 4 different SSP scenarios (1-2.6, 2-4.5, 3-7.0, and 5-8.5) and M = 17 climate models available for these scenarios (one member used per model); see tables S1 and S2. For a given climate model m ∈ [ 1 , M ] , scenario s ∈ [ 1 , N S ] , and year t ∈ [ 2000 , 2100 ] , the AMOC A is decomposed as follows (2) A ( m , s , t ) = a ( m , s , t ) + ref ( m , s ) + ε ( m , s , t ) where ref is the reference computed as the 1970-to-2000 AMOC mean, a is the 4° polynomial fitted to the anomalous AMOC ( A − ref ), and ε are the residuals. The model variance MOD ( t ), the scenario variance S ( t ), and the internal variability variance IV are defined as (3) IV = 1 M ∑ m = 1 M V s , t ε ( m , s , t ) (4) MOD ( t ) = 1 N S ∑ s = 1 N S V m a ( m , s , t ) (5) S ( t ) = V s 1 M ∑ m = 1 M a ( m , s , t ) where a and ε are defined in Eq. 2 . V x represents the variance on the dimension x . For example, in Eq. 4 , the variance on climate models m is V m a ( m , s , t ) = 1 M ∑ m = 1 M a ( m , s , t ) − a ( s , t ) ¯ m 2 , with a ( s , t ) ¯ m = 1 M ∑ m = 1 M a ( m , s , t ) . In 2000, the model variance is close to zero because the time series are anomalies compared to 1970 to 2000. The scenario variance before 2015 is zero because the SSP scenarios start in 2015. Both the model variance and the model uncertainty depend on the differences between climate models but have different definitions: One is a variance, while the other is a multiple of the SD (see Eq. 9 ). Notations Here ( X 1 , Y 1 ) , … , ( X M , Y M ) is the sample of M random variables associated with the realizations ( x 1 , y 1 ) , … , ( x M , y M ) , with M as the number of climate models. Here, we use univariate Y and multivariate X : y ∈ ℝ and x ∈ ℝ p , with p > 1 . Bold letters correspond to vectors or matrices, while nonbold letters correspond to scalars. The theoretical mean of X , mean of Y , covariance matrix of X , covariance of Y , and the covariance matrix between X and Y are denoted as μ X , μ Y , Σ X , σ Y , and Σ XY , respectively. These parameters are estimated from the output of climate models. The estimates used here, denoted as μ ˆ X , μ ˆ Y , Σ ˆ X , σ ˆ Y and Σ ˆ XY , are expressed in terms of empirical means, empirical SDs, and empirical covariances, defined here as μ ˆ X = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M x i ∈ ℝ p , μ ˆ Y = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M y i ∈ ℝ , Σ ˆ x = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M ( x i − μ ˆ X ) T ( x i − μ ˆ X ) ∈ ℝ ( p , p ) , σ ˆ Y 2 = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M ( y i − μ ˆ y ) T ∈ ℝ , and Σ ˆ XY = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M ( x i − μ ˆ X ) T ( y i − μ ˆ Y ) ∈ ℝ p . The observed values in the real world of X are noted x 0 . The goal of OC methods is to constrain the estimate and the model uncertainty of Y by the real-world observations x 0 of a related variable X . Comparison of the MMM and OC methods Each method can be expressed as a regression model, which can be described as a function f that relates X to Y with a given error ε (6) Y = f ( X ) + ε In the case of the MMM method (i.e., unconstrained), f does not depend on X ; see the section MMM further in the article. Each method produces a different estimate; the value of Y constrained by the real-world observation x 0 is denoted as f ˆ ( x 0 ) , where f ˆ is the estimate of f . Here, each method is evaluated in two ways: by considering model uncertainty and by calculating the leave-one-out error. The former represents the error around the estimate provided by the method, while the latter represents the method’s ability to correctly predict the projections of climate models, as evaluated against test data. The best method is the one that minimizes this second error and provides the best estimate of future AMOC. Definition of the model uncertainty (unconstrained or constrained) The empirical SD of ε , the method’s error, is estimated here as the root mean square error (7) σ ˆ ε = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M ε ˆ i 2 (8) with ε ˆ i = y i − f ˆ ( x i ) where f ˆ is estimated using all M models. In the literature, the uncertainty associated with a given probability level 1 − α controlled by z is usually expressed as z σ ˆ ε , where z is the Gaussian quantile of order 1 − α of a distribution N ( 0 , 1 ) . For example, z = 2 is often used to describe a 95% probability range (=0.05). This is a user-dependent choice. z = 1.68 gives a 90% probability range, which is used throughout this article. Overall, we use the interval described by Eq. 9 as the estimate f ˆ ( x 0 ) centered around the model uncertainty z σ ˆ ε (9) [ f ˆ ( x 0 ) ± z σ ˆ ε ] This interval is roughly analogous to a statistical probability interval, containing the correct value of f ( x 0 ) with a given probability. The expression of f ˆ and σ ˆ ε change from one method to the other. If the method is the MMM, then the interval from Eq. 9 describes the unconstrained estimate with the unconstrained model uncertainty. If the method is an OC method, it describes a constrained estimate more or less a constrained model uncertainty. The expressions of f ˆ ( x 0 ) and z σ ˆ ε are given below for the unconstrained case and for each OC method where possible. Equation 9 does not rigorously represent a probability interval. A more rigorous approach would be to use a confidence or prediction interval, as described in detail in ( 63 ). This takes into account the uncertainty on the estimators f ˆ ( x 0 ) and σ ˆ ε , depending on the number of climate model used. The (nonrigorous) interval obtained using Eq. 9 is, for example, for the MMM method, [ μ ˆ Y ± z σ ˆ Y ] , whereas for the same example, the more statistically rigorous confidence interval is [ μ ˆ Y ± t M − 1 σ ˆ Y 1 + 1 M ] , where t M − 1 is the student quantile with M – 1 df controlling the confidence and M is the number of climate models considered. This interval describes a higher level of model uncertainty because it takes into account the quality of the estimates, which depends on the data size M . However, we cannot use confidence intervals here because we do not have their expressions for methods other than the MMM and unregularized linear regression. Consequently, the model uncertainty is expressed by z σ ˆ ε throughout this paper. Leave-one-out error definition Cross-validation is a useful tool frequently used in the literature to evaluate a method’s ability to fit data ( 24 , 26 , 64 ). Because of the limited number of samples (32 climate models), a leave-one-out cross-validation was chosen. This approach is often referred to as the “perfect model framework” approach, e.g., ( 64 ). From the M climate model realizations ( x 1 , y 1 ) , … , ( x M , y M ) , one climate model i is isolated and used as a reference (the perfect model). The regression of the OC method is learned ( f is learned) on the M – 1 others and tested on the isolated model i to compute the absolute error between the future projection estimated by the OC method and the “true” value from the isolated model. The leave-one-out error, written ε ˆ LOO , is the root mean square error for all possible combinations of isolated model (10) ε ˆ LOO = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M y i − f ˆ i ( x i ) 2 where f ˆ i is f estimated using all models except the i th. If this error is low, the method correctly predicted most of the isolated models used as a test. If this error is high, the method may have overfitted the rest of the data. This approach treats climate models as independent and equally plausible. Assumption The expression of the model uncertainty lies on different assumptions. As can be seen from Eq. 7 , climate models are used, as they are independent and equally plausible. While these two assumptions are contestable, they are often used by almost all OC methods. Only some methods based on a weighted average approach reject these assumptions. However, to compare all OC methods, we use the same uncertainty quantification for all of them, where all climate models have the same weight, and methods have the same definition of the uncertainty; see Eq. 7 . As can be seen from Eq. 6 , the OC method learns a regression function from climate models and uses it with real-world observations to obtain a constrained estimate of the projected variable. This assumes that the ensemble of climate models accurately captures the relationships between variables. However, as mentioned in ( 33 ), the relationships between variables in an ensemble of climate models can be wrong due to shared errors on the simulation of some processes. This is why the relationships should be validated from a physical point of view, as we attempted to do in the current article. However, the constrained model uncertainty does not take into account observation error. We chose to neglect this for three main reasons. First, for some OC methods, for example, those based on nonlinear regression, it is not possible to obtain the constrained model uncertainty taking into account the observational error without tricks. For example, Schlund et al. ( 24 ) fitted a linear statistical model to their nonlinear statistical model locally to account for observational noise. However, this is an approximation and may not work for other OC methods (e.g., weighted average). Second, integrating the observation error into the constrained model uncertainty calculation requires knowledge of the variance-covariance matrix of the observation error, i.e., knowing the variance of the observation error of each variable in X and the covariance of the error between each variable in X . This can be difficult to obtain without making strong assumptions that lead to an underestimation of the error. For instance, consider the North Atlantic SST averaged between 1900 and 2020. While the observational database can provide a measurement error for each grid point within this region and for each year, determining the error of the overall spatiotemporal average requires strong assumptions about spatiotemporal dependencies. This can lead to a substantial underestimation of observational noise. Furthermore, observational error comprises not only measurement error but also internal variability, the estimation of which is a subject of much debate. Third, observational error is often very small compared to intermodel dispersion, i.e., there is a high signal-to-noise ratio. Therefore, we argue that the observational noise can be neglected in this case. Multimodel mean To predict the future climate from a multimodel ensemble of projections, a first approach is to define f as the MMM of the ensemble of M climate model projections (11) f ˆ ( x 0 ) = 1 M ∑ i = 1 M y i = μ ˆ Y This method is not an OC method, as it does not depend on the observation x 0 . It is the most basic approach used as a reference. Using Eq. 7 , the unconstrained model uncertainty is consequently defined as (12) z σ ˆ ε = z 1 M ∑ i = 1 M ( y i − μ ˆ Y ) 2 = z σ ˆ Y The estimate [ f ˆ ( x 0 ) ] and its uncertainty ( z σ ˆ ε ) are thus directly express as the empirical mean and z times the SD of Y . Weighted average approach The weighted average OC method predicts the future variable Y by averaging the multimodel ensemble of climate model projections, with a different weight w i for each climate model i (13) f ˆ ( x 0 ) = 1 ∑ i = 1 M w ˆ i ∑ i = 1 M w ˆ i Y i One special case of this approach is model selection [e.g., ( 64 , 65 )], which is equivalent to assigning a weight of zero to certain models. Unlike the other approaches used here, the weighted average approach assumes that climate models are not equally plausible. Moreover, Brunner et al. ( 18 ) or Giorgi and Mearns ( 19 ) also assume that climate models are dependent. On the one hand, Brunner et al. ( 18 ) developed a method based on performance and independence, where a climate model has a high weight if its values are close to the observations (performance) and far from the other climate models (independence). On the other hand, Giorgi and Mearns ( 19 ) also favors performance but favors dependence by assuming that similarities between climate model results represent reliable convergence. Nevertheless, both articles define the weights empirically. We use here the weight expression proposed in Brunner et al. ( 18 ) (14) w ˆ i = exp − ( D i / σ D ) 2 Σ j = 1 M exp − ( S i j / σ S ) 2 with D i 2 = ‖ x i − x 0 ‖ 2 and S ij 2 = ‖ x i − x j ‖ 2 . σ D ∈ ℝ and σ S ∈ ℝ are two parameters. They are calibrated as in ( 18 ): A leave-one-out error is calculated for each pair of values ( σ D , σ S ) in the range 20 to 200% of the median of D i and S i , and we keep the pair of parameters that minimizes this error. The expression of the constrained model uncertainty, z σ ˆ ε , can be computed using the previous definition of σ ˆ ε ; see Eq. 7 . Linear regression The linear regression approach is often used in the literature on OCs ( 21 , 23 ). Multivariate linear regression can be written as (15) Y = E [ Y ∣ X ] + ε , with E [ Y ∣ X ] = β 0 + β T X where β ∈ ℝ p represents the coefficients assigned to each of the p observable variables of X and β 0 ∈ ℝ represents the intercept. As shown in supplementary proof 1, Eq. 15 can be written as Y = μ Y − β T ( μ X − X ) + ε . Thus, the function described by Eq. 16 can be written as (16) f ( X ) = μ Y − β T ( μ X − X ) Applying this equation to the real-world observation x 0 through f ˆ , the estimation of f , the estimate of linear regression using the observation x 0 can be expressed as (17) f ˆ ( x 0 ) = μ ˆ Y − β ˆ T ( μ ˆ X − x 0 ) Unregularized linear regression In the case of the unregularized linear regression, which is the most commonly used, the coefficients are defined as β ˆ T = Σ ˆ YX Σ ˆ X − 1 , where Σ ˆ YX and Σ ˆ X are the empirical covariance between Y and X and the empirical variance-covariance matrix of X . In this case, the constrained estimate is (18) f ˆ ( x 0 ) = μ ˆ Y − Σ ˆ YX Σ ˆ X − 1 ( μ ˆ X − x 0 ) Equation 18 shows that the constrained estimate, f ˆ ( x 0 ) , is the unconstrained estimate, μ ˆ Y , to which a term is added. This term corrects the unconstrained estimate. The correction depends on the strength of the relationship between X and Y and also on the difference between the MMM and the real-world observation, ( μ ˆ X − x 0 ) , which can be interpreted as a “multimodel bias.” Using Eq. 7 , the constrained model uncertainty can be written (not shown) as (19) z σ ˆ ε = z σ ˆ Y 2 − Σ ˆ YX Σ ˆ X − 1 Σ ˆ XY Equation 19 shows that the constrained model uncertainty is always smaller than the unconstrained model uncertainty, σ ˆ Y 2 . The reduction of the model uncertainty depends in particular on the strength of the relation between X and Y . Ridge-regularized linear regression Multiple linear regression can have the risk of overfitting, a known risk when having a number of samples (climate models) of the same magnitude or less than the number of variables. To limit overfitting, some linear regression methods use regularization. Here, we use ridge-regularized linear regression. Many other approaches have been tested (Lasso, ElasticNet, orthogonal matching pursuit, etc.). However, only ridge is shown in the article because it provides the best cross-validation performance. It is also better suited to our case because the observable variables are strongly correlated. This regularization is well known and is used to limit overfitting by limiting the amplitude of the coefficients of the observable variable (the value of ‖ β ‖ 2 2 ). Here, the ridge-regularized linear regression parameter that controls the strength of the regularization is automatically chosen as the parameter that minimizes the leave-one-out on a set of parameter values. In addition, the observable variables X are standardized in ridge-regularized linear regression. Both unregularized linear regression and ridge-regularized linear regression are used in the comparison between OC methods. Compared to the unregularized linear regression of the previous section, this method has a different expression of β ˆ and consequently a different constrained estimate f ˆ ( x 0 ) and constrained model uncertainty z σ ˆ ε , defined respectively in Eqs. 18 and 7 . Random forest Recently, nonlinear OC methods have emerged, mostly based on random forest regression ( 24 – 26 ). While they can learn more complex relationships, this comes at a cost: They require a large number of samples for training. To illustrate this type of nonlinear regression method, we use a random forest regressor comprising 500 decision trees, with no size limit on the decision trees and with the square root of the number of observable variables considered at each split. The constrained estimate f ˆ ( x 0 ) and constrained model uncertainty z σ ˆ ε cannot be analytically expressed here, but just like the other OC methods, they are respectively expressed as the method prediction of x 0 and z times its error expressed in Eq. 7 . Conditional distribution approach Ribes et al. ( 27 ) and Bowman et al. ( 28 ) propose methods that estimate the distribution of Y as its conditional distribution given the observation. Ribes et al. ( 27 ) developed a univariate method to constrain the estimate and the model uncertainty of the future value of a variable, while Bowman et al. ( 28 ) developed a multivariate method to constrain the entire time series of a climate variable. By adapting them to constrain the estimate and the model uncertainty parameters of a univariate future variable Y using a multivariate observable variable X , they both produce the same estimate of the distribution parameters of Y . Furthermore, if observational noise is neglected, as it is here, both Ribes et al. ( 27 ) and Bowman et al. ( 28 ) give the same constrained estimate ( Eq. 18 ) and constrained model uncertainty ( Eq. 19 ) as the unregularized linear regression method; see equation 12 in ( 27 ) and see equation 4 in the supplementary of Bowman et al. ( 28 ). Consequently, this method is represented by the unregularized linear regression method here. Acknowledgments We would like to thank J. Mignot, G. McCarthy, E. Eresanya, L. Borchert, B.
