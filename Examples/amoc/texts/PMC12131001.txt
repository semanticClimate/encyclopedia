Since the 1950s, the TP has undergone accelerated warming, leading to profound changes in its hydrological and ecological systems as well as regional dust activities. Currently, over 60% of the TP is classified as arid or semiarid. This harsh environment, characterized by loose surface sediments, sparse vegetation, and strong winds, makes the TP a major contributor to the global dust cycle. Dust emitted from the TP not only impacts local societies and economies but also exerts complex feedbacks on climates, including influences on nutrient cycling, surface albedo, radiative forcing, and cloud formation. 1 Meanwhile, dust plays a key role in maintaining soil fertility and buffering capacity, forming the foundation for alpine ecosystems. 2 Given these significant interactions, understanding the variability of dust activity over the TP and its driving mechanisms is both critical and urgent. Temperature regulates dust activities over the TP on multiple timescales Modern observations indicate that dust activity in the arid or semiarid regions primarily occurs during cold seasons, such as winter and spring, and is closely linked to near-surface wind patterns. During winter, air temperature over the TP drops more significantly than in surrounding areas at similar altitudes. This creates descending air masses over the TP, driving cool, dry air flows outward from the plateau. Modern near-surface (10 m) wind field observations from 1979 and 1998 support this phenomenon. 3 These temperature-induced gradient winds are a primary driver of dust activity on the TP. Since the 1970s, dust activity on the TP has declined in response to the increasingly positive phase of the winter Arctic oscillation (AO) or North Atlantic oscillation (NAO), reflecting that the decadal-timescale (10 1 years) variability of dust activity is related to these climatic indices. When the AO/NAO is in a negative phase, the Azores high decreases, while the Icelandic low increases, leading to lower atmospheric pressure in middle latitudes and higher pressure in high latitudes. This pattern facilitates the transport of cold air from high to middle latitudes via the Westerlies, causing air temperatures to drop across most middle latitudes. The resulting air temperature drop intensifies near-surface winds, amplifying dust activity over the TP ( Figure 1 ). Over the centennial-to-millennial timescale (10 2 –10 3 years), geological records reveal that increased dust activity across the TP aligns with the periods of a weakened Atlantic meridional overturning current (AMOC). The weakening of the AMOC occurs abruptly, with conditions persisting for hundreds to thousands of years. A slowdown in the AMOC reduces poleward oceanic heat transport, leading to a cooler North Atlantic sea surface. This cooling propagates across the Northern Hemisphere via thermal advection, lowering air temperature and strengthening near-surface winds, thereby enhancing dust activity over the TP ( Figure 1 ). It is noteworthy that recent AMOC weakening, attributed to Greenland ice sheet melting, operates on a decadal timescale and may merely mitigate Arctic warming rather than dominate changes in Northern Hemisphere winter temperatures and dust activities over the TP. Over the orbital timescale (10 4 years), the long-term trend of dust activity across the TP corresponds to variations in Northern Hemisphere summer insolation (NHSI), mainly modulated by Earth’s orbital precession. 3 Higher dust activity typically coincides with lower NHSI and larger precession. During large precession phases, the Northern Hemisphere summer solstice occurs at aphelion and the winter solstice at perihelion, as is the case today. In contrast, during smaller precession phases (e.g., ∼10 4 years ago), the summer solstice occurred at perihelion and the winter solstice at aphelion. Larger precession values result in lower NHSI and higher Northern Hemisphere winter insolation (NHWI), although Earth’s orbital obliquity plays a minor role. However, winter temperatures may not respond linearly to NHWI, as predicted by the transient climate simulation of the last 21,000 years. 4 Given much higher NHSI compared to NHWI, especially at high latitudes, NHSI plays a dominant role due to the “summer remnant effect”, whereby Arctic sea ice loss in summer, driven by high NHSI, persists into winter and increases winter temperatures. In addition to Arctic sea ice, feedbacks from dust and vegetation also significantly influence winter temperatures. In summary, smaller precession values elevate NHSI, accelerating Arctic sea ice melt and amplifying both summer and winter warming in the Northern Hemisphere. This has resulted in weakened near-surface winds and reduced dust activities over the TP ( Figure 1 ). Although dust activity over the TP since the last interglacial period has generally followed the trend of NHSI over the orbital timescale, the magnitude of dust activity does not always correspond to NHSI. For instance, during marine isotope stage (MIS) 3 (∼57–30 thousand years ago [ka]), dust activity was higher than during MIS 4 (∼75–57 ka), despite higher NHSI in MIS 3. 3 This discrepancy may be attributed to extensive glacial advances during MIS 3, which increased surface albedo and intensified glacial anticyclones, strengthening near-surface winds and promoting dust activity. 3 During the Holocene (∼12 ka to present), dust activity was lower in the middle Holocene compared with the early and late Holocene. 5 This pattern differs from the gradually decreasing NHSI trend. Evidence suggests that glacier advance increased slightly during the early Holocene, decreased during the middle Holocene, and intensified substantially during the late Holocene. 5 Besides residual ice sheet, heightened glacier activity during the early Holocene likely contributed to high dust activity, despite high NHSI levels. Summary and prospect Dust activity over the TP primarily responds to global climate change across multiple timescales. Mountain glaciers on the TP modulate the amplitude of dust activity’s orbital timescale response to these changes. Noteworthily, despite growing recognition of the importance of understanding the drivers and climatic effects of TP dust activity, existing records remain limited. Observational stations for dust events, especially in the western TP, are sparse. Geological records of dust activity in the northern TP predominantly cover the Holocene, while records from the southern TP often lack recent segments due to the region’s complex sedimentary environments. Expanding the temporal and spatial coverage of dust observation records is, therefore, crucial for comprehending the long-term evolution of dust activity. Decreases in winter temperatures have driven increased dust activity across multiple timescales. However, due to the lack of seasonal (particularly winter) temperature reconstructions during the Holocene, there remains a significant gap between simulated and reconstructed temperature trends. This discrepancy, known as the “Holocene temperature conundrum”, 4 highlights the challenges in reconciling proxy and model data. Given the close relationship between TP dust activity and winter temperature over multiple timescales, studying dust activity offers a promising avenue for addressing this conundrum. Recent studies have shown that enhanced dust levels have reduced global mean effective radiative forcing since preindustrial times, partly counteracting global warming. 1 Although dust is incorporated as a module in present climate models, its effects are often excluded from simulations due to high uncertainties surrounding its radiative interactions, particularly with clouds. Further research is needed to better constrain the radiative effects of dust on climate and improve the representation of dust in climate models, providing additional insights into the “Holocene temperature conundrum”. Moreover, further attention should also be paid to the ecological and environmental effects of dust, as these exogenous fine particles not only supply nutrients and provide source materials for soil formation but also enhance ecosystem services ( Figure 1 ). Looking ahead, we speculate that dust activity over the TP will continue to decline as global warming intensifies. However, under carbon-neutral conditions, dust activity may increase in response to decreases in atmospheric CO 2 and winter temperature.
