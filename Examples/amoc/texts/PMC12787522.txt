While previous studies have reported an increase in future fire weather under global warming scenarios, there is still a limited understanding on how it may evolve under mitigation scenarios. Using idealized emission-driven climate simulations, this analysis examines changes in the extreme fire weather conditions under net-zero emission (ZeroE) and net-negative emission (NegE) scenarios. Compared to peak CO 2 levels, the NegE scenario achieves substantially greater fire danger mitigation, particularly in climatologically high-fire-danger regions, because of lower temperatures and increased relative humidity. In contrast, the ZeroE scenario leaves fire danger elevated globally, especially in Northern low-latitude regions. Regional responses are shaped by differences in atmospheric moisture supply, influenced by changes in the strength of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation and the latitudinal position of the intertropical convergence zone. These findings highlight the crucial role of aggressive CO 2 removal strategies in mitigating fire weather dangers and underscore the limitations of ZeroEs alone in addressing global wildfire challenges. In recent decades, the frequency and magnitude of wildfires have increased, driven by climate change ( 1 ). Record-breaking wildfires have occurred across the globe, affecting regions such as Europe (EU), Australia, North America, and South America ( 2 – 6 ). Although the combination of several effects, including human activities and land-use factors, has contributed to a recent decline in global burned area ( 7 ), climate-induced changes have increasingly promoted fire-conducive conditions, leading to an increase in climate-attributed burned area in recent decades ( 8 ). The key fire weather conditions such as high temperatures, low humidity, weak to no precipitation, and strong winds exacerbate fire danger. Temperature increase, relative humidity/precipitation decrease (drying), and strong wind speeds jointly influence the flammability of fuels and the spread, intensity, and ignition potential of wildfires ( 9 ). Intensification of fire weather conditions under climate change has led to a global increase in the frequency of severe wildfires ( 10 – 13 ). Fire weather conditions (i.e., hot, dry, and windy) and wildfire likelihood are projected to increase substantially across various regions worldwide, including areas historically experiencing fewer fires ( 14 ). Rising temperatures and changes in relative humidity, precipitation, and wind speed are expected to increase the frequency and severity of extreme fire weather events by the late 21st century, possibly making fires longer-lasting, more severe, and more widespread, consequently posing greater dangers to natural and human systems ( 15 – 18 ). These studies indicate that limiting global warming to below 2°C above preindustrial levels could markedly reduce fire weather dangers compared to a scenario with warming above 3°C. Therefore, the global warming mitigation efforts are crucial to prevent worsening fire dangers and consequent risks ( 19 – 21 ). To effectively limit global warming to 2°C, and preferably to 1.5°C, it is crucial not only to reduce CO 2 emissions but also to actively remove CO 2 from the atmosphere. Negative emission scenarios involving carbon dioxide removal (CDR) will help lower atmospheric CO 2 concentrations more effectively. The recent studies, which evaluated the climate system’s response in the CDR scenario, argued that even if atmospheric CO 2 levels rapidly decrease to preindustrial/present levels, the climate systems cannot recover immediately or even exhibit irreversible behaviors ( 22 – 29 ). Because of the ocean’s large thermal inertia, surface temperatures, especially over the Southern Ocean, remain high even after returning to the initial CO 2 concentration levels, and a delayed response of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) causes a strong hysteresis in deep ocean circulation. Their consequence appeared in the position change of the intertropical convergence zone (ITCZ), which stayed south of its original location for a long while after CO 2 levels recovered ( 30 ). These pivotal climate elements expand their impacts on the regional climate phenomena, and thus, the irreversible changes are also evident in climate characteristics such as surface temperature, precipitation, humidity, and sea surface temperature, varying according to geographic location and specific variables [e.g., ( 31 )]. Accordingly, the fire weather condition, which is generally governed by regional climate change, is also expected to show hysteretic behavior. A representative study by Kim et al. ( 32 ) demonstrated that global fire danger exhibits hysteresis under the CDR scenario, characterized by an increase in the Northern Hemisphere and a decrease in the Southern Hemisphere, when comparing the doubled CO 2 period during the CO 2 ramp-up and ramp-down phases. This hysteresis in fire danger is primarily influenced by relative humidity and is attributed to atmospheric moisture redistribution resulting from the southward shift of the ITCZ. Considering the perceived hysteresis in fire danger, it is crucial to understand how it responds to net-zero CO 2 emissions, given that achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement essentially first required reaching net-zero CO 2 emissions. Thereafter, more robust climate change mitigation requires the aggressive removal of CO 2 , referred to as net-negative emissions (NegEs). To enhance the effectiveness of climate change mitigation policies and to develop long-term climate stabilization strategies, it is important to understand and cross-compare the effects of various CO 2 -emission reduction scenarios, including net-zero emissions (ZeroEs) and NegEs under parallel but diverging and more realistic pathways. Furthermore, examination of extreme (i.e., exceptionally strong) fire weathers that may cause devastating damage is particularly needed. However, studies that analyze the response of extreme fire weathers to CO 2 reduction scenarios, including net-zero and net-negative CO 2 emissions are currently lacking. In addition, it is essential to identify the regional variations in the response of extreme fire weathers to different CO 2 emission reduction scenarios and understand the mechanisms driving these differences. To address this limitation and enhance our understanding, in this study, we investigated the response of mean and extreme fire weather to CO 2 reduction policies using Community Earth System Model 2 (CESM2) experiments focused on ZeroE and NegE scenarios. Figure S1 shows the imposed CO 2 emissions and the simulated atmospheric CO 2 concentrations in the scenarios. We used the fire weather index (FWI) ( 33 ), which is one of the most widely applied fire potential indices [e.g., global fire forecasting model of the ECMWF (European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts)] and one of the most effective indices in representing actual fire danger across tropical and extratropical regions ( 34 ). This study examined changes in global fire weather under varying CO 2 reduction scenarios by analyzing the responses of extreme fire weather and evaluating the contributions of meteorological factors, such as temperature and relative humidity. It also investigated the mechanisms driving regional differences in these responses. From this perspective, the use of FWI throughout this study is considered appropriate. RESULTS Response of fire weather to the CO 2 pathway The annual mean FWI for the current climate (P0, 2001 to 2031; see Materials and Methods), as simulated by the CESM2 model, varied substantially across different regions of the world ( Fig. 1A ). Regions such as Australia, Sub-Saharan Africa (SSAF), and parts of South America exhibit very high FWI values ranging between 50 and 80, indicating extreme fire danger. These areas are characterized by warmer and drier climates, making them prone to frequent and intense wildfire activity. Central Asia, the western United States (USA), and eastern South America (ESAM) show FWI values ranging from 30 to 50, reflecting high fire danger. In contrast, regions such as Northern Eurasia, Northern North America, and East Asia display lower FWI values, indicating moderate to low fire danger. Fig. 1. Spatial distribution of annual mean FWI. ( A ) Ensemble-mean annual mean FWI based on CESM2 during the current period (P0, 2001 to 2031) and ( B ) annual mean FWI from ERA5 (2001 to 2024), with hatching indicating the fire frequency based on GFED5 (2001 to 2022). The CESM2 simulation for the current climate tends to overestimate FWI compared to ECMWF Reanalysis version 5 (ERA5) reanalysis data ( Fig. 1 ). This overestimation is particularly evident in wildfire-prone regions, such as Africa, inland South America, and Australia. In addition, CESM2 shows a tendency to overestimate FWI values in some mid-latitude regions of the Northern Hemisphere, including parts of Europe and Central Asia, where observed fire danger is relatively low. Despite these biases, CESM2 successfully represents the overall geographical patterns of FWI distribution. This study analyzed the relative changes in fire weather under various CO 2 scenarios compared to the current climate, focusing on scenario-based differences to minimize the influence of biases in absolute values. A significant increase in the global mean FWI is observed from P0 to 2107 (the year of peak atmospheric CO 2 ), aligning with rising CO 2 concentrations ( Fig. 2A ). In the current climate (P0), the global mean FWI is 18.8 and progressively increases by +1.2 and +2.5 by P1 (2039 to 2069) and P2 (2092 to 2122), respectively. The increase from P0 to P1, and subsequently from P1 to P2, shows a linear rate of change; therefore, the results for P1 are omitted in subsequent analyses. Under the NegE scenario, as CO 2 concentrations decline and reach levels similar to the 2000s by N4, the FWI decreases, showing an increase of only +0.3 compared to P0. After N4, the FWI increases slightly, approaching a quasiequilibrium state of N7. When CO 2 emissions reach net zero at Z4 under the ZeroE scenario, the global mean FWI remains 1.8 higher than the current climate, indicating a persistent increase in fire danger despite achieving ZeroEs. The global mean FWI responds linearly to the global mean daily maximum temperature but shows a modest asymmetry between the CO 2 ramp-up and ramp-down phases: FWI increases slightly more per degree of warming from P0 to P2 than it decreases during the subsequent periods (from P2 to N4/Z4) (fig. S2). Fig. 2. Temporal and spatial changes in FWI. ( A ) Time series of global mean FWI and ( B ) daily maximum temperature (Tmax) from 2001 to 2400. ( C to H ) Spatial distributions of changes in annual mean FWI relative to the current period (P0). To examine regional patterns, we analyzed the distribution of FWI changes for time slices compared to the present (P0). With increasing CO 2 concentrations (from P0 to P2), most regions globally, excluding South America, experience a significant increase in fire danger ( Fig. 2, C and D ). The most pronounced increases are observed in regions already characterized by high fire danger. Some regional discrepancies from Kim et al. ( 32 ) are found for the P2-to-P0 changes, particularly in the Sahel and South America. These differences likely arise from both model and methodological factors. While the previous study used CESM1, this study is based on CESM2, which exhibits a more pronounced weakening of the AMOC signal (i.e., a more pronounced warming hole over the northern North Atlantic) under elevated CO 2 (fig. S3). This greater AMOC weakening amplifies the hemispheric temperature contrast between the Northern and Southern Hemispheres, leading to a more pronounced southward shift of the ITCZ. In addition, CESM2 exhibits stronger ocean warming in the Southern Hemisphere. These changes affect precipitation and near-surface relative humidity patterns, contributing to the regional contrasts in FWI changes. In addition, their analysis focused on the peak 3-month fire season, whereas this study considers annual mean FWI, which may also account for the differences, particularly in mid-latitude South America where the fire season is not well defined. Under the NegE scenario, when CO 2 concentrations return to present-day levels (N4; Fig. 2E ), fire danger notably decreases in North and South America compared to the present. However, regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa, India, and Central America (CAM) exhibit higher FWI values than current levels. By the quasiequilibrium state (N7; Fig. 2F ), regional variance decreases compared to the N4 period ( Fig. 2E ). This indicates that the asymmetric response of FWI to CO 2 is not uniform across the world, and this FWI hysteresis is consistent with the previous study using CESM1.2 ( 32 ). In the ZeroE scenario, even after achieving ZeroEs (Z4; Fig. 2G ), most regions, excluding parts of South America and the central United States, maintain elevated FWI compared to the present, persisting through Z7 ( Fig. 2H ). Notably, for Africa, India, and Central America, FWI increases compared to the P2 period despite achieving ZeroEs. According to the NegE scenario, FWI increases are mitigated in most regions. However, in low-latitude areas of the Northern Hemisphere, Southern Africa (SAF), and Australia, FWI is projected to remain higher than present levels, even with aggressive CO 2 reduction efforts. In the ZeroE scenario, FWI persists across most regions, indicating that achieving net-zero CO 2 emissions alone may not be sufficient to fully mitigate fire danger. To compare the response of fire weather under CO 2 emission reduction scenarios after reaching peak CO 2 concentrations (P2), the differences between NegE and ZeroE scenarios were analyzed. Examining regional differences in response to the emission reduction scenarios reveals that effective NegEs, maintaining CO 2 levels at those of the 2000s, lead to a decrease in FWI in many areas such as Africa, India, Central America, Europe, and East Asia compared to the ZeroE scenario ( Fig. 3A ). This mitigation effect is particularly pronounced in regions where FWI has significantly increased because of elevated CO 2 concentrations, emphasizing that areas most affected by CO 2 -driven increases in FWI experience the greatest benefits from NegEs. In South America and central North America, FWI appears higher under the NegE scenario compared to the ZeroE scenario. However, this reflects the fact that FWI decreases relative to the present in both scenarios, with a larger decrease observed under the ZeroE scenario. The regional differences in FWI responses between the two scenarios are more pronounced during the quasi-equilibrium state (N7/Z7 period) than during the N4/Z4 period when the CO 2 concentration differences between the scenarios are the largest ( Fig. 3B ). This suggests that achieving effective CO 2 removal through NegEs can mitigate fire dangers and that the impact becomes more pronounced when CO 2 emissions are maintained at net zero over the long term. Response of extreme fire weather danger to the CO 2 pathway To analyze the response of extreme fire weather to CO 2 emission reduction scenarios, we examined the frequency of extreme fire weather indicated by FWI 95d and the intensity of extreme fire weather represented by FWI max (definitions are described in Materials and Methods). The FWI 95d is currently about 18 days but is projected to exceed 31 days globally during the P2 period, making it 1.7 times the current level ( Fig. 4A ). This result is similar to previous studies based on CMIP6 models, which reported that FWI 95d would increase by an average of 1.8 times at a global warming level of 3.0°C compared to the present ( 35 ). According to the NegE scenario, as CO 2 concentrations decrease, the frequency of extreme days is significantly reduced to ~20 days by N4, followed by a slight increase toward N7. In contrast, under the ZeroE scenario, while the frequency decreases slightly after reaching ZeroEs (Z4), it remains significantly higher than the present level (28 days). Specifically, only 15% of the increase from the present to the P2 period is mitigated under the ZeroE scenario, while the NegE scenario achieves more than 70% mitigation of this increase. Fig. 4. Temporal and spatial changes in extreme FWI. Time series of ( A ) FWI 95d and ( B ) FWI max , ( C to F ) spatial changes relative to the current period (P0), and ( G and H ) differences between NegE and ZeroE experiments. (Dots denote strong consistency among ensemble members, indicating that at least five of the six members showed the same trend as the ensemble mean.) The response of FWI max showed a pattern comparable to FWI 95d under CO 2 reduction scenarios. Now, the global-mean FWI max is about 68 (see the P0 period in Fig. 4B ), and it is projected to increase by +7 (about 10%) during the P2 period ( Fig. 4B ). The extreme intensity during the P2 period also showed a similar increase to the projected increase (9%) at a global warming level of 3.0°C compared to the present ( 35 ). According to the NegE scenario, the maximum intensity decreases back to the current level by N4, effectively eliminating the increase observed in P2. By N7, the intensity shows a slight increase but remains close to the current level. Under the ZeroE scenario, however, mitigation is limited, with the FWI max showing a slight reduction after reaching ZeroEs (Z4) but remaining at levels like the P2 period by Z7. The analysis suggests that while the NegE scenario mitigates more than 70% of the strengthening in extreme fire weather intensity, the ZeroE scenario achieves less than 5% mitigation, highlighting the limitations of ZeroEs alone in addressing the maximum intensity of extreme fire dangers. The spatial distributions of changes in extreme fire weathers demonstrate distinct regional differences ( Fig. 4, C to F ). Even under the ZeroE scenario, the frequency and intensity of extreme fire dangers show significant increases compared to the present across most regions globally, except for parts of South America and central North America. These increases are particularly pronounced in low-latitude regions of the Northern Hemisphere, which already experience high fire danger. However, under the NegE scenario, the increases in the frequency and intensity of extreme fire dangers in these regions are substantially mitigated compared to the ZeroE scenario ( Fig. 4, G and H ). While the degree of mitigation varies between scenarios, both scenarios display similar regional response patterns for the frequency and intensity of extreme fire dangers, closely aligning with the spatial patterns observed for mean FWI. To compare regional responses in extreme fire dangers, we analyzed the annual and seasonal responses of extreme frequency and intensity across eight regions (fig. S4). In most regions, both the frequency and intensity of extreme fire dangers increase during the P2 period compared to the current period (P0) ( Fig. 5 ). Under the NegE scenario, the N7 period demonstrates significant mitigation effects. However, the ZeroE scenario during the Z7 period exhibits regional differences. In regions such as Europe, the United States, and eastern South America, the ZeroE scenario alone is effective in reducing extreme fire dangers compared to the P2 period. Conversely, in low-latitude regions of the Northern Hemisphere, including South Asia (SAS), Sub-Saharan Africa, and Central America, the frequency and intensity of extreme fire dangers are projected to increase during the Z7 period, exceeding even the levels observed during the P2 period of peak CO 2 concentration. Each region has a distinct primary wildfire season, and the responses of extreme fire weathers during these key seasons are found to be similar to the patterns observed on an annual scale ( 36 ). These results demonstrate that while net-zero CO 2 emissions can reduce extreme fire weathers in some regions, they fail to address the heightened dangers in low-latitude regions of the Northern Hemisphere. This highlights the limitations of ZeroEs alone in mitigating global wildfire dangers and underscores the need for more comprehensive strategies to address regional disparities effectively. Fig. 5. Contribution of meteorological variables to fire weather responses To understand the contribution of changes in meteorological variables to FWI under different CO 2 emission scenarios, we analyzed the changes in FWI corresponding to variations in these variables under both NegE and ZeroE scenarios. Figure 6 illustrates the impact of differences in meteorological variables between the two scenarios on FWI changes. Compared to the ZeroE scenario, the mitigation of temperature increases under the NegE scenario significantly reduces temperature-induced fire danger across all regions ( Fig. 6A ). Unlike temperature, other variables exhibit regional differences ( Fig. 6, B to D ). In particular, among the four meteorological variables, relative humidity change–induced FWI ( Fig. 6B ) responses show the highest spatial correlation with changes in the mean FWI ( Fig. 3B ). In the NegE scenario, compared to the ZeroE scenario, fire danger in the tropical regions of the Northern Hemisphere, Europe, and Southern Africa is mitigated by changes in relative humidity. In contrast, fire danger increases in regions like Siberia and parts of North America because of relative humidity changes. In particular, in South America, where fire danger is projected to decrease under all CO 2 emission reduction scenarios relative to the present ( Fig. 2, F to H ), the changes in FWI driven by relative humidity and precipitation show an opposite trend compared to temperature ( Fig. 6, A to D ). The significant reduction in fire danger observed in the ZeroE scenario can be well explained by changes in FWI driven by relative humidity, precipitation, and wind speed, rather than temperature. In other words, if NegE scenarios are successfully implemented, the mitigation of rising temperatures will lead to a global reduction in fire dangers. However, variables such as relative humidity and precipitation show regional differences under the emission reduction scenarios, ultimately determining regional patterns of fire danger mitigation or intensification. When comparing the sum of ΔFWI (change in FWI) values attributed to the four meteorological variables in Fig. 6 (A to D) with the total ΔFWI ( Fig. 3B ) in differences between the N7 and Z7 periods, the aggregated contributions from individual variables slightly underestimate the overall changes (fig. S5). This suggests the presence of nonlinear factors in the calculation of FWI. However, the method effectively captures most of the magnitude and spatial patterns of change, demonstrating its utility in explaining the contributions of individual meteorological variables to fire weather danger. The comparison of total FWI changes under the two scenarios and the contributions of each meteorological variable to FWI changes reveals distinct regional differences ( Fig. 6E ). Overall, temperature and relative humidity are identified as the dominant contributors to FWI changes. However, in eastern South America, precipitation shows a higher contribution to FWI changes compared to other regions, whereas in Southern Australia (SAU), temperature plays a more significant role than other variables, showing distinct regional differences in the leading drivers of changes in fire danger across different climate states ( 13 ). These findings highlight that the contributions of meteorological variables influencing FWI responses to CO 2 reduction scenarios vary by region. In addition, when comparing the responses of meteorological variables for the annual mean state and the extreme fire weather days (top 5% FWI days), temperature is found to contribute in the same direction for both the mean and extreme states ( Fig. 7 ). In most regions, temperature increases are more pronounced under the ZeroE scenario during extreme conditions. Relative humidity also shows more distinct regional differences under extreme conditions, with a substantial decrease in relative humidity in Central America, where extreme fire weathers occur most frequently. Precipitation exhibits similar patterns to relative humidity. Wind speed is found to be stronger during extreme conditions compared to the mean state. These contributions of meteorological variables under extreme conditions are more pronounced under the ZeroE scenario than the NegE scenario. Fig. 7. To further understand the distinct differences in fire danger during the N7/Z7 period, an analysis was conducted on the changes in temperature and atmospheric moisture supply, which substantially influence fire weather ( Fig. 8 ). Compared to the N7 period under the NegE scenario, the Z7 period under the ZeroE scenario exhibits significantly higher temperatures. Notably, the global sea surface temperature for Z7 is much warmer than that for P0, especially over the Southern Hemisphere, except for the North Atlantic, which shows relative cooling. As mentioned in Introduction, this pronounced asymmetric global sea surface temperature response observed during Z7 is driven by the strong thermal inertia of the Southern Ocean and the weakening of the AMOC, which cause a southward shift of the ITCZ ( 25 , 30 ). The changes in AMOC and ITCZ under CO 2 reduction scenarios reveal that as CO 2 levels increase from P0 to P2, the AMOC weakens, creating a hemispheric thermal imbalance that shifts the ITCZ southward ( Fig. 9, A and B ). After P2, the delayed recovery of the AMOC and shifts in the ITCZ under CO 2 reduction scenarios are observed, in agreement with previous studies ( 37 , 38 ). In the NegE scenario, these effects gradually reverse, while in the ZeroE scenario, recovery is substantially delayed. Notably, in the ZeroE scenario, the model spread among ensemble members is wider, indicating greater uncertainty. Analyzing the AMOC recovery across ensemble members reveals that weaker AMOC recovery results in a more pronounced southward shift of the ITCZ ( Fig. 9C ). Fig. 9. This ITCZ shift alters precipitation patterns in low-latitude regions, substantially affecting atmospheric moisture distribution ( Fig. 9D ). Specifically, as the ITCZ moves farther south, tropical regions in the Northern Hemisphere experience reduced precipitation and become drier, while tropical regions in the Southern Hemisphere exhibit increased precipitation and become relatively wetter, in agreement with earlier findings ( 39 , 40 ). Consequently, the ZeroE scenario demonstrates a more pronounced weakening of the AMOC and sustained southward displacement of the ITCZ compared to the NegE scenario. These mechanisms determine the changes in relative humidity according to the two scenarios ( Fig. 8, C and D ), which in turn markedly affect changes in fire weather. Clearly, the changes are substantially mitigated in the NegE scenario compared to the ZeroE scenario. Regionally, the responses under the ZeroE scenario highlight notable contrasts. For example, South America exhibits a distinct fire weather response compared to other regions. Despite increased temperatures during Z7 relative to the present ( Fig. 8B ), enhanced moisture transport from the equatorial Atlantic results in higher relative humidity, leading to a substantial reduction in fire weather compared to current levels. In contrast, low-latitude regions in the Northern Hemisphere, such as Sub-Saharan Africa, India, and Central America, experience significant increases in fire weather because of reduced moisture supply caused by the southward shift of the ITCZ ( Fig. 8H ). These regions, characterized as tropical rainforest areas, also experience substantial reductions in plant transpiration because of increased CO 2 levels ( 41 ), which likely contribute to the heightened fire danger ( Fig. 8J ). In conclusion, changes in relative humidity driven by variations in temperature and atmospheric moisture supply emerge as key determinants of fire weather, reaffirming previous findings ( 13 , 32 ). While temperature increases globally, changes in moisture supply exhibit strong regional differences. As a result, these differences act as a major mechanism driving the distinct regional fire weather responses between the NegE and ZeroE scenarios. DISCUSSION This study examined the global and regional responses of fire weather to CO 2 reduction scenarios, focusing on both average and extreme fire weathers. The results highlight that achieving net-zero CO 2 emissions (ZeroEs) alone is insufficient to mitigate fire dangers effectively in many regions, especially in low-latitude areas of the Northern Hemisphere. The analysis of average fire weather reveals that under the ZeroE scenario, fire danger remains elevated compared to the present across most regions, even during the quasiequilibrium state (Z7). In contrast, the NegE scenario substantially mitigates fire dangers in many areas by reducing CO 2 concentrations to the levels of the 2000s, emphasizing the effectiveness of aggressive CO 2 removal strategies. Regional disparities in fire danger mitigation are evident, with South America showing a reduction in fire danger because of increased precipitation and relative humidity, while regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa, India, and Central America experience persistent or heightened dangers under the ZeroE scenario because of reduced moisture supply and increased temperatures. The response of extreme fire weather (FWI 95d and FWI max ) also reflects the patterns observed for the average state. Under the ZeroE scenario, the frequency and intensity of extreme fire weathers remain significantly higher than the present in most regions, particularly in tropical regions of the Northern Hemisphere. However, the NegE scenario demonstrates a notable mitigation of extreme fire weathers, reducing their frequency and intensity by more than 70% compared to peak CO 2 levels (P2). The distinct regional responses to the two scenarios are found to be linked to changes in temperature and atmospheric moisture supply. The ZeroE scenario exhibits a pronounced southward shift of the ITCZ because of the delayed recovery of AMOC and the strong thermal inertia of the Southern Ocean. This leads to wetter conditions in the Southern Hemisphere tropics and drier conditions in the Northern Hemisphere tropics. These changes in relative humidity play a dominant role in driving changes in fire weather dangers. This study underscores the critical importance of adopting comprehensive CO 2 reduction strategies that go beyond achieving ZeroEs to effectively address global and regional disparities in fire danger. While the NegE scenario demonstrates considerable potential in mitigating fire dangers through NegEs and a faster recovery of climate systems, the ZeroE scenario alone falls short, particularly in tropical regions of the Northern Hemisphere. These findings emphasize the need for sustained CO 2 removal efforts and the inclusion of region-specific strategies in climate mitigation policies. This study focused on changes in fire weather conditions (FWI) rather than on actual wildfire occurrences. While the FWI is among the most reliable indicators of fire danger across both tropical and extratropical regions ( 34 ), its representativeness varies by region and may not fully account for the spatial and temporal distribution of actual wildfire events. More detailed, region-specific research can be conducted in the future, taking into account local characteristics and customized indices. In addition, the analysis in this study relies on a single general circulation model (CESM2). Biases in the CESM2 simulation ( Fig. 1 ) possibly affect the magnitude of the responses. Although the results are presented as anomalies relative to the baseline period, potential shortcomings may remain in simulating the magnitude of trends and variability of the FWI, warranting caution and further validation of quantitative estimates beyond the qualitative findings. Nonetheless, the physical mechanisms underlying the main results are consistent with previous reports ( 30 , 38 ), supporting the physical plausibility of our conclusions. Moreover, the results derived from the uniquely designed CESM2 simulations are valuable for understanding changes in fire dangers under net-zero and net-negative CO 2 emissions. The possible shortcoming can be addressed in future research by using multiple general circulation models to calculate ensemble means, potentially reducing model biases and providing more robust insights. Furthermore, the aggregated contributions of meteorological variables in FWI calculations, derived from the additive decomposition approach, may slightly underestimate overall FWI changes because of nonlinear interactions among these variables. However, the overall spatial patterns and relative contributions remain robust, indicating that this limitation does not affect the qualitative interpretation of the results. Although this approach effectively captures the magnitude and spatial patterns of most changes, further exploration is needed to improve our understanding of these nonlinear effects and their role in driving fire weather. MATERIALS AND METHODS Data We used data from CESM2 ( 42 ) to simulate global climate responses under CO 2 reduction scenarios. CESM2 integrates multiple components via a central coupler: the atmosphere [CAM6 (Community Atmospheric Model Version 6)], ocean [POP2 (Parallel Ocean Program Version 2)], sea ice [CICE5 (Community Ice Code Version 5)], land [CLM5 (Community Land Model Version 5)], land ice [CISM2 (Community Ice Sheet Model Version 2)], river [MOSART (Model for Scale Adaptive River Transport)], and waves [WW3 (WaveWatch-III)]. The atmospheric model has a horizontal resolution of ~1° and 32 vertical layers. The ocean model features a longitudinal resolution of 1.125°, with the latitudinal resolution ranging from 0.27° near the equator to 0.5° at the poles, and 60 vertical layers. In this study, experiments were conducted under two CO 2 emission scenarios: NegEs and ZeroEs. The NegE experiment was integrated over 400 years, with CO 2 emissions starting at 6.7 gigatonnes of carbon (GtC)/year in 2000, increasing linearly to a peak of 21.6 GtC/year by 2050, then declining to a negative minimum of −21.5 GtC/year in 2196, and maintaining net-zero CO 2 emissions thereafter until 2400 (fig. S1). The ZeroE experiment was also integrated over 400 years, with CO 2 emissions following the same pathway as the NegE experiment until 2050, then decreasing to zero by 2123, and maintaining net-zero CO 2 emissions for the remainder of the simulation until 2400. Both scenarios shared the same CO 2 emission pathways until 2123, after which the ZeroE experiment maintained net-zero CO 2 emissions, while the NegE experiment followed a more aggressive mitigation pathway. Each experiment includes six ensemble members, performed with different initial conditions. To calculate the FWI, daily maximum near-surface temperature (TREFHTMAX), daily precipitation (PRECT), daily mean surface wind speed (WSPDSRFAV), and daily minimum relative humidity were used for each experiment. Daily minimum relative humidity was calculated as the minimum of the 6-hourly near-surface relative humidity (RHREFHT) within each local day for CESM2. In addition, ERA5 reanalysis data for the period of 2001 to 2024 were used to evaluate the performance of CESM2 in simulating the current climate ( 43 ). For ERA5, daily minimum relative humidity was derived from 3-hourly data using the same approach to ensure consistency between datasets. To verify the distribution of actual fire occurrences, daily burned area data from GFED5 (Global Fire Emissions Database Version 5.0) were also used ( 44 ). Fire weather index The FWI system, a globally used fire danger rating system, was developed by the Canadian Forest Service ( 45 ). This system consists of six components (table S2). The first three moisture indices include the Fine Fuel Moisture Code, the Duff Moisture Code, and the Drought Code. These indices represent moisture levels at different fuel layer depths and track the impact of weather conditions on fuel moisture. In addition, there is the Initial Spread Index, which is calculated using moisture and wind speed data near the surface to estimate the initial spread of wildfires. The Buildup Index represents the potential total amount of fuel available for combustion. The final component, FWI, is derived by combining the Initial Spread Index and Buildup Index and describes the overall intensity of a fire. The calculation of the FWI requires daily 24-hour accumulated precipitation as well as temperature, humidity, and wind speed at 1200 local time. Given the difficulties in obtaining observed and model data at 1200 local time, we followed prior global analyses and used daily maximum temperature, daily minimum relative humidity, daily accumulated precipitation, and daily mean wind speed to calculate the daily FWI ( 10 , 20 ). The FWI was calculated from both CESM2 and ERA5 data using the same daily-proxy inputs to ensure comparability. Although this daily-proxy approach may slightly underestimate noon-based FWI magnitudes, the relative changes remain robust and do not qualitatively affect the results ( 45 ). For the FWI calculations, we used the Fortran code provided by Wang et al. ( 46 ). In addition, for the analysis of extreme fire weather, we defined extreme fire weather indices using FWI values ( 20 ). FWI 95d represents the frequency of extreme fire danger days, defined as the number of days exceeding the 95th percentile threshold of the baseline period (P0, 2001 to 2031). The 95th percentile was computed once per grid using daily FWI during P0 and applied consistently to all subsequent periods. FWI max denotes the annual maximum FWI value, indicating the peak fire danger index throughout the year. To identify robust changes in FWI across the ensemble, grid points where at least five of six members agreed in the sign of change were considered consistent. Ensemble agreement (≥5/6 members) was therefore used as a robustness measure instead of a statistical significance test. Time slice methodology Considering that atmospheric CO 2 directly interacts with climate states more than carbon emissions, we applied the “time slice” method used by Liu et al. ( 38 ) to compare fire weather on the basis of CO 2 concentrations. We selected key years at regular intervals along the CO 2 concentration pathways of the NegE and ZeroE experiments and defined a time slice as the 31-year average centered on each key year. To analyze the current climate, we defined P0 as the initial 31 years (2001 to 2031), during which the NegE and ZeroE experiments follow the same emission pathway. This period exhibits atmospheric CO 2 levels comparable to the present and can be used as a baseline period. P1 is defined as the period when CO 2 emissions peak, and P2 is defined as the period when atmospheric CO 2 concentrations peak [726 ppm (parts per million)]. Following P2, time slices 3 through 7 were set at ~50-year intervals according to the two scenarios (fig. S1). For each scenario, the NegE experiment is labeled with “N,” and the ZeroE experiment is labeled with “Z.” The key years for the selected time slices are 2054 (P1), 2107 (P2), 2160 (N3/Z3), 2213 (N4/Z4), 2266 (N5/Z5), 2319 (N6/Z6), and 2372 (N7/Z7). Contribution of meteorological variables to fire weather responses We estimated the contribution of changes in individual meteorological variables to the difference in FWI between the NegE and ZeroE scenarios. For example, to calculate the FWI change due to temperature (table S1), we computed the difference in daily Tmax (Δ Tmax daily ) between the NegE and ZeroE scenarios (NegE minus ZeroE) during the Nn / Zn ( n = 3 to 7) period. This difference was then subtracted from the original Tmax during the Nn period to create an adjusted temperature dataset ( Tmax adj_daily ). Using Tmax adj_daily together with relative humidity, precipitation, and wind speed from the Nn period, we calculated a new FWI ( FWI′ Tmax ). The impact of temperature changes on FWI (Δ FWI Tmax ) was determined by comparing this adjusted FWI′ Tmax with the original FWI for the N n period. The same procedure was repeated for other meteorological variables to isolate and quantify their specific contributions to changes in FWI between the NegE and ZeroE scenarios.
