The Earth’s energy imbalance (EEI) that develops at the top of the atmosphere is accommodated by gains or losses of energy in Earth’s heat reservoirs, leading to temperature and sea level change. The global ocean has stored about 90% of the EEI from anthropogenic forcing, but the attribution of past changes of EEI remains largely unknown, thus obscuring our understanding of past climate change. Here, we reconstruct changes in mean ocean temperature over the past 150,000 years that, with a reconstruction of ice sheet–volume changes, allow us to isolate the contributions of the dominant ocean and ice sheet heat reservoirs to the global energy inventory as well as to derive their associated contributions to EEI. We attribute orbital-scale EEI variability to joint precessional and CO 2 forcing that caused changes in rates of ice sheet energy storage. The global energy budget is defined as Δ Ν = Δ F − Δ R , where Δ Ν is the Earth’s energy imbalance (EEI) equivalent to the change in the top-of-atmosphere (TOA) net radiative flux (watts per square meter), Δ F is the incoming effective radiative forcing (watts per square meter), and Δ R is the Earth’s radiative response (watts per square meter) [=λΔ T , where λ is the climate feedback parameter (watts per square meter per kelvin) and Δ T is the change in surface temperature (kelvins)] ( 1 , 2 ). Due to energy conservation, EEI must be equal to the rate of energy gain or loss from the Earth’s heat reservoirs (atmosphere, cryosphere, land, and ocean), with attendant changes in the global energy inventory that lag the forcing because of the thermal inertia of the climate system and thus provide an important constraint on equilibrium climate sensitivity ( 1 , 3 , 4 ). The current global energy budget is derived from satellite radiometers that measure the TOA net radiative flux and from measurements of ocean, atmosphere, and land heat content and ice melting that document changes in the global energy inventory in response to increasing EEI. For the period 2006–2020, the EEI from anthropogenic increases in greenhouse gas concentrations and aerosols is 0.76 ± 0.2 W m −2 ( 5 ), with ∼90% of the excess energy being stored in the global ocean and the remaining ∼10% distributed among Earth’s other heat reservoirs ( 5 , 6 ). In contrast, the partitioning of past changes of EEI among the dominant ocean and ice sheet heat reservoirs and their attribution remains largely unknown, thus obscuring our understanding of past, long-term changes of the climate system. Based on reconstructions of changes of mean ocean temperature (ΔMOT) and ice sheet volume, one study ( 7 ) found a positive EEI associated with nearly equivalent energy gains from ocean warming and land-ice melting during Termination I [TI; 21 to 10 thousand years ago (ka)]. Notably, they also identified two peaks in EEI that correspond to strong reductions of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC) and millennial-scale ΔMOT variability during Heinrich Stadial 1 (HS1; ∼18 to 14.7 ka) and the Younger Dryas (YD; ∼12.9 to 11.7 ka). Although models also find a positive EEI during a period of reduced AMOC ( 8 ), they do not reproduce the peaked EEI response found in the reconstruction, suggesting that they are missing other radiative forcings or responses. A recent study ( 9 ) used a benthic δ 18 O stack (δ 18 O b ) ( 10 ), which reflects a combined signal of ΔMOT and ice sheet changes, to reconstruct changes in the global energy inventory and EEI from these two heat reservoirs for the past 150 thousand years (kyr). They found similar millennial- and orbital-scale variability in EEI as during TI ( 7 ), but because they could not isolate the contributions of ocean and ice sheet storage to the global energy inventory, they were unable to distinguish between possible forcings in explaining the EEI signal. Here, we address these questions by first reconstructing ΔMOT (referenced to 0 to 1 ka) over the past 150 kyr. Our ΔMOT reconstruction shows substantial millennial-scale variability that is associated with large reductions in the AMOC during Heinrich stadials. We then use our ΔMOT reconstruction and a sea level stack ( 11 ) to quantify the contributions of the ocean and land-ice heat reservoirs to changes in the global energy inventory, allowing us to attribute reservoir-specific changes in EEI to specific forcings. Last, we use a transient climate simulation to show that the radiative responses to a large reduction of the AMOC and a corresponding increase in CO 2 result in the peaked EEI responses seen in our reconstruction. Consistent with noble gas data, our reconstruction suggests that ΔMOT increased by 3.1° ± 1°C during Termination II (TII; ∼140 to 129 ka), peaked at a level that was up to 1°C higher than the present interglaciation, and then decreased to 0.1° ± 1.3°C by ∼125 ka. Shackleton et al . ( 14 ) then inferred from the noble gas data that ΔMOT stabilized until 120 ka (the end of their record), whereas our more highly resolved reconstruction shows that ΔMOT continued to decrease, reaching −1.5° ± 1.5°C by 118 ka. ΔMOT then experienced a more-gradual long-term decrease with millennial-scale variability (∼0.5°C range) until ∼75 ka when it reached a long-term average of about ∼−2°C also with millennial-scale variability (0.5° to 1°C range) that continued until the start of TI ∼19 ka, including a pronounced millennial-scale decrease in ΔMOT during the LGM that is not resolved by the noble gas data ( Fig. 1A ). ΔMOT then increased by 2.7° ± 0.4°C during TI that included an oscillation during the Antarctic Cold Reversal, in good agreement with the noble gas data ( 9 , 14 , 15 ) and other reconstructions ( 17 ). We propose that much of the millennial-scale ΔMOT variability that dominates our reconstruction between 118 and 20 ka ( Fig. 1A ) was forced by the AMOC, with millennial-scale CO 2 changes of ∼20 parts per million (ppm) that also occurred likely in response to changes in the AMOC ( 18 , 19 ) acting as a feedback on ΔMOT. This hypothesis is based on single forcing runs in the isotope-enabled transient climate evolution (iTRACE) simulations of TI that find that MOT increases by 1° to 1.5°C in response to large AMOC reductions, or comparable to our reconstruction ( Fig. 1A ), as opposed to a ∼0.2°C increase from 20-ppm CO 2 forcing (scaled to the full CO 2 forcing) and negligible ice sheet and orbital forcing ( 17 ). Figure 2 assesses this hypothesis by examining changes in ΔMOT during Heinrich stadials 4, 5, 6, and 8 when large AMOC reductions ( 20 , 21 ) caused the bipolar seesaw response of Northern Hemisphere (NH) surface cooling and Southern Hemisphere (SH) surface warming ( 22 ) with a corresponding ∼20- to 30-ppm increase of CO 2 ( 23 ). In each case, ΔMOT paralleled changes in SH surface temperature, requiring that the bipolar seesaw model be expanded to include mean ocean as well as surface temperature changes ( 17 , 24 , 25 ). Although Heinrich stadials 1 and 11 also experienced large AMOC reductions (fig. S2) ( 20 , 26 ), they differ from the other stadials examined here by having occurred during TI and TII, respectively, which experienced greater CO 2 and ice sheet forcing. Nevertheless, the iTRACE simulation finds that warming from AMOC reductions still contributed ∼50% to the total transient MOT increase during TI ( 17 ). On this basis, we argue that the large and prolonged (∼7 kyr) AMOC reduction during HS11 ( 26 ) also played a pivotal role in explaining the large increase in Atlantic temperature (∼6°C) and MOT (∼3°C) during TII ( Figs. 1, A and B , and 2F ). The full-depth warming of the Atlantic basin in our reconstruction that leads and exceeds ΔMOT during TII ( Fig. 1B ), with a subsequent temperature increase in the Indo-Pacific basin that is in phase with ΔMOT ( Fig. 1C ), is a fingerprint of AMOC-induced Atlantic warming that is propagated to other ocean basins over hundreds of years (fig. S3) ( 17 , 24 , 25 ). Moreover, Clark et al . ( 25 ) showed that the greater subsurface warming induced by the longer interval of reduced AMOC during TII can explain the more-rapid sea level rise compared with TI. We next examine the cause of the ∼2.5°C MOT decrease immediately following its peak warmth as the start of the Last Interglaciation (LIG) ( Fig. 3A ). An important insight is the strong correlation of ΔMOT and Antarctic temperature [coefficient of determination ( R 2 ) = 0.74], suggesting a connection between MOT and SH surface temperature ( Fig. 3A ) ( 12 , 15 , 16 ). As discussed, AMOC forcing causes joint millennial-scale changes of MOT and SH temperature and, with CO 2 and ice sheet forcing, warming during TI and TII. However, LIG SH surface temperature and MOT began to decrease at ∼130 ka and were within ∼20% of their LGM values by 118 ka in the absence of any AMOC ( 26 , 27 ) or CO 2 forcing and with minimal ice sheet forcing ( Fig. 3B ), thus requiring an additional forcing to explain these changes. One clear candidate is provided by single forcing experiments with climate models which show that obliquity forcing strongly influences Antarctic and Southern Ocean (SO) surface temperatures and sea-ice extent ( 28 ), consistent with proxy changes during this post-LIG interval ( Fig. 3A ). A decrease of SO sea surface temperatures (SSTs) and attendant increase in sea-ice extent would then cause an increase in formation rate and a decrease in the temperature of Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW), with both leading to a decrease in MOT. Cooling of North Atlantic Deep Water from obliquity forcing of North Atlantic surface waters ( 28 ) may have further contributed to a decrease in MOT by itself as well as by its cooling effect on AABW ( 29 ). We then infer that responses to obliquity forcing after the LIG and before TI would have been modulated by CO 2 and ice sheet forcing ( Fig. 3B ). Fig. 3. Potential forcings of ΔMOT. Changes in Earth’s global energy inventory and energy imbalance We converted our ΔMOT reconstruction and a sea level stack of ice sheet changes ( 11 ) to equivalent changes in energy (Δ E ocean and Δ E ice , respectively) ( Fig. 4A ) and summed them to derive changes in the global energy inventory (Δ E global ) ( Fig. 4B ) (Materials and Methods). Our results demonstrate that, like today, Δ E global , as determined by the TOA energy budget, was partitioned by the major energy flows between the atmosphere heat reservoir (Δ E atm ) and the ocean and ice sheet reservoirs, with the residual being Δ E atm that, while very small, parallels Δ E global and is manifested as changes in global mean surface temperature ( 30 ). We note that Δ E atm is negligible so that ΕΕΙ primarily comprises changes in E ocean and E ice . At any given time, however, the individual contributions of Δ E ocean and Δ E ice to Δ E global may be constructive or destructive ( Fig. 4A ) so that times of strong energy gains by the ocean, for example, could be offset (enhanced) by energy losses (gains) by ice sheets, with this interference of the two signals reflecting the different forcings, processes of heat exchange, and response times of the two heat reservoirs. We derived the EEI and its components from the rates of Δ E ice and Δ E ocean (EEI ice and EEI ocean ) and Δ E global (EEI) ( Fig. 4D ) (Materials and Methods). Our EEI reconstruction is in good agreement with one derived from a δ 18 O b stack ( 9 ) that represents a combined signal of the two heat reservoirs (fig. S4). Shackleton et al . ( 9 ) inferred that changes in EEI represent an AMOC control that was modulated by orbital forcing of ice sheet surface mass balance, but attribution of the signal to these forcings is not possible without isolating EEI ice and EEI ocean . By now doing so, we can attribute EEI ice to energy gains and losses that are associated with precessional forcing modulated by CO 2 forcing ( Fig. 4E ). EEI ice varies from about −0.05 to 0.05 Wm −2 except (i) during TI and TII when it increased to 0.13 to 0.14 Wm −2 from strong CO 2 forcing ( Fig. 3B ) that was used for additional ice sheet mass loss from melting and (ii) ∼35 ka when it only increased by ∼0.05 Wm −2 in association with the weakest precessional forcing and weak CO 2 forcing ( Fig. 4E ). In contrast, peaks in EEI ocean occurred during each of the longer millennial-scale Heinrich stadials in the Greenland ice core ( Fig. 4D and fig. S5), most of which have an established AMOC control ( 20 , 21 , 26 ). Notably, there are no corresponding peaks during the intervening Dansgaard-Oeschger events that may reflect the weaker AMOC changes at those times ( 21 , 26 , 31 ). EEI ocean variability is about twice as large as EEI ice , ranging from about −0.1 to 0.1 Wm −2 except during TI and TII when additional strong CO 2 and ice sheet forcing ( 17 ) caused it to increase to 0.27 to 0.29 Wm −2 ( Fig. 4C ). Our reconstructed peaks in EEI ocean represent a two-stage pattern that begins with an emerging stage of a positive EEI ocean peak followed by a diminishing EEI ocean stage during Heinrich stadials ( Fig. 4D and figs. S5 and S6), corresponding to a faster followed by a slower increasing rate of MOT ( Fig. 2 ), as was also found by Baggenstos et al . ( 7 ) for HS1 and the YD. This two-stage EEI response is simulated in a state-of-the-art climate model in iTRACE ( 17 ). We note, however, that data smoothing has likely modified the original structure of this pattern, obscuring exactly when and how quickly the positive EEI ocean emerges and diminishes in the reconstruction. From the oceanic perspective, the increased MOT or initial buildup of positive EEI is caused by the strong meltwater flux over the northern North Atlantic, which weakens deep convection and the associated convective mixing of upward heat flux from the deep ocean to the surface, leading to a subsurface warming (and surface cooling) and, in turn, increased MOT there ( 32 ). The resulting weakening of the AMOC also reduces the northward oceanic heat transport, accumulating heat and increasing MOT in the tropical and South Atlantic throughout the ocean depth. Our energy budget analysis of the iTRACE simulation ( Fig. 5 ) further shows that this two-stage EEI response can be understood overall as an initial stage of rapid buildup of MOT owing to the surface cooling in the NH followed by a slow stage when the MOT increase is slowed by the damping associated with the Planck feedback on the surface warming over the SH that accompanies the bipolar seesaw response of surface temperature to the weakening AMOC. The first stage corresponds to the initial 200 years (18 to 17.8 ka) when the MOT increases rapidly with a positive EEI peaking at 0.4 W m −2 ( Fig. 5F ). The global energy budget is dominated by the change in the NH. The ocean surface heat loss is decreased (warming effect) by the strong reduction of turbulent heat flux ( Fig. 5C ) accompanying the surface cooling ( Fig. 5A ), which overwhelms the increased heat loss (cooling effect) of net shortwave radiation ( Fig. 5C ) associated with expanding sea ice and increasing cloud albedo ( Fig. 5A ) and generates an increased downward net surface heat flux (warming effect) ( Fig. 5C ). At the TOA, the change of surface heat flux leads to a strong reduction of outgoing longwave radiation (warming effect) that overwhelms the increased loss of outgoing shortwave radiation (cooling effect) ( Fig. 5E ), leading to the rapid buildup of MOT that corresponds to a positive EEI peak ( Fig. 5E ). After the first stage of 200-year gain, the MOT increase is slowed with positive EEI or increasing rate, gradually diminishing toward the end of HS1 (∼15 ka). This reduction of MOT increasing rate is due to the increased turbulent heat flux (cooling effect) ( Fig. 5D ) from the delayed warming over the SH surface ( Fig. 5B ) ( 25 , 33 , 34 ), which warms the atmospheric column and then increases the TOA loss of outgoing longwave radiation (cooling effect) as a negative Planck feedback ( Fig. 5E ). Meanwhile, the strong cooling effect of increased loss of TOA longwave radiation is strongly compensated by the warming effect of reduced reflection of outgoing shortwave radiation in the SH ( Fig. 5E ) that is amplified by positive surface feedback from the retreating sea ice and decreasing cloud albedo ( Fig. 5B ). This shortwave surface warming is further enhanced by the increased CO 2 concentration in the atmosphere that increases downward net longwave radiation modestly on the surface (warming effect) in both hemispheres ( Fig. 5, C and D ), as shown in our additional sensitivity experiments (fig. S7). Thus, the MOT increase in the second stage could have been much smaller in the absence of positive surface sea-ice feedback and the increased CO 2 forcing. The iTRACE simulation is largely consistent with previous climate model simulations in response to an AMOC reduction although the two-stage feature of the positive EEI is less clear than in iTRACE ( 8 , 35 ), likely because these simulations adopted short and idealized meltwater forcing, did not consider the 20- to 60-ppm increase in CO 2 (∼1 W m −2 ), and perhaps had a weaker sea-ice albedo positive feedback. DISCUSSION We have attributed EEI over the past 150,000 years to (i) precessional forcing modulated by CO 2 forcing that is recorded by changes in energy storage of ice sheets and (ii) radiative forcing from AMOC and CO 2 variability that is recorded by changes in energy storage of the global ocean. During TII and TI, the large increases in CO 2 forcing further amplified EEI, causing additional increases in energy storage of both reservoirs. However, differences in EEI variability associated with each reservoir-specific forcing and response time caused the two signals to interfere and thus modulated EEI throughout most of the past 150,000 years ( Fig. 4C ). In contrast, the current EEI is from anthropogenic forcing ( 3 , 6 ) with ongoing storage of excess energy being partitioned largely (∼90%) in the global ocean. Nevertheless, how EEI will change in the future is dependent on the forcing as well as on the physical behavior and response time of the main heat reservoirs. For example, given the longer response time of the ice sheets, we can expect that EEI used for melting will increase ( 3 ), with some projections suggesting 10 to 25 m of global sea level rise for a lower end emission scenario similar to representative concentration pathway (RCP) 4.5 and 30 to 45 m for a high-end emission scenario similar to RCP 8.5 over the next few millennia ( 36 , 37 ). These projections, however, do not include scenarios in which various mitigation strategies result in a future decrease in anthropogenic forcing. At the same time, ice sheet dynamical effects that cause irreversible melting may prolong higher energy storage under reduced forcing ( 38 ) as well as increase the rate of energy storage ( 39 ). Last, anthropogenic forcing may cause a reduction of the AMOC ( 40 ) and thus increase EEI, although this possibility remains uncertain ( 41 ). MATERIALS AND METHODS Calculating regional and mean ocean temperature change from benthic δ 18 O We used the ΔMOT reconstruction for 0 to 20 ka from ( 17 ) because of its higher resolution and better age control than our stack (fig. S8). To account for species-dependent calibrations, we reconstructed regional and mean ocean temperature stacks for 20 to 150 ka from 246 globally distributed benthic δ 18 O records from the Lisiecki and Stern database ( 10 ) (fig. S1 and table S1). δ 18 O measured on benthic foraminifera (δ 18 O b ) records a combination of the temperature of the water during shell calcification (δ 18 O T ) and the δ 18 O of seawater (δ 18 O sw ), which is controlled by the amount of land ice and water-mass hydrography. Pore-water measurements of deep-sea cores ( 42 , 43 ) confirm that the volume-weighted global average increase in δ 18 O sw at the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM; 21 ka) is 1.0 ± 0.1 per mil (‰). To obtain δ 18 O sw over the past 150 kyr, we divided this 1‰ increase by the LGM sea level lowstand (−130 m) ( 11 ) to derive the relationship of −0.0077‰ m −1 and applied it to a sea level stack ( 11 ). We do not account for the possible influence of δ 18 O sw gradients in basin-specific δ 18 O records, but two lines of evidence suggest that these are small and would have a negligible effect on our results. First, we note that the end-member δ 18 O of the two major water masses of the ocean today (North Atlantic Deep Water and AABW) differs by ∼0.4 to 0.5‰ ( 44 ). Climate model simulations of mixing of these two water masses in the world’s oceans during the last deglaciation suggest that, at any one location, differences from today in contributions of these two water masses to δ 18 O sw were generally <0.2‰ ( 45 ), suggesting a temperature uncertainty of <0.8°C. Small interbasin δ 18 O sw gradients are also suggested by δ 18 O sw data from deep Atlantic sites that range from 0.7.0 to 0.8‰ and by larger values from the Pacific (1.0 ± 0.1‰) and from Southern Ocean source waters (1.17 ± 0.1‰) ( 42 , 43 ). The deep Pacific porewater δ 18 O sw ( 42 ) aligns with the global average (1.0 ± 0.1‰), whereas Atlantic δ 18 O sw was ∼0.2‰ less, suggesting a temperature bias of the deep Atlantic records of ∼0.8°C. However, the deep Atlantic only accounts for ∼12% of the global ocean volume ( 10 ) so that any temperature bias on our ΔMOT reconstruction would be negligible. This small effect on our ΔMOT reconstruction is supported by its good agreement with ΔMOT derived from noble gases measured in Antarctic ice cores ( Fig. 1A ). The regional and ΔMOT stacks from 20 to 150 ka were generated as follows. At the 500-year resolution for each of the 246 δ 18 O b records, we randomly generated 1000 time series of δ 18 O sw that fall within ±1σ of the δ 18 O sw record, thus accounting for uncertainty in the alignment of the δ 18 O sw record with the individual δ 18 O b record. We then subtracted each of the 1000 δ 18 O sw time series from the individual δ 18 O b record, resulting in 1000 different δ 18 O T series for each site. We converted each of the 1000 δ 18 O T realizations into temperature using calibrations for Cibicidoides and Planulina ( n = 218) and Uvigerina spp. ( n = 28) (table S1 and data S1) ( 46 ). We organized each site’s 1000 temperature realizations by ocean basin, with 152 sites in the Atlantic and 94 sites in the Indo-Pacific. For the 1000 temperature realizations at each site within an ocean basin, we took the first realization of all records and stacked them. We then repeated this for all realizations with the result being 1000 temperature stacks for the respective ocean basin. We take the mean and standard deviation of the 1000 temperature stacks for each ocean basin to generate our temperature stacks with their temperature uncertainty for each ocean basin. The two regional stacks were then weighted by their respective basin volumes (Atlantic, 26%; and Indo-Pacific, 74%) and summed to derive ΔMOT. We calculated the uncertainty on the ΔMOT stack as the square root of the sum of squares of the standard deviation of the regional stacks and the δ 18 O b analytical error (0.04‰ converted to temperature). Age model uncertainty We applied an age model to each of the 246 globally distributed benthic δ 18 O records in the Lisiecki and Stern database ( 10 ) used here (fig. S1). Age model development follows the same steps as in ( 10 ), including using their R-code. Using the automated alignment software Match, all Atlantic benthic δ 18 O records were aligned to the North Atlantic MD95-2042 benthic δ 18 O record, which has an age model based on correlation of tie points in its SST proxy signal to well-dated Greenland ice-core and speleothem records. All Indian and Pacific benthic δ 18 O records were aligned to the Pacific MD97-2120 benthic δ 18 O record, which has an age model based on its alignment to the MD95-2042 benthic δ 18 O record. Following ( 10 ), we applied a constant 750-year lag to the deep Indian and Pacific records (>2000 m) suggested by modern deepwater ages and an additional lag of 2000 years to the start of TII in the deep Indian records as suggested by a lag of the same amount identified during TI in 14 C-based deep Indian record age models. Lisiecki and Stern ( 10 ) acknowledge that this 2000-year lag is speculative but note that the deep Indian Ocean only accounts for 7% of the volume-weighted global stack and so has a small influence on the Indo-Pacific and global stacks. For 11.63 to 143 ka, we apply 1σ age uncertainty derived by Lisiecki and Stern ( 10 ) for the North Atlantic target record (MD95-2042) to our two regional temperature stacks and ΔMOT stack, with age uncertainty at tie points based on the Greenland ice-core and speleothem records and then using the Bacon software to develop age model uncertainty between tie points (fig. S9). Age uncertainty was not provided from 0 to 11.63 or 143 to 150 ka so we applied a constant uncertainty based on the nearest age (11.63 ka and 143 ka) from ( 10 ) to our two regional temperature stacks and ΔMOT stack. Lisiecki and Stern ( 10 ) apply an additional 2σ age uncertainty of 2000 years to the Indo-Pacific stacks and their global stack “to allow for regional differences in the timing of δ 18 O change.” They use four lines of evidence to assess their 2000-year additional uncertainty but other than during TI in the deep Indian Ocean, there is agreement that regional differences in timing for much of the ocean are considerably smaller (0 to 800 years). We thus assess the additional 2σ 2000-year age uncertainty with low confidence and only apply the 1σ age uncertainty derived from the MD95-2042 target record (fig. S9), suggesting that our uncertainty may be too small by at most several hundred years. We account for the age uncertainty of our Atlantic, Indo-Pacific, and MOT stacks by randomly perturbing each stack at 500-year intervals by the 1σ age uncertainty 1000 times. We then take the mean of the 1000 time series and add its uncertainty (at 1σ) to the temperature uncertainty on each stack as the square root of the sum of squares, thus accounting for both the age uncertainty and the temperature uncertainty of the records used in each stack. Calculating ice, ocean, atmospheric, and global energy and energy imbalance We quantified the equivalent energy changes associated with our ΔMOT reconstruction ( ∆ E ocean ) , a sea level stack of ice sheet changes ( ∆ E ice ) ( 11 ), and a global temperature record ( ∆ E atm ) ( 30 ), following the calculations in ( 7 , 9 ) ∆ E ocean = ∆ T × V ocean × ρ sw × cp sw where ∆ T is ΔMOT, V ocean is the modern ocean volume (1.3324 × 10 9 km 3 ) ( 47 ), ρ sw is the average density of seawater (1.03 g/cm 3 ), and cp sw is the temperature-dependent specific heat of seawater (3850 J/kg °C). We calculated uncertainty by converting the ΔMOT uncertainty to energy by using the ∆ E ocean equation ∆ E ice = ( ∆ SL × A ocean × ρ fw × L fw ) × 1.10 where ∆ SL is the change in global sea level ( 11 ), A ocean is the modern ocean area (361.84 × 10 6 km 2 ) ( 47 ), ρ fw is the density of freshwater (1 g/cm 3 ), and L fw is the latent heat of fusion of freshwater (334,000 J/kg). This is all multiplied by an additional 10% to account for the energy required to bring a sea level equivalent volume of ice to the melting point from ∼−20°C. We calculate uncertainty by converting the global sea level uncertainty ( 11 ) to energy using the same ∆ E ice equation ∆ E atm = ∆ T × m atm × cp atm where ∆ T is global mean air temperature change (°C) ( 30 ), m atm is the mass of Earth’s atmosphere (5.14 × 10 18 kg) ( 48 ), and cp atm (1005 J/kg °C) is the specific heat of the atmosphere. We calculate uncertainty by converting global temperature uncertainty ( 30 ) to energy using the ∆ E atm equation. Given the small size of the atmospheric reservoir, we find that ∆ E atm is three orders of magnitude less than the ∆ E ocean and ∆ E ice and, while included in our Δ E global calculation, it is not considered further. We summed Δ E ocean , Δ E ice , and Δ E atm to calculate global energy change (Δ E global ). The uncertainty on ΔE global was calculated as the square root of the sum of squares of the uncertainties of Δ E ocean , Δ E ice , and Δ E atm . Last, for Δ E ocean , Δ E ice , Δ E atm , and Δ E global and their respective uncertainties, we applied a running average of 2500 years, reducing their age ranges from 0 to 150 ka to 1.5 to 148.5 ka. We derived the energy imbalance associated with the rates of Δ E ocean , Δ E ice , Δ E atm , and Δ E global (EEI ocean, EEI ice , EEI atm , and EEI, respectively) using the following equation ( 7 , 9 ) EEI = ( ∂ ∆ E ∂ t × 1 A Earth ) where ∂ ∆ E is the change in energy over time ( ∂ t ) in seconds associated with our age resolution (1.578 × 10 10 s in 500 years) and A Earth is the area of the Earth (5.1 × 10 14 m 2 ). To calculate the uncertainty for EEI ocean , we first used a Monte Carlo simulation to generate 1000 Δ E ocean scenarios by randomly sampling values between the upper and lower uncertainty of Δ E ocean at each time step. Then, each scenario was converted from Δ E ocean to EEI ocean using the EEI equation above. We then performed a bootstrap analysis whereby, at each time step, 1000 mean EEI ocean scenarios were generated by randomly selecting 50% of the Monte Carlo–derived EEI ocean records, with replacement, and averaging them. We then took the uncertainty as one standard deviation across the 1000 mean EEI ocean scenarios for each time step and smoothed it with a running average of 2500 years. We then calculated the uncertainty for EEI ice from 0 to 150 ka using the same procedure as above as applied to the upper and lower uncertainty of Δ E ice . Last, the uncertainty on EEI was calculated as the square root of the sum of squares of the uncertainties of EEI ocean and EEI ice . The sea level stack used here ( 11 ) is based on a principal components analysis of several sea level reconstructions that have varying resolution and were derived from varying methodologies, with such a stack achieving a more objective reconstruction that substantially increases the signal-to-noise relative to any individual reconstruction. Here, we assess the robustness of our results based on this stack by comparing them to EEI ocean , EEI ice , and EEI using six individual sea level reconstructions (fig. S10). Three of these were used in the sea level analysis in ( 11 ) to derive their principal component 1 (PC1) ( 48 – 50 ): One ( 51 ) is a variation of the record ( 52 ) used in ( 11 ), and two are newer reconstructions ( 53 , 54 ). These results show that, despite the different methods used to reconstruct sea level and their differing resolutions, millennial-scale signal in EEI ocean is robust, with much of it lining up with EEI ocean peaks seen in our reconstruction using PC1 in ( 11 ). In particular, of the 15 EEI ocean peaks in our reconstruction between 150 and 20 ka, 12 to 14 peaks occur in the EEI ocean reconstructions based on the six individual sea level records. There is also a clear relationship between EEI ice and precession in five of the reconstructions based on the individual sea level records, with the exception being the record in ( 48 ). iTRACE simulation iTRACE is a state-of-the-art transient global climate and water isotope simulation of the last deglaciation, performed using iCESM1 ( 17 ). The model configuration features an atmosphere and land surface component at a nominal 2° horizontal resolution (1.9° × 2.5°) with 30 vertical levels, and an ocean and sea-ice component at a nominal 1° resolution with 60 vertical levels in the ocean. Four forcing factors—continental ice sheets (ICE), orbital-induced solar insolation (ORB), greenhouse gas concentrations (GHG), and meltwater fluxes (MWF)—were applied additively to simulate deglacial climate evolution since the LGM (20 ka). The continental ice sheet topography was updated every 1000 years according to the ICE-6G reconstruction ( 55 ), while greenhouse gas concentrations (CO 2 , CH 4 , and N 2 O) were prescribed based on ice-core records ( 56 ). Meltwater forcing was implemented following sea level reconstructions to reproduce the millennial-scale AMOC variations. By assuming linearity of the responses, the difference between different pairs of the factorized-forcing simulations can be used to approximately estimate the impact of each individual forcing. For example, (ICE + ORB + GHG) − (ICE + ORB) isolates the effects of greenhouse gas forcing. Decadal mean variables for surface and TOA energy budget are analyzed in Fig. 5 .
