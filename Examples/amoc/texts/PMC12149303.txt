Over the last 800,000 years, Earth’s climate has experienced periodic glacial and interglacial cycles driven by orbital parameters, CO 2 concentrations, and ice sheet volumes. To understand how these climate forcing factors influence climate on orbital scales, high-resolution and continuous paleoclimate simulations are crucial. This study employed the CESM1.2.2 Earth System Model for four 800,000-year orbital-accelerated transient climate simulations, including a full-forcing experiment and three single-factor forcing experiments. The results were compared with geological proxy records from various regions, showing good agreement in mid-low latitude monsoon systems, mid-latitude sea surface temperatures, and high-latitude North Atlantic climate variability. However, the model underestimated sea surface temperature fluctuations in the tropics and Southern Hemisphere mid-latitudes. Despite this, it accurately depicted high-latitude climate characteristics. These climate simulation datasets include global annual mean surface temperature and precipitation distributions with a horizontal resolution of 3.75°. Overall, these simulations provide a reliable and verifiable climate dataset, offering strong support for studying the driving mechanisms of past glacial-interglacial climate changes and interpreting long-term climate signals in geological records. These orbital-scale climate changes are accompanied by numerous changes in the Earth’s surface environment, including the reorganization of global atmospheric circulation 1 , 2 , fluctuations in ice sheets and sea levels 3 , and the reshaping and even extinction of species distributions 4 , 5 . Understanding the global climate and ecological environmental changes and their driving mechanisms over the past 800,000 years often relies on paleoclimate reconstruction, which is mainly based on paleoclimatic proxy indicators or paleoclimate simulations. In recent decades, with the continuous emergence of a large number of high-resolution geological evidence, including marine sediments, ice cores, loess deposits, and speleothems, these records carry key information about atmospheric and oceanic circulation at high and low latitudes, greatly deepening our understanding of global climate changes on the orbital scale since the Pleistocene. The first high-resolution oxygen isotope (δ 18 O) time series based on planktonic foraminifera revealed the sawtooth changes in Earth’s high-latitude ice volume over the past 450,000 years, exhibiting a quasi-periodic oscillation characteristic of about 100,000 years 6 . Antarctic ice core records documented temperature changes in the Antarctic region on orbital and millennial scales 7 , 8 . Proxy records reveal divergent orbital controls on Asian monsoon systems: (1) Chinese loess deposits link glacial-interglacial cycles (~100-kyr) to winter/summer monsoon intensity 9 – 11 , while (2) high-resolution low-latitude speleothems δ 18 O records indicate precessional forcing (~20-kyr) dominates the low-latitude monsoon variability over the past 640,000 years 12 . Recent findings suggest spatial decoupling: the glacial cycles (~100-kyr) modulate precipitation via land-sea thermal gradients, whereas precession (~20-kyr) drives Intertropical Convergence Zone migrations and frontal rainfall intensity 13 . Carrasqueira et al . 14 found a tight coupling between widespread Asian droughts over the past 550 ka and global ice-volume variations (~100-kyr) 14 , suggesting that low-latitude precipitation may have a teleconnection with high-latitude ice-sheet evolution through reorganization of atmospheric moisture transport pathways. Furthermore, a series of high-resolution records have revealed the long-term characteristics of changes in the Southern and Northern Hemisphere westerlies 15 , widespread Asian arid events 14 , tropical Pacific Walker circulation 16 , Hadley circulation 17 , Intertropical Convergence Zone 18 , and Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) 19 . However, the spatial distribution limitations of continental and oceanic records have, to some extent, restricted our in-depth understanding of the evolutionary characteristics and dynamics of orbital-scale climate change over the past 800,000 years of the Pleistocene. Transient simulations of the last 800 ka Due to the limitations of paleoclimatic proxy data, the study of orbital-scale climate change mechanisms faces significant challenges. Therefore, conducting paleoclimate numerical simulations, especially transient forcing simulations, is particularly important. Whether using medium complexity Earth System Models (EMICs) or atmosphere-ocean coupled General Circulation Models (CGCMs), instantaneous forcing of atmosphere/ocean coupled simulations can help us understand how orbital-scale forcings are translated into global and regional climate responses. Currently, long-term paleoclimate simulation work for the past 800 thousand years can be mainly categorized into the following 4 types: Statistical emulation of paleoclimate responses: Recent studies employ emulator-based frameworks to synthesize climate responses from pre-designed numerical experiments. For example, Lyu et al . 20 utilized a Gaussian process emulator trained on 61 HadCM3 snapshot experiments to quantify the relative impacts of orbital parameters, carbon dioxide, and Northern Hemisphere ice sheets on East Asian summer precipitation variability over the past 800 ka. Similarly, Similarly, Krapp et al . 21 developed a high-resolution terrestrial climate reconstruction for the last 800 kyr by integrating 72 HadCM3 simulations spanning 120 ka with climate forcings (CO₂, orbital parameters, and land types), utilizing a multivariate emulator to derive monthly temperature, precipitation, and bioclimatic variables 21 . Transient forcing simulations based on medium complexity models: That is, using simple ocean-atmosphere coupled models to conduct transient forcing simulations since the past 150 ka. Clement et al . 22 pointed out that tropical climate changes caused by orbital forcing would subsequently produce synchronous teleconnection climate responses globally 22 . Ganopolski et al . 23 , 24 used medium complexity Earth System Models to successfully simulate the eight glacial cycles for the first time, revealing the source mechanism of the strong 100-kyr periodic signal in global ice volume changes 23 , 24 . A study using the medium complexity LOVECLIM model conducted a transient simulation covering the past 408 ka, incorporating the historical changes of greenhouse gas concentrations, ice sheet volume, and orbital parameters during that period 25 . The simulation successfully reproduced the long-term trends of temperature and sea ice reconstructed from geological records from glacial to interglacial periods. Recent studies employing the intermediate complexity model LOVECLIM1.3 have demonstrated a strong inverse correlation between simulated local Antarctic summer temperatures and total air content records for the past 440 ka in the Antarctic EPICA Dome C ice core 26 . This suggests that total air content records can serve as a unique proxy for Antarctic summer temperature changes. More recently, Wu et al . 27 revealed approximately 10-kyr half-precession cycle in western equatorial Pacific temperature variability across the past 800,000 years through transient simulations, linking surface ocean warming to equatorial insolation peaks and deep-ocean dynamics to hemispheric insolation, with modulation by eccentricity, CO₂, and ice-sheet forcing. In addition, simulation studies using medium complexity models have shown that, although the changes in solar radiation at the orbital scale are relatively slow, they may be one of the key drivers of abrupt changes during glacial inceptions over the past 800 ka 28 . Multiple slice equilibrium simulations using coupled ocean-atmosphere models: Due to the high cost of transient simulations of complex atmosphere-ocean coupled models, an alternative is to use multiple discrete segment simulations to approximate the “quasi-continuous” distribution of climate over the past 800 ka. Davies-Barnard et al . 29 used an atmosphere-ocean-vegetation coupled model to conduct 62 equilibrium state simulations over the past 120 ka to study the impact of land vegetation changes on climate feedback. To study the long-term evolution characteristics of the Asian monsoon at the orbital scale, a study used atmosphere-ocean coupled models to conduct 77 equilibrium state simulation experiments covering climate changes over the past 425 ka 30 . These multi-segment simulations provided strong simulation evidence to support the “tripole” pattern of changes in Chinese monsoon precipitation at the orbital scale. Armstrong et al . 31 used the HadCM3BB-v1.0 atmosphere-ocean coupled model to conduct 219 snapshot experiments covering the past 800 ka, reasonably capturing the millennial-scale climate change characteristics of North Africa, providing simulation evidence for understanding the driving mechanisms of North African humid periods over the past 800 ka 31 . Matsushita et al . 32 used a time-slicing simulation method, dividing the past 450,000 years into 91 periods, each interval being 5,000 years, to simulate the global climate changes over the past 450,000 years, finding that the changes in East Asian summer monsoon precipitation were affected by the interaction of different ocean basins, leading to opposite trends in precipitation in China and Japan during increased summer insolation 32 . Transient climate simulations via orbital forcing acceleration method: Due to the rapid response characteristics of atmospheric circulation to climate forcing, orbitally accelerated transient simulations significantly reduce computational time through accelerated evolution of orbital parameters (eccentricity, obliquity, precession). This approach enables complex coupled atmosphere-ocean models to simulate multi-millennial to million-year-scale climate evolution while preserving key climatic signatures. By compressing the ratio of physical time to computational time, the method enhances simulation speed by 1–2 orders of magnitude, overcoming traditional computational bottlenecks to achieve high-resolution numerical simulation of long-term climatic processes. For example, Kutzbach et al . 33 , Chen et al . 34 , Xie et al . 35 , and Liu et al . 36 used orbital acceleration driving technology to conduct several transient forcing simulation experiments on the global monsoon system over the past 300 ka, in-depth studying the response mechanisms of these key circulation systems to orbital-scale forcing. Lu et al . 37 studied the orbital-scale response of the tropical El Niño-Southern Oscillation, while Jian et al . 38 and Wang et al . 39 focused on the changes in heat content of the western Pacific warm pool. Recently, Timmermann et al . 40 and Yun et al . 41 used atmosphere-ocean coupled models to conduct an orbital acceleration-driven transient experiment covering the past 3 million years. This study revealed the potential impact of orbital-scale climate change on the migration paths of ancient humans, providing a new perspective on how ancient humans adapted to long-term changes in Earth’s climate 40 . In summary, most climate simulations over the past 800,000 years have primarily utilized transient forcing experiments with intermediate-complexity models and multiple slice experiments with complex atmosphere-ocean coupled models. Fewer studies have employed fully coupled complex climate models for transient simulations, particularly those incorporating both single-forcing and full-forcing combinations. Compared to earlier 300,000-year simulations covering three glacial-interglacial cycles 34 , 39 , this study leverages the recent reconstructions of climatic boundary conditions (e.g., Bereiter et al . 42 , and ice sheet data from this study) to perform transient simulations spanning 800,000 years, incorporating both full-forcing and single-forcing experimental configurations. This approach not only extends the temporal scope of climate simulations but also facilitates systematic investigation of periodic variations in climate system responses to multiple forcing factors, thereby offering critical insights for reconciling long-term paleoclimate records with simulations. Methods CESM1.2.2 To quantify the sensitivity of the Earth’s climate system to orbital forcing, CO 2 concentration, and changes in ice sheet volume, this study employed the Community Earth System Model version 1.2.2 (CESM1.2.2) 43 to simulate the climate of the past 800,000 years. The CESM1.2.2, with its complex physical processes and parameterization schemes such as interactive clouds, realistic simulation of tropical processes, three-dimensional ocean circulation, and a fully dynamic atmospheric model, can accurately reproduce the observed climate state. The model primarily consists of six component models: the atmosphere (Community Atmosphere Model version 4, CAM4), the ocean (Parallel Ocean Program version 2, POP2), the land (Community Land Model version 4, CLM4, including a prescribed vegetation model), the sea ice (Community Ice Code 4, CICE4), the river routing (River Transport Model, RTM), and a coupler that facilitates flux exchange between them. Specifically, the atmospheric model has a resolution of T31, with 48 × 96 horizontal grid points, approximately 3.75° in horizontal resolution, and is divided into 26 layers in the vertical direction. The ocean model has a resolution of gx3v7, with 100 × 116 horizontal grid points, approximately 3° in horizontal resolution, and has 60 layers in the vertical direction. The land model CLM4 has the same horizontal resolution as the atmospheric model CAM4, while the ocean POP2 module and the sea ice CICE4 module use the same resolution. The river routing module employs a higher resolution of approximately 0.5° × 0.5° to provide a more detailed simulation of surface hydrological processes. The CESM has been widely employed in paleoclimate research, including deep-time snapshot simulations 44 , 45 and orbital-scale transient climate simulation 38 , 41 . However, several inherent limitations of the model may introduce simulation uncertainties: (1) the coarse-resolution version exhibits systematic cold biases 46 ; (2) transient simulations fail to account for paleogeographic changes in land-sea distribution; and (3) the fixed modern river routing scheme does not incorporate drainage reorganization induced by glacial meltwater. Previous studies have demonstrated that prescribed land-sea configurations 47 and invariant river routing 48 , 49 can introduce systematic biases in regional temperature patterns and large-scale circulation features. Nevertheless, the Earth system model remains capable of effectively capturing the fundamental response characteristics of the climate system to orbital forcing over the past 800 ka. Transient orbital and trace gas parameters setup (800–0 ka) For the 800,000-yr climate simulation experiment, we followed the method of Berger 50 to set the variations of the three orbital parameters that determine the amount of incoming solar shortwave radiation (Fig. 1a ). The greenhouse gas concentrations in the experiment were based on the main greenhouse gas information from ice core records, including the methane forcing curve (Fig. 1b ) 51 , 52 and the atmospheric CO 2 concentration (Fig. 1c ) 42 , 53 . Atmospheric nitrous oxide (N₂O) concentrations over the past 800 kyr have been reconstructed from Antarctic ice-core records 54 . In our study, however, N₂O concentration was prescribed at Pre-Industrial values due to the lack of continuous records similar to methane. While this simplifies model input, it may introduce uncertainty. Köhler et al . 55 found that during the last glacial-interglacial cycle, CO₂ (~65%) and CH₄ (~25%) were the main drivers of greenhouse gas induced radiative forcing, with N₂O contributing only about 10%, corresponding to a global temperature change of 0.1–0.2°C. Ice sheet, vegetation mask and other forcings In this study, transient experiment simulations were conducted by periodically prescribing Northern Hemisphere ice sheet extent and elevation boundary conditions in both atmospheric and land models to simulate climatic impacts of ice sheet evolution. Previous studies have predominantly constructed transient ice sheet forcing fields using scaling methods based on sea-level curves or benthic δ¹⁸O records, interpolating data from a single glacial-interglacial cycle to generate long-term boundary conditions 35 , 37 . However, this methodology, constrained by single-cycle baseline ice sheet data, fails to adequately capture the spatial heterogeneity of ice sheet dynamics under diverse climatic regimes. This limitation introduces systematic biases in reconstructing three-dimensional ice sheet geometry (thickness and extent), which propagate into uncertainties in climate simulations. Furthermore, the selection of reconstruction records (e.g., alternative δ¹⁸O records or sea-level change proxies) introduces additional boundary condition uncertainties. Unlike previous studies 35 , 37 , the ice sheet data spanning the past 800,000 years used in this work were derived from simulations using the Parallel Ice Sheet Model (PISM) 56 . Specifically, we employed the glacial index method 57 to synthesize temperature and precipitation conditions over the past 800 ka, which drove the PISM model for offline simulation of Northern Hemisphere ice sheet extent and elevation (details in Supplementary Text S1). By implementing the aforementioned glaciological modeling approach, this study successfully generated ice sheet evolution datasets with enhanced temporal and spatial resolution, partially mitigating uncertainties in ice sheet elevation and spatial configuration. However, the glacial index method has two limitations when it comes to constructing continuous climatic distributions: First, the formulation of glacial indices depends critically on selected geological records, which can introduce biases into the accuracy of simulations. Secondly, inherent uncertainties persist in defining reference climatic conditions for both the present day and the last glacial maximum period. These methodological constraints collectively propagate biases through ice sheet evolution simulations. The equivalent sea-level change curve corresponding to ice sheet volume in transient forcing experiments is shown in Fig. 1d , while Figure S1 comprehensively demonstrates the thickness and spatial distribution of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets during eight glacial maxima. During the process of the transient forcing experiment simulation, we periodically updated the area and height distribution of the terrestrial ice sheets every 500 years, which, considering the 100-year orbital acceleration factor, is equivalent to adjusting once every 5 model years. In this instance, the model year is defined as the 356-day time span, which corresponds to 17,520 time steps (i.e. 365 days × 24 hours × 60 minutes/day) in a climate model run at a specific integration time step (typically 30 minutes). Although the model initialization used modern vegetation distribution data, in the transient forcing simulation of glacial and interglacial cycles, for grid points where the ice sheet grew or retreated in the Northern Hemisphere, we correspondingly replaced the modern vegetation distribution of that grid point with an ice sheet type and adjusted the corresponding topographic height values in the topography file to achieve the climate model’s response to the given ice sheet extent and height forcing. In addition, the river route settings in all experiments were identical to those of the Pre-Industrial. It should be noted that the experiments did not consider sea-level changes caused by ice sheet melting, changes in land-sea distribution, long-term changes in the Antarctic ice sheet, and the meltwater pulse effects from ice sheet retreat. For example, previous studies have shown that during periods of increased solar insolation, melting of the Northern Hemisphere ice sheets causes meltwater discharge into the North Atlantic, slowing the AMOC, causing a cold anomaly in the North Atlantic, and weakening the East Asian monsoon via atmospheric teleconnections 58 , 59 . In addition, we are aware that some local climatic factors, such as global vegetation changes 60 , regional cryosphere distribution 61 , and sea surface temperature biases 62 , were not considered in the model. These factors could potentially affect the simulation results. Inclusion of these elements in future simulation studies is key to improving and refining the simulations. Orbital Acceleration method and Single-Forcing Simulations To address the computational demands of simulating climate evolution over the past 800 ka, the orbital acceleration method has been developed in paleoclimate transient simulations 63 – 66 . This approach proportionally compresses the timescale of climate forcing to accelerate simulations, with the main advantages of (1) significantly improving computational efficiency and (2) preserving climate response properties under appropriate compression ratios 66 . Adopting this method, we implement a compression ratio of 100 to condense 800,000 years of climate evolution into 8,000 model years. While simulations with ratios below 100 can adequately reproduce climate responses to climate forcings 66 , a critical limitation arises: deep ocean temperature responses exhibit a lag of approximately 2–3-kyr compared to uncompressed simulations 64 , 67 . Furthermore, independent paleoclimatic evidence indicates inherent phase lags of 2–5-kyr in Asian monsoon responses to orbital forcing 12 , 14 . We hypothesize that the temporal compression approach may amplify timing uncertainties in terrestrial climate responses (e.g., monsoon systems), as it potentially alters internal feedback dynamics within the climate system. To disentangle the individual climatic effects of ice sheets, orbital parameters, and greenhouse gases, we designed single-forcing experiments based on the linear additivity assumption. By isolating climate forcings (ice sheet boundary conditions, orbital configurations, greenhouse gas concentrations), this method quantifies their distinct contributions. Its validity has been demonstrated in Holocene transient simulations through multi-forcing attribution analyses 68 , 69 . 784ka Equilibrium Experiment Setup The initiation time for the past 800,000-year transient experiments was chosen with the following considerations: (1) The equilibrium slice experiment, serving as the foundation for transient simulations, required sufficient integration duration to achieve a quasi-equilibrium state. Since this slice experiment was initialized with modern boundary conditions, selecting the first interglacial period (~800 ka) rather than a glacial period as the starting point avoids substantial adjustments to boundary conditions (e.g., orbital parameters, global ice volume, and CO₂ concentration), thereby accelerating equilibration; (2) Following the simulation start time of previous studies 70 , the equilibrium climate of 784,000 years ago was chosen as the initial state for the transient experiments. Therefore, we first conducted a climate simulation experiment 784,000 years ago, with detailed experimental configurations shown in Table 1 . A 3000-year equilibrium simulation under 784 ka boundary conditions (Table 1 ; Supplementary Fig. S2a ) achieved a quasi-equilibrium state, defined by a global top-of-atmosphere net radiative imbalance <0.05 W m−² (Supplementary Fig. S2b ) and a centennial linear trend <0.02 W m−² century−¹ (Supplementary Fig. S2c ), ensuring stabilized energy exchange among atmosphere, ocean, and land systems with negligible climate drift. Data Records This article conducted four sets of orbital acceleration simulations, including one full-forcing simulation and three separate-forcing simulations. The dataset is presented in the form of “800,000-year glacial-interglacial orbital acceleration simulation dataset” and is stored in NetCDF data format. The data can be accessed in the Zenodo repository ( https://zenodo.org/records/14961655 ) 71 and it is licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC-BY 4.0). The climate variables include monthly data for the full-forcing experiment (ALL), the CO₂ component-forcing experiment (CO₂), the orbital component-forcing experiment (ORB), and the ice-sheet forcing experiment (ICE). These variables are: 2-meter air temperature (TAS; units: °C), surface temperature (TS; units: °C), precipitation (PRECT; units: m s−¹), near-surface zonal wind (UBOT; units: m s−¹), and near-surface meridional wind (VBOT; units: m s−¹). The data dimensions for TAS, TS, PRECT, UBOT, and VBOT are 94080 (months) × 48 (latitudes) × 96 (longitudes). Technical Validation Modeled Global Mean 2-m Air Temperature and Precipitation Firstly, we present the temporal evolution of the annual mean temperature (Fig. 2a ) and precipitation (Fig. 3a ) simulated by the full-forcing experiment over the past 800,000 years. During the initial simulation phase (784–750 ka), both temperature (Fig. 2a ) and precipitation (Fig. 3a ) exhibit rapid fluctuations and a declining trend. This likely reflects the climate system’s rapid response to transient forcings, including declining CO₂ concentrations and gradual ice sheet expansion, with potential contributions from internal climate variability. Fig. 3 As in Fig. 2 , but for global mean precipitation over the past 800 ka (gray represents the original sequence, blue represents a 21-year running mean, units: mm d −1 ). Overall, the model reasonably reproduces the alternating characteristics of cold and warm periods between glacial and interglacial periods over the past 800,000 years (Fig. 2a ). The full-forcing experiment captures global temperature fluctuations of approximately 3 °C (Fig. 2a ), aligning with simulations from the LOVECLIM intermediate-complexity model (Figure S3a , data are available at https://apdrc.soest.hawaii.edu/projects/paleomodeling/800k.php ) 72 . This result reasonably reproduces the glacial-interglacial temperature changes over the past 800 kyr. However, this amplitude is weaker than estimates from proxy records 73 . This discrepancy may primarily stem from the underestimation of climate sensitivity in the low-resolution version of CESM 74 . Due to the reduced climate sensitivity, the model’s temperature response to equivalent orbital and greenhouse gas forcing is dampened, resulting in a smaller simulated glacial-interglacial temperature amplitude compared to proxy-derived estimates. Additionally, the full-forcing experiment employs modern land-sea distribution as the boundary condition for the model, thus failing to include the potential impact of changes in land-sea distribution on global temperature changes. For precipitation, the full forcing experiment simulates glacial-interglacial fluctuations of ~0.22 mm d−¹ (Fig. 3a ), notably weaker than the ~0.40 mm d−¹ amplitude derived from the full forcing transient simulation with the LOVECLIM model (Figure S4a ) 72 . The individual forcing experiments, including orbital forcing, CO 2 forcing, and ice sheet forcing experiments, can be used to assess the relative contributions of these three factors to long-term changes in global mean temperature. In the CO 2 forcing experiment, the amplitude of change in global mean temperature is relatively small, approximately 1.2 °C (Fig. 2b ). The temperature changes closely follow the fluctuations of the CO 2 curve, especially after the Mid-Brunhes Event (MBE) 75 , 76 , when the global temperature amplitude suddenly increases (Fig. 2b ), consistent with previous findings of a significant increase in climate system amplitude after the Mid-Brunhes. Yin (2013) conducted a detailed study of this phenomenon, proposing that the enhanced amplitude of climate system responses after the Mid-Brunhes Event may result from changes in Antarctic bottom water formation and Southern Ocean ventilation driven by variations in insolation 77 . The CO 2 forcing experiment captures this important feature well, while the full-forcing experiment is somewhat deficient in reproducing this feature (Fig. 2a ). The ice sheet forcing experiment simulates a relatively small amplitude of global temperature change, approximately 1.0°C (Fig. 2c ). This magnitude is intermediate between those of CO₂ forcing (~1.2 °C; Fig. 2b ) and orbital forcing (Fig. 2d ), suggesting that ice sheet changes, while less dominant than CO₂ forcing, exert a non-negligible influence on global temperature. Ice sheet dynamics modulate regional temperatures, particularly in high-latitude regions, via vertical temperature lapse rate adjustments and albedo effects induced by changes in ice sheet elevation and spatial extent. These regional impacts manifest as distinct temperature spikes in the ice sheet forcing experiment, potentially linked to abrupt climate events during glacial-interglacial transitions. In the orbital forcing experiment, despite the significant impact of changes in orbital parameters on seasonal solar shortwave insolation (for example, Fig. 1c ), their influence on annual mean temperature is relatively modest (Fig. 2d ). This result is corroborated by the annual mean temperature simulated in the high-resolution orbital-alone transient simulation with iCESM (Figure S3c ) 78 , 79 . Therefore, the amplitude of annual mean temperature simulated in the orbital forcing experiment is the smallest among all individual forcing experiments, approximately 0.8°C (Fig. 2d ). Regarding changes in precipitation, the CO 2 forcing experiment contributes the most to the precipitation amplitude in the full-forcing experiment, about 0.17 mm d −1 , while the orbital forcing experiment contributes the least, only 0.13 mm d −1 (Fig. 3d ). By comparison, the orbital-alone high-resolution transient simulation with iCESM yields a larger precipitation amplitude of 0.20 mm d−¹ (Figure S4c ) 78 , 79 . Second, wavelet cross-spectral analysis of SST variations demonstrates strong coherence between tropical proxy records and simulations at orbital periodicities of 20-kyr, 40-kyr, and 100-kyr (Figure S5 ), indicating that tropical SST evolution integrates influences from multiple orbital-scale forcing. Tropical basins emerge as critical regions for cross-latitude and cross-hemispheric climate interactions. In the Western Pacific Warm Pool, wavelet coherence between simulated and reconstructed SSTs 80 , 81 exhibits fragmented alignment across the 100-kyr, precessional, and obliquity bands (Figure S5a ), with the simulated 100-kyr signal appearing weaker than its proxy counterpart. In contrast, simulated SSTs in the Indian Ocean, Eastern Pacific, and Atlantic show robust coherence with proxy records 82 – 87 at the 100-kyr period and minimal phase differences (Figures S5b–d ). Precessional cycles display sporadic coherence peaks across these basins, while obliquity cycles exhibit negligible alignment. In the Western Pacific, analysis of wavelet cross-spectral coherence between single-forcing experiment (CO₂, ice sheet, and orbital) SST, their linear syntheses, and proxy record reveals that CO₂-forced (GHG; Figure S6a ) and ice sheet-forced (ICE; Figure S6b ) SSTs exhibit strong 100-kyr periodicity alignment with proxy record, whereas orbital-forced SSTs (ORB; Figure S6c ) show weaker 100-kyr coherence. Linear synthesis of SST anomalies from the three experiments demonstrates enhanced coherence with proxy records at both 100-kyr and 40-kyr periodicities (Figure S6d ), indicating that single-forcing experiments effectively resolve CO₂ and ice sheet forcing impacts on SST variability. However, the full-forcing simulation exhibits marked attenuation in 100-kyr coherence signals between simulated SST and proxy record in the wavelet cross-spectrum (Figure S5a ). This discrepancy likely arises from model uncertainties, where nonlinear interactions among orbital insolation, CO₂, and ice sheet forcings in the Western Pacific may suppress 100-kyr signal amplitudes during full-forcing simulations, while concurrent application of multiple forcings could amplify model parametrization errors, further attenuating 100-kyr signal intensity. In the eastern Pacific, simulations align well with proxy records at the 100-kyr cycle (ODP Site 846) 82 , 83 . However, the simulated SST show pronounced 20-kyr precessional spectral peaks (not shown), whereas proxy records display minimal coherence in this band (Figure S5b ). Considering that ENSO—a key tropical air-sea interaction—is closely tied to SST oscillations in the eastern Pacific, and ODP Site 846 is located within the modern ENSO core region (the Niño3 zone), we suggest that precessional forcing may have strongly influenced eastern Pacific SST changes over the past 800,000 years. This idea matches earlier studies showing that ENSO activity during glacial-interglacial cycles was mainly controlled by precession 22 , 37 , 65 . However, why the eastern Pacific ODP 846 record is dominated by the 100-kyr cycle with a weak precession signal remains to be investigated. Comparison with East Asian Monsoon System Records We compared the simulated June precipitation sequence with the oxygen isotope records from the stalagmites in Sanbao Cave, East Asia 12 , 88 . The simulated June precipitation time series aligns with the long-term trend and the 20-kyr periodicity of the stalagmite δ 18 O isotope (Fig. 5a and S7a ), confirming that the δ 18 O records reflect precession-driven Asian summer monsoon precipitation changes. However, the cross-spectral analysis shows that the precipitation indicator from the stalagmites lags 90° in phase behind the simulated June precipitation 89 , indicating the influence of additional forcing factors. Wavelet cross-spectral analysis of individual forcing experiments reveals that the precessional signal in precipitation predominantly originates from ORB (Figure S8c ), with ICE (Figure S8a ) and GHG (Figure S8b ) contributing minimally to this spectral component. Crucially, the ALL experiment demonstrates significantly stronger coherence between simulated precipitation and geological records in the precessional band compared to the linearly superposed results from three individual experiments (ICE + ORB + GHG) (Fig. 5a and S8d ). This demonstrates that the three forcing factors interact in a complex manner within the climate system to enhance the 20-kyr signal. Moreover, the simulated June precipitation exhibits a weak 100-kyr periodicity (Figure not shown), showing little consistency with the stalagmite records (Figure S7 ). Previous studies have shown that the growth and retreat of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets can modulate the Asian monsoon intensity by exciting stationary waves 90 , 91 . It is therefore reasonable to identify the 100-kyr signal in the monsoon rainfall sequence spectrum. Secondly, the δ 13 C record from the Jingyuan site on the Chinese Loess Plateau (Data are available at East Asian Paleoenvironmental Science Database http://paleodata.ieecas.cn/index.aspx ) 92 aligns well with the simulated annual precipitation sequence (Fig. 5b ). Previous studies have indicated that this site is jointly controlled by the 100-kyr and precession cycles 92 . The simulated annual precipitation demonstrates a high degree of consistency with the record at these two orbital periods (Figure S7b ). This suggests that the simulated annual precipitation fluctuations on the Loess Plateau during glacial-interglacial cycles are controlled by global ice volume or CO 2 (100-kyr cycle) (Figure S9 ). Furthermore, a comparison was made between the simulated annual mean temperature and the AP (Arboreal pollen) content from Zoige Basin lake sediments (The data were obtained from the supplementary material of Zhao et al . 93 , available at this website https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aay6193 ), which is a proxy for annual mean temperature. The long-term trends of the two show good consistency (Fig. 5b ), and they also have good consistency at the 100-kyr and 20-kyr periodicities (Figure S7c ). Finally, the temperature reconstructed from the Luochuan site on the Chinese Loess Plateau (Data are available at East Asian Paleoenvironmental Science Database http://paleodata.ieecas.cn/index.aspx ) 94 shows a glacial-interglacial change of 4–10 °C, which is comparable to the amplitude of the simulated summer surface temperature (Fig. 5d ). This suggests that the reconstructed Luochuan temperature record mainly reflects summer temperature changes. Meanwhile, the wavelet cross-spectrum analysis shows that the simulated summer mean temperature and the record have good consistency at the 100-kyr periodicity (Figure S7d ). Further analysis indicates that the 100-kyr periodicity in the full forcing experiment is primarily attributable to the global ice volume and CO 2 forcing experiments (Supplementary Figures S10a and S10b ), suggesting that the summer mean temperature on the Loess Plateau is jointly governed by global ice volume and CO 2 changes. Comparison with Mid-Latitude Sea Surface Temperature Records Comparison of the simulated mid-latitude North Atlantic SST with limited proxy SST record changes shows that the interglacial climate is relatively stable with minor SST fluctuations, while strong cooling occurs during glacial periods (Fig. 6a ). The simulated glacial-interglacial SST amplitude (~12 °C) matches the magnitude of proxy data from IODP Site U1313 (Fig. 6a ) 95 , 96 . In contrast, mid-latitude South Atlantic ODP Site 1089 proxy reconstructions show ~10 °C variability 97 , 98 , whereas simulated amplitudes are substantially lower (~4.7 °C; Fig. 6b ). Wavelet cross-spectral analysis demonstrates strong agreement in the dominant periodicities between simulations and records (Figure S11 ): North Atlantic mid-latitude SST is governed by 100-kyr cycles with weaker 40-kyr and 20-kyr signals (Figure S11a ), while Southern Hemisphere summer SST exhibits 100-kyr-dominated spectral characteristics consistent with proxy records (Figure S11b ). Further analysis demonstrates that the simulated 100-kyr periodicity in Southern Hemisphere summer SSTs primarily originates from CO₂ forcing (Figure S12 ), while Northern Hemisphere ice volume (Figure S12a ) and orbital forcing (Figure S12c ) contribute moderately after 450 ka. Collectively, despite systematic underestimation of SST amplitudes, the simulations successfully replicate the long-term evolutionary patterns and dominant periodic signals of mid-latitude oceanic temperatures. Secondly, the simulated mid-latitude North Pacific SST shows good agreement with the ODP Site 1020 reconstructed SST 99 , 100 in both amplitude (simulated ~8 °C and reconstructed ~10 °C) and long-term trends (Fig. 6c ), with the simulated amplitude slightly smaller than that of the North Atlantic co-latitude SST simulations. Cross-spectral analysis further demonstrates significant coherence between simulated North Pacific SSTs and proxy records at both 100-kyr and 40-kyr cycles (Figure S11d ). In the South Pacific, the simulated SSTs align with the long-term trends of the ODP Site 1125 reconstructed SST from Peterson et al . 101 , 102 , but the simulated amplitude (~3 °C) is significantly lower than the reconstructed values (Fig. 6d ). However, the 100-kyr periodic signal remains highly consistent in the mid-latitude South Pacific (Figure S11d ). These results indicate that the glacial-interglacial variability of mid-latitude Pacific SSTs in both hemispheres is dominated by the 100-kyr periodicity, and the model effectively captures the mechanisms of climate forcing at different timescales (100-kyr and 40-kyr). Simulated High-Latitude Temperature, AMOC, and Interglacial Climate Abrupt Events This section compares the simulated high-latitude climate evolution with reconstructed records. Firstly, the Antarctic Vostok ice core records key high-latitude climate changes 7 , 103 , showing that Antarctic temperatures are relatively stable during interglacials but experience abrupt temperature drops of tens of degrees Celsius at the end of interglacials (Fig. 7a ). Simulation results show that the sea surface temperature (SST) of the high latitudes in the North Atlantic exhibits similar large temperature drops during the transition from interglacial to glacial periods (see Fig. 7b ). Additionally, the stability of the AMOC system is an important indicator for measuring glacial-interglacial climate changes. The simulated 800 ka evolution of AMOC index shows that AMOC rapidly weakens near the end of each interglacial, accompanied by a sharp decline in sea surface temperature in the high latitudes of the North Atlantic (Fig. 7b ). Compared with the abrupt events in marine sediment records from IODP Site U1385 19 , 104 , the simulation results basically match the geological records (Fig. 7 d, gray shaded rectangles). Fig. 7 Comparison of simulated high-latitude climate abrupt events in the North Atlantic with proxy abrupt event indicators, where ( a ) inferred Antarctic temperature anomalies based on δ 18 O (units: °C) 7 , 103 ; ( b ) the simulated high-latitude North Atlantic SST change sequence (units: °C), with the site taken from the region of formation of the North Atlantic Deep Water (location 60.9°N, 0.8°E), used to indicate the amplitude of simulated high-latitude climate changes, ( c ) the calculated simulated AMOC strength index (defined as: the maximum transport value below 500 meters depth north of 26.5°N, units: SV, equivalent to 10 6 m 3 ), ( d ) the global climate rapid change events of the last 800 ka from IODP site U1385 (37.57°N, 10.1°W) 19 . The right column shows wavelet cross-spectral coherence distributions: upper panel depicts coherence between the simulated SST and Antarctic temperature anomalies, while the lower panel displays coherence between AMOC and IODP U1385 record. It is evident that our transient simulation based on a complex climate model has successfully reproduced the rapid cooling signals in high latitudes during glacial-interglacial cycles. While our experimental results demonstrate substantial consistency between the AMOC response simulated using orbital acceleration techniques and those from non-accelerated intermediate-complexity models 28 , we explicitly acknowledge the methodological limitations of representing deep ocean circulation dynamics through such acceleration approaches. As previously mentioned, the imposed temporal scaling likely induces a lagged response of slowly evolving oceanic processes to rapid orbital-scale forcing perturbations, thereby introducing biases in simulated AMOC variability. We caution that the simulated AMOC responses to climatic forcing may not fully capture the transient feedback mechanisms operating within the actual climate system. Wavelet cross-spectral analysis reveals significant coherence regions between simulated high-latitude SST, AMOC strength, and proxy records at the 100-kyr orbital cycle, with weaker signals detected at the 40-kyr obliquity cycle (Fig. 7 , right column). Comparative analysis of individual and full forcing experiments demonstrates that the 100-kyr periodicity of the North Atlantic high-latitude SST variability is predominantly regulated by ice sheet dynamics (Figure S13a ), which modulate ocean circulation and SSTs via topographic effects and albedo feedbacks; while orbital forcing (Figure S13c ) plays a secondary role in modulating these signals, while CO₂ forcing (Figure S13b ) demonstrates negligible contributions. The comparison of the above model results with proxy data shows that the simulation results can reasonably explain the high-latitude climate changes revealed by proxy data, providing strong support for further research into the mechanisms of global climate change during glacial-interglacial periods.
